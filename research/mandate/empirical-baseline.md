# Empirical baseline and force-design implications

## Purpose of this baseline

This chapter establishes enough evidence to define the design problem. It is not a complete disaster-loss inventory, because no single U.S. dataset measures all hazards, direct and indirect mortality, service outages, displacement, social loss, and government spending on a consistent basis.

The absence of such a dataset is itself a force-design finding. The future institution needs a national operational-measurement system capable of relating hazards to service loss, human consequences, deployed capacity, time to effect, and recovery.

## The national demand signal is multi-hazard

FEMA's National Risk Index compares 18 natural hazards: avalanche, coastal flooding, cold wave, drought, earthquake, hail, heat wave, hurricane, ice storm, inland flooding, landslide, lightning, strong wind, tornado, tsunami, volcanic activity, wildfire, and winter weather.[^nri] This is broader than NOAA's costly weather and climate event series and still excludes human-caused hazards and many secondary consequences.

The implication is simple: a national force sized only to hurricanes, floods, and wildfire will miss consequential geophysical, temperature, water, and cascading-infrastructure missions. “All hazards” cannot mean identical units for every hazard; it means one national architecture able to combine common service-restoration functions with truly specialized control capabilities.

## Economic loss is large, rising, and badly fragmented

NOAA's archived 1980–2024 billion-dollar event series records 403 weather and climate events above its inflation-adjusted threshold, with $2.918 trillion in estimated direct losses and 16,941 deaths. The five years from 2020 through 2024 account for 115 events, $746.7 billion, and 2,520 deaths.[^noaa-summary]

Those numbers are useful and routinely misunderstood:

- the threshold excludes smaller events, even when their cumulative social burden is large;
- geophysical hazards such as earthquakes are absent;
- NOAA includes estimated insured and uninsured direct losses but excludes much of health, mortality valuation, natural capital, supply-chain loss, and downstream welfare cost;
- inflation adjustment does not normalize changes in population, wealth, building stock, or development in hazard-prone areas; and
- increasing event counts and losses reflect the combined effects of exposure, vulnerability, data and aggregation practice, and climate-driven change in some extremes—not one causal factor.[^noaa-method]

Separate official estimates illustrate the missing scale but should not be added mechanically. FEMA and USGS estimate national annualized earthquake loss of at least $14.7 billion, excluding lifeline infrastructure, long-term indirect loss, and secondary effects.[^earthquake] The Congressional Budget Office estimates that floods caused an average $46 billion per year in damage from 2014 through 2023, in 2023 dollars, and projects climate conditions likely in 2050 to increase that damage by one-quarter to one-third. CBO's scope includes property, public infrastructure, emergency response, loss of use, business interruption, agriculture, forestry, and offshore energy, but excludes death, injury, and quality-of-life loss.[^cbo-flood]

These estimates prove scale; they do not form a clean total.

## Mortality follows service failure, not only hazard contact

Disaster mortality is especially sensitive to definitions. Death certificates, direct incident attribution, indirect attribution, and modeled excess mortality answer different questions.

CDC found 15,707 U.S. deaths in which excessive natural heat was listed as an underlying or contributing cause from 1999 through 2020, while warning that death-certificate data undercount heat when certifiers do not document heat stress.[^cdc-heat] In Hurricane Irma, CDC identified 129 related deaths across Florida, Georgia, and North Carolina: 11 direct, 115 indirect, and three possibly related. Indirect pathways included worsening chronic conditions, interrupted emergency medical services, loss of air conditioning, oxygen dependence, and carbon-monoxide poisoning during power outages.[^cdc-irma]

Puerto Rico's Hurricane Maria count shows the magnitude of the denominator problem. The territory initially reported 65 direct deaths; it later adopted an estimate of 2,975 excess deaths for September 2017 through February 2018. Alternative studies and population assumptions produced materially different estimates.[^gao-mortality]

For force design, the lesson is not to choose one number. It is to model the pathways. Power, cooling, oxygen, dialysis, medication, communications, water, transport, and routine care are life-safety systems. A force designed only around trauma and extraction will miss much of the avoidable mortality in a prolonged catastrophe.

## Displacement is a core outcome, but the United States measures it weakly

An experimental Census Household Pulse Survey estimated that approximately 2.86 million U.S. adults had been displaced by a natural disaster in the prior year during a September–October 2023 survey window.[^census-displacement] The estimate has a wide margin of error and inherits the limitations of an internet, recall-based, cross-sectional survey. It does not establish how long people were displaced, whether they returned, or how many moves occurred.

The project should therefore treat displacement as a time-phased service failure, not a single count:

1. protective evacuation;
2. short sheltering;
3. temporary displacement within the community;
4. extended relocation because housing or lifelines remain unavailable; and
5. permanent migration.

The force can influence each stage differently. Evacuation lift, climate-safe shelter, utility bridges, debris clearance, temporary public works, housing production, schools, healthcare, and market restoration determine whether a protective move becomes long-term displacement.

## Compound events and infrastructure cascades are the force-sizing case

The Fifth National Climate Assessment concludes that interconnected people, infrastructure, commodities, goods, services, and natural systems transmit failures across sectors. When power fails, transportation, water and wastewater, telecommunications, healthcare, and economic activity can fail with it.[^nca-complex]

The assessment identifies increasing risk from simultaneous or rapidly successive extremes. Recent examples include concurrent western heat, drought, wildfire, smoke, and public-health effects; back-to-back flooding in the Northeast; and the coupling of extreme temperature, generation failure, fuel constraints, water disruption, and medical stress during the 2021 Texas winter storm.[^nca-compound][^nca-energy]

It also reports that major U.S. power outages affecting more than 50,000 customers were about 64 percent more frequent during 2011–2021 than during 2000–2010.[^nca-energy] Outage frequency is not the same as customer-hours, mortality, or restoration time, but it confirms that mobile power and thermal safety cannot be niche capabilities.

Concurrency appears in operational evidence as competition for national capacity. Four sequential disasters in 2017 overwhelmed FEMA's available workforce, produced shortages of debris-removal contractors, and delayed clearance and housing activity. At the response peak, 54 percent of deployed FEMA staff were serving in positions for which they did not hold the agency's “Qualified” title.[^gao-2017]

The 2024 National Preparedness Report documents the same structural risk: overlapping hurricane and wildfire seasons, unanticipated events competing with pre-positioned responses, scarce generators and portable air cleaners, limited warehouse space, and communities reporting material logistics and supply-chain capability gaps.[^npr-2024]

A credible force therefore cannot be sized to one median event. Its design reference is the simultaneous loss of several lifelines across more than one theater while transport, commercial capacity, and the response workforce are themselves degraded.

## Repeated operational bottlenecks

Major-event reviews converge on a failure chain:

`access loss → information loss → weak prioritization → constrained distribution → fuel and power failure → water and medical degradation → debris and housing delay → unstable transition to recovery`

### Access and receiving capacity

After Hurricane Maria, damage to ports, airports, roads, bridges, the grid, and communications increased transit time and constrained Puerto Rico and the U.S. Virgin Islands' capacity to receive and distribute national resources.[^fema-2017] This distinguishes **national stock** from **delivered flow**. A warehouse of commodities or a ship offshore is not an effect if the affected theater lacks functioning reception, onward movement, fuel, local distribution, and information.

### Information and communications

Maria disabled an estimated 95 percent of Puerto Rico cell sites immediately after landfall. FEMA reported that some teams had to fly back to transmit infrastructure findings and that air-reconnaissance information did not reliably reach decision-makers. Satellite devices were constrained by activation, weather, line of sight, and user proficiency.[^fema-2017]

GAO separately found that unclear federal communications-restoration roles could contribute to confusion and delay, leading DHS to clarify the FCC's role in emergency guidance.[^gao-comms] Survivable communications must therefore be designed as an exercised operational service with field power, local access, backhaul, trained users, spectrum and priority arrangements, repair, and logistics—not as a box of satellite phones.

### Power, fuel, and water

Puerto Rico required USACE to install temporary generators at more than 1,200 critical facilities—four times the previous post-storm record—and to employ microgrids in nine isolated communities. The mission required thousands of contractors, site assessment, connection, maintenance, fuel, quality assurance, grid work, and helicopter access; final customer reconnection took roughly 11 months.[^usace-power][^gao-grid]

Winter Storm Uri exposed a different version of the same coupled problem. Texas recorded 1,985 public water systems under boil-water notice, affecting approximately 16.35 million people. Water systems lost treatment and pumping power; generators failed mechanically in freezing conditions; and frozen components created cascades in systems purchasing treated water.[^tceq-uri]

“Mobile power” is therefore not a generator inventory. It is a portfolio of load assessment, generation, energy storage, fuel or renewable input, switchgear, transformers, protection, interconnection, distribution, maintenance, spares, operating crews, emissions and noise control, and restoration sequencing.

### Medical match and endurance

GAO found that HHS requested Defense Department teams specialized in trauma and surgery after Hurricanes Irma and Maria when isolated communities predominantly needed chronic and primary care. HHS did not have a full understanding of the capabilities and limits of its support agencies.[^gao-health]

Earlier Katrina evidence showed facilities planned for far shorter isolation than occurred: one hospital planned for staff to remain three days, but replacements did not arrive for two weeks; it held three days of food and supplies and a week of generator fuel.[^gao-vulnerable]

The relevant capacity is not “beds.” It is usable care by acuity and specialty, staffed over time, with patient movement, pharmacy, oxygen, dialysis, laboratory, infection control, food, water, waste, power, communications, security, records, and relief crews.

### Debris, contaminated land, and housing

Debris is not a cleanup afterthought. It controls access, utility repair, housing placement, environmental safety, and return. For wildfire major disasters in fiscal years 2019–2023, debris removal represented 51 percent of FEMA Public Assistance obligations, compared with 24 percent for non-wildfire disasters. Burned structures can leave contaminated ash and soil that prevent temporary housing inside the burn zone until land and utilities are safe.[^gao-wildfire]

This implies a standing production system for characterization, segregation, hazardous-material handling, excavation, transport, disposal, recycling, site certification, utility coordination, and temporary housing—not simply generic earthmoving equipment.

## Three distinctions that must govern the force

### Stock is not flow

Supplies can exist nationally while broken ports, bridges, fuel nodes, warehouses, communications, trucks, drivers, and receiving organizations prevent delivery. Force packages must be sized end to end, from source through last mile and waste return.

### Nominal capacity is not usable capacity

A generator without switchgear and fuel, a hospital team with the wrong specialties, a communications device without trained users, or a contractor without priority access is not operational capacity. Readiness must be measured as complete mission packages under the conditions of use.

### Rescue is not stabilization

Initial life safety and sustained civilian life support have different equipment, professional mixes, clocks, and command needs. Search and rescue may peak in hours; water, power, healthcare, debris, shelter, and utility repair can remain critical for months. The force needs doctrine, units, and transfer criteria for both.

## Denominator guardrails for later models

The financial and benefits model must preserve the following categories separately:

| Topic | Categories that must not be merged |
| --- | --- |
| Loss | observed event loss; expected annual loss; insured loss; direct total loss; indirect economic loss; federal obligation; federal outlay |
| Dollars | nominal; CPI-adjusted; construction-cost-adjusted; exposure or replacement value; present value |
| Mortality | certified direct; certified direct plus indirect; modeled excess mortality; value-of-statistical-life monetization |
| Displacement | people; households; evacuation; temporary displacement; movement count; permanent migration |
| Power | customers interrupted; people affected; megawatts unavailable; customer-hours; critical-load service; time to restoration |
| Water | systems affected; population under notice; treatment capacity; potable output; service-days lost; wastewater release |
| Capacity | owned inventory; mission-capable inventory; crewed capacity; deployable capacity; delivered effect; sustainable concurrent capacity |

The first workbook will encode these distinctions as explicit dimensions rather than bury them in notes.

## Research gaps that matter more than another anecdote

The case literature has reached diminishing returns on the existence of cross-lifeline failure. The next evidence effort should prioritize missing measurements:

1. time-phased national demand curves by service effect and event type;
2. customer-hours and restoration distributions for power, water, communications, healthcare, mobility, and housing;
3. current mission-capable federal, state, mutual-aid, commercial, and nonprofit capacity by function;
4. the receiving capacity required to convert strategic lift into local delivery;
5. responder self-sustainment and rotation requirements;
6. the number and scale of simultaneous events that exhaust specialized people, aircraft, contractors, warehouses, generators, and transport;
7. measurable stabilization and transfer thresholds; and
8. an integrated all-hazard loss and outcome ledger to replace incompatible partial series.
