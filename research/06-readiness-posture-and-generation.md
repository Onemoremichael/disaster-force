# Readiness, posture, and force generation

## Readiness is the product

The Department of Resilience does not principally buy vehicles, facilities, or headcount. It buys **credible time-bounded promises of civilian effect**.

A readiness claim is valid only when a complete mission package can:

1. receive lawful direction;
2. assemble qualified people and functioning equipment;
3. move from its current location;
4. enter or create a receiving node in the damaged system;
5. begin producing the required service;
6. distribute that service to intended users;
7. sustain output through planned rotations and resupply; and
8. transfer the service without a second collapse.

This definition is deliberately stricter than “available to deploy.” It shifts readiness reporting from administrative status to an end-to-end consequence chain.

## The readiness equation

For a mission package (m) at time (t), usable ready capacity is constrained by its weakest essential component:

\[
C^{ready}_{m,t} = \min(P, E, L, R, S, I, A)_{m,t}
\]

where:

- (P) = qualified personnel and replacement crews;
- (E) = functioning, configured equipment;
- (L) = lift and movement capacity;
- (R) = reception and onward-movement capacity;
- (S) = sustainment, spares, fuel, and consumables;
- (I) = physical, data, utility, and command interfaces; and
- (A) = authority and agreements required for employment.

Each term is expressed in the same effect unit—such as critical-load megawatts, compliant liters delivered, staffed critical-care bed-days, route tonnage, or people safely sheltered—not as an unrelated percentage. The minimum matters because zero safe grid connection makes a warehouse of generators operationally equivalent to zero delivered power.

Time to stabilized life-support is decomposed as:

\[
T^{effect} = T_{decision} + T_{alert} + T_{marshal} + T_{move} + T_{receive} + T_{setup} + T_{connect} + T_{distribute}
\]

This allows investment to target the actual delay. Faster aircraft do little when the binding term is debris-blocked distribution, unassessed electrical loads, unavailable operators, or a missing wastewater discharge permit.

## A national concurrency standard

A force equal in seriousness to defense should not be sized to the average disaster, the most recent disaster, or one maximum event. It should be sized to a **declared simultaneous-campaign standard**.

The initial standard to test is:

> **3C + 5M + 2G + 20R**

- **3C:** three concurrent catastrophic domestic stabilization campaigns;
- **5M:** five additional major regional operations;
- **2G:** two major international or allied response operations; and
- **20R:** a 20 percent immediately employable residual reserve in nationally scarce effects after those commitments.

At least one catastrophic domestic case must be no-notice, one must be a territory or remote/island case, and two must create correlated infrastructure failures. At least one global operation must require intercontinental access and independent sustainment. The standard should be tested through six months, not merely initial entry.

This is a design hypothesis, not a derived answer. It is intentionally more demanding than historical single-event planning. Later models will compare it with lower and higher standards, including a national grid or public-health catastrophe that does not fit regional counting.

### Why a residual reserve is explicit

Systems optimized to deploy everything to the current emergency create a national readiness cliff. NIFC preparedness levels already make the danger visible: at the highest level, national resources are heavily committed, inactive areas approach drawdown limits, and emergency measures are needed to sustain operations.[^nifc-pl] The Department should treat residual capacity as a policy choice, not as whatever happens to remain.

### Campaign definitions

A **catastrophic campaign** is not defined by declaration or dollar loss. It is a multi-region or metropolitan-scale breakdown that requires national command, strategic mobility, several service-effect portfolios, multiple crew rotations, and material production or repair.

A **major operation** can be led by one geographic command with limited national reinforcement and does not consume a dominant share of more than one nationally scarce capability.

A **global operation** is sized by effect and access, not diplomatic visibility. A small search team is not one of the two global campaigns; a sustained multi-sector stabilization effort is.

## Readiness bands

The Pass 1 readiness categories become operational promises:

| Band | Employment promise | Typical role | Readiness evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| R0 — Persistent | Already operating or present in the community | Forecasting, liaison, monitoring, mitigation, daily-response interface | Staffed watch, functioning local systems, current relationships and plans |
| R1 — Immediate | Initial effect within 0–6 hours of civil authorization | Reconnaissance, command integration, local rescue, communications, initial health and hazard action | Alert crew, loaded kit, local transport, no external reception dependency |
| R2 — Rapid | Initial effect within 6–24 hours | Regional mission modules and first national entry packages | Certified crew and equipment, transport slot, reception plan, 72-hour support |
| R3 — Reinforcing | Initial effect within 24–72 hours | Heavy regional and national task groups | Complete movement and staging plan, seven-day support, replacement crew identified |
| R4 — Mass | Full campaign output within 3–7 days | Strategic fleets, major public works, large medical and shelter capacity | Multiple lift cycles, theater distribution, 30-day sustainment and maintenance plan |
| R5 — Enduring | Continuous output from day 7 through 180+ | Rotational stabilization, infrastructure bridging, housing and economic continuity | Relief units, depot support, industrial replenishment, transfer milestones |

An R1 designation cannot be applied to an entire heavy system because a liaison team can arrive quickly. Every module reports initial and full effect separately.

## Preparedness conditions

The department should maintain national **Resilience Conditions** from RC-1 through RC-5. They differ from unit readiness bands: a condition describes national demand and the measures needed to preserve options.

| Condition | National posture | Illustrative actions |
|---|---|---|
| RC-1 — Routine | Demand within local and regional capacity; strategic reserve intact | Training, maintenance, mitigation missions, ordinary global deployments |
| RC-2 — Elevated | Credible seasonal or event risk in one or more commands | Increase watches, protect maintenance completions, stage selected modules, warn reserves and industry |
| RC-3 — National reinforcement | Multiple regions active or one catastrophic campaign likely | National allocation board convenes; strategic lift and stocks prioritized; reserve activation begins |
| RC-4 — General surge | Several campaigns compete for scarce effects | Cancel nonessential training deployments; mobilize reserve formations and certified industry; activate allied arrangements |
| RC-5 — National mobilization | Demand threatens the concurrency standard or a systemic national failure is underway | Emergency production, broad reserve activation, extraordinary transport and infrastructure priority, continuous cabinet-level allocation |

Condition changes should be driven by forecast demand **and** current force availability. A quiet hazard week with half the fleet in depot is not RC-1 readiness.

## Force-generation cycle

### Four force pools

Every major formation belongs to one of four pools:

1. **Available** — certified and inside its employment window;
2. **Committed** — deployed, pre-positioned beyond routine posture, or reserved for a named contingency;
3. **Preparing** — training, integrating replacements, or completing scheduled maintenance for the next window; and
4. **Regenerating** — recovering people and equipment, incorporating lessons, and restoring stocks.

The pools prevent the same unit from being simultaneously counted as deployed, available, and training. Strategic assets may use different cycle lengths, but not different accounting logic.

### Rotation factors

A deployment lasting days may be supported by one crew plus relief. A mission lasting months requires several crews, maintenance teams, and replacement equipment. For each module:

\[
Q^{owned}_m \geq \frac{D^{peak}_m \times K^{concurrency}_m \times F^{rotation}_m}{A^{material}_m \times A^{transport}_m}
\]

where (D^{peak}) is effect demand, (K^{concurrency}) captures simultaneous campaigns, (F^{rotation}) is the force-generation depth, and the two availability terms prevent nominal inventory from masquerading as usable capacity.

The model should test at least:

- 1.5–2 complete crews per continuously operated short-duration module;
- 3–4 crews or units per enduring deployed requirement;
- higher factors for licensed clinical staff, aviation, maritime nuclear systems, hazardous-material operations, and other fatigue-sensitive work; and
- equipment pools that include depot maintenance, training, test, attrition, contamination hold, and strategic reserve.

These are analytical ranges, not final standards.

### Regeneration is operational work

Returning equipment to a depot, replenishing a cache, treating responder injury, restoring family stability, retraining a crew, updating software, cleaning contaminated systems, and converting lessons into design changes are all part of the campaign. The operation is not complete when the public deployment ends.

## A layered national posture

The Force should not be centralized in a few spectacular bases or dispersed so evenly that scarce capability cannot be maintained. It needs six layers.

### 1. Community interface network

Permanent liaison, planning, data, recruitment, and small rapid-response elements would be distributed across states, territories, tribal compacts, and major metropolitan areas. These are not mini-bases full of every capability. They are the institutional connective tissue that makes national capacity usable locally.

### 2. Forward operating stations

Smaller stations place high-frequency, time-sensitive modules near recurring hazards and hard-to-reach populations. They support R0–R2 effects, local exercises, maintenance, and pre-impact staging. Stations may be seasonal or mobile where risk migrates.

### 3. Regional resilience complexes

Each domestic geographic command would operate several large complexes containing joint formations, air and ground mobility, warehouses, maintenance, training areas, medical and utility modules, command facilities, and reception capacity. No region should depend on one complex.

### 4. Strategic mobility and production bases

These bases connect heavy formations to major airports, seaports, rail, highways, pipelines, and inland waterways. They contain loading infrastructure, strategic stocks, deployable port and airfield capacity, and the maintenance needed for major air and maritime systems.

### 5. National depots, arsenals, laboratories, and proving grounds

Deep maintenance, overhaul, production surge, destructive testing, environmental qualification, and long-term storage require facilities separated from daily operational bases. These sites are part of readiness and should be geographically redundant.

### 6. Global access and pre-positioning network

The Department would maintain standing liaison offices, access agreements, forward stocks, partner training sites, and selected logistics hubs abroad. This posture is a foreign-policy instrument: rapid, visible stabilization capacity offered under host-nation authority.

## Basing as a network-design problem

Base locations should be selected through a transparent optimization model rather than congressional inheritance or a single hazard map. The objective should minimize population- and vulnerability-weighted time to delivered effect while preserving survivability and cost discipline.

The model must include:

- correlated hazard exposure of bases and their support utilities;
- access to redundant air, sea, rail, road, and inland-water routes;
- distance to high-risk populations and critical infrastructure;
- travel-time distributions under damaged-network conditions;
- workforce, training space, maintenance, water, and energy requirements;
- environmental and community burdens;
- territorial, tribal, Arctic, island, and remote obligations;
- interaction with reserve recruiting and industrial suppliers;
- the ability to receive units from other commands; and
- adversarial or cyber disruption for international and national-security cases.

The design should favor **paired and dissimilar nodes**. A coastal maritime complex may need a protected inland counterpart connected by rail and water. An earthquake region should not keep all bridge, communications, and medical modules on the same fault system. A wildfire aviation base should have seasonal alternates outside the same smoke and wind regime.

## Pre-positioning doctrine

Pre-positioning buys time only when it includes the whole effect chain. The department should distinguish:

- **warm position:** crewed or on watch, connected, and capable of immediate effect;
- **ready position:** equipment maintained and loaded with assigned crews and movement plan;
- **cached position:** inspected equipment and consumables with a separate crew and transport requirement;
- **industrial position:** components, tooling, and contracts able to produce or repair capacity within a stated time; and
- **paper position:** an agreement or catalog source, which is not counted as ready capacity.

Forecastable hazards should trigger anticipatory movement based on expected avoided delay and the risk of trapping the asset. Units should be allowed to stage before formal incident activation; employment still follows the civil-authority rules in the institutional architecture.

## Receiving the force

A damaged community cannot be assumed to absorb a large federal deployment. Reception is therefore an organic mission.

The first joint package should be able to:

- survey ports, airfields, roads, waterways, communications, fuel, power, water, and staging land;
- establish austere command and air-ground-maritime movement control;
- open or construct initial reception nodes;
- support responders without consuming scarce local food, lodging, fuel, or medical care;
- create cargo and personnel accountability;
- separate clean, contaminated, medical, and hazardous flows; and
- expand throughput in deliberate increments.

FEMA's US&R model recognizes that even self-sufficient teams still need secure space to store equipment, plan, eat, and sleep.[^fema-reception] At peer-force scale, reception must be designed like a major operation, not an assumed local courtesy.

## Strategic mobility

An equal force cannot rely entirely on defense lift or commercial capacity that disappears under correlated demand. The Department should own or control a mobility floor sufficient to execute its concurrency standard, while contracting and allied capacity provide economical depth.

Strategic Mobility Command must manage:

- intercontinental and continental airlift;
- heavy and high-volume sealift;
- coastal, riverine, and lake movement;
- rail and common-user trucking;
- accessible evacuation transport;
- medical evacuation and en-route care;
- movement of hazardous, oversized, refrigerated, and high-value cargo;
- deployable cargo handling and transshipment; and
- national movement priorities during constrained infrastructure.

Platform choices are deliberately deferred. The requirement is an assured movement portfolio with known payload, cube, range, runway or port dependence, loading time, fleet availability, crew depth, fuel demand, and reception burden.

## Sustainment and endurance

Every campaign plan should contain four supply horizons:

1. **entry load** carried with the module;
2. **initial bridge** arriving before the entry load is exhausted;
3. **steady-state pipeline** supporting continuous output and rotations; and
4. **industrial replenishment** replacing consumed, damaged, contaminated, or life-limited items.

The Department should calculate a daily theater demand signal for fuel, water, food, medical materiel, repair parts, consumables, waste removal, responder shelter, and transport. It should also report the service burden imposed by its own presence. A force that restores 100 megawatts while consuming 40 megawatts locally has a different net effect than one supported from an independent system.

## Peacetime employment that generates readiness

A force of this scale cannot remain idle between catastrophes, but routine work must improve rather than consume readiness. Appropriate missions include:

- prescribed fire and landscape-risk reduction;
- levee, drainage, watershed, and coastal restoration;
- utility hardening and rapid-repair exercises;
- public-health missions and reserve clinical practice;
- bridge, port, airfield, and communications contingency construction;
- community cooling, drought, and water-security support;
- large national and allied exercises;
- international disaster response and partner training;
- prototype testing and operational experimentation; and
- public works that exercise the exact crews and systems required for deployment.

The governing test is whether the activity produces trained crews, validated equipment, updated plans, stronger civil relationships, or reduced hazard exposure. Routine work that merely keeps personnel busy is a readiness tax.

## Global posture as statecraft

Global Resilience Command should treat stabilization capacity as a sustained diplomatic offer rather than episodic charity. It would maintain:

- bilateral and regional access agreements;
- common technical and data standards;
- multinational exercises and exchange officers;
- forward assessment and liaison teams;
- pre-cleared customs, aviation, maritime, medical, and hazardous-material procedures;
- modular stocks compatible with partner infrastructure;
- partner maintenance and training capacity; and
- transfer, cost, and sovereignty rules negotiated before crisis.

The European rescEU system shows that centrally financed capacities can be hosted across participating states and mobilized when national systems are insufficient.[^resceu-posture] The Department of Resilience would extend the idea into a much larger American capability that allies can plan around and eventually co-produce.

## Readiness reporting and public accountability

The Department should publish two views of readiness.

The **operational view** may protect sensitive infrastructure or vulnerability details but must give leaders exact unit, crew, lift, sustainment, and risk data.

The **public view** should report:

- required versus ready effect capacity by portfolio and readiness band;
- the share unavailable for personnel, equipment, maintenance, transport, sustainment, or interface reasons;
- residual national capacity under the concurrency standard;
- time-to-effect exercise results;
- deployed effect and population served;
- safety, environmental, equity, and responder-health outcomes;
- regeneration time and cost; and
- major model assumptions and uncertainty.

Counting assets, employees, dollars obligated, or missions opened is inadequate. The public promise is delivered and sustainable civilian effect.

## Decisions carried into the quantitative pass

The modeling program must now determine:

1. the effect demand for each design reference mission;
2. which effects are nationally scarce versus locally abundant;
3. the force-generation and rotation factor for every module;
4. the number and geometry of geographic commands, complexes, stations, depots, and global hubs;
5. the organic-versus-contracted mobility floor;
6. the active, reserve, state, tribal, auxiliary, and industrial mix;
7. the procurement and industrial ramp needed to reach 3C + 5M + 2G + 20R;
8. readiness loss from maintenance, training, contamination, and transport competition;
9. annual operating, recapitalization, and mobilization cost; and
10. sensitivity to a higher or lower national ambition.

Only after these outputs exist should the project specify major platforms. The platform is the physical consequence of the readiness promise.
