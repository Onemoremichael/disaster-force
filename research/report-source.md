# Disaster Force: projecting resilience as national power

**Status:** Research manuscript in development

**Audience:** Senior U.S. national-security, resilience, infrastructure, public-health, humanitarian, and industrial-policy leadership

**Geography:** United States with allied and global deployment

**Time horizon:** Mature force after a 25-year build

## Executive answer

The mature institution should be designed around assured restoration of civilian life-support systems—not around ownership of spectacular platforms or the bureaucratic boundaries of existing agencies. Its core performance measure is time to stabilized life-support: how quickly a defined population receives a specified bundle of minimum services, at stated quality and duration, despite damaged infrastructure and simultaneous demand.

The proposed end state is a **Department of Resilience**: a cabinet-level peer to the defense establishment with six operating services; unified domestic and global commands; strategic mobility and sustainment; active, reserve, state, territorial, tribal, auxiliary, and industrial components; academies and technical colleges; laboratories and proving grounds; and a sovereign production, depot, and logistics base. It preserves civil authority over community priorities while radically centralizing the ownership of scarce, mobile capacity.

## Scope and assumptions

The project assumes full political authorization for a zero-based build. A $1 trillion, 25-year program is treated as one illustrative research-and-capital tranche, not as a ceiling on an institution intended to match the seriousness of national defense. Current agencies are evidence and potential transition sources, not protected design constraints; functions may be absorbed, split, transferred, or retired.

## Research findings

Pass 1 finds that:

1. Different hazards repeatedly converge on the same service failures: power, water, mobility, communications, healthcare, shelter, safety, and environmental control.
2. Current U.S. doctrine is primarily a coordination and support architecture in which local jurisdictions and infrastructure owners retain operating and restoration responsibility.
3. Catastrophic events break that model when local receiving capacity fails, several lifelines collapse together, and multiple events compete for the same national people, contractors, transport, stocks, and facilities.
4. Stock is not flow, nominal capacity is not usable capacity, and rescue is not stabilization.
5. The force must be sized as complete mission packages with command, lift, reception, specialty match, sustainment, maintenance, transfer, and regeneration—not as inventories of isolated equipment.
6. Compound and concurrent events, not a single median disaster, are the correct force-sizing case.

See [Force-design framework](02-force-design-framework.md) and [Empirical baseline](03-empirical-baseline.md).

Pass 2 finds that:

1. Coordination cannot substitute for ownership. A national promise requires complete mission packages, organic readiness, mobility, sustainment, replacement crews, and regeneration.
2. The appropriate scale is a national resilience establishment, not an enlarged emergency-management agency. Six operating services should generate forces; unified geographic and functional commands should employ them.
3. The force should be sized against a simultaneous-campaign standard. The initial hypothesis is 3 catastrophic domestic campaigns, 5 major regional operations, 2 global stabilization operations, and a 20 percent residual reserve in scarce effects.
4. Readiness is constrained by the weakest link among qualified people, functioning equipment, lift, reception, sustainment, interfaces, and authority.
5. The national posture requires community interfaces, forward stations, regional complexes, strategic mobility bases, national depots and laboratories, and overseas access and pre-positioning.
6. Existing institutions may seed the transition but do not define the end state. The Department should possess its own air, land, maritime, infrastructure, health, and community-stabilization professions and industrial systems.

See [Institutional architecture](04-institutional-architecture.md), [Force composition](05-force-composition.md), and [Readiness, posture, and force generation](06-readiness-posture-and-generation.md).

Pass 3 finds that:

1. The correct demand record is a population or critical function, in a location and access class, requiring a service level during a time interval.
2. Force demand is the gap between phased service requirements and credible restoration by households, utilities, communities, markets, and partners—not the whole gross outage.
3. Ten design reference missions are needed to expose different requirements for warning, access, correlation, duration, geography, workforce, and international employment.
4. USGS's HayWired and ARkStorm scenarios provide initial external anchors: millions without water and large displacement in a no-notice earthquake case; mass evacuation, hundreds of miles of flooding, and months of lifeline restoration in a megaflood case.
5. Water, food, shelter, sanitation, power, thermal safety, health, communications, mobility, and public works require phased effect units. Production at the plant or arrival node is not delivered service.
6. Time to stabilized life-support is measured when the target population receives the stabilization bundle, not when the first federal element arrives.

See [Mission-engineering method](07-mission-engineering-method.md), [Design reference missions](08-design-reference-missions.md), and [Service-level library](09-service-level-library.md).

## Material limitations and disagreements

- The United States has no harmonized all-hazard ledger for loss, service interruption, displacement, mortality, response capacity, and spending.
- NOAA event losses, FEMA expected annual losses, CBO estimates, insurance claims, federal obligations, and social costs measure different objects and cannot be added without adjustment.
- Mortality estimates vary substantially across direct, indirect, and excess-mortality methods.
- Current public sources do not expose mission-capable capacity, throughput, endurance, or simultaneous-theater coverage for many federal and commercial capabilities.
- The 3C + 5M + 2G + 20R standard is an ambitious design hypothesis pending quantitative demand and rotation models.
- Service boundaries, command geography, active–reserve mix, personnel strength, and funding parity require sensitivity analysis before becoming recommendations.
- Only two design reference missions currently have external quantitative anchors; the remaining cases require sourced low, base, and high demand curves.
- Emergency service-level references are planning inputs, not universal safety standards or substitutes for U.S. codes, clinical judgment, and local requirements.
- Platform concepts remain premature until time-phased requirements, alternatives, and lifecycle implications are documented.
