# Force-design framework

## Interim finding

The mature force should not be organized around hazards alone. Fire, flood, storm, earthquake, heat, drought, and biological events have specialized control problems, but they repeatedly converge on the same social failure: communities lose the systems that keep people alive and make organized action possible.

The force therefore requires two intersecting structures:

1. **Service-effect portfolios** organized around the civilian systems to be stabilized.
2. **Operational functions** that assemble, project, employ, sustain, and transfer the capacity required to stabilize them.

Hazard specialization sits inside that matrix. It does not replace it.

## From force projection to resilience projection

Army doctrine treats force projection as more than transportation. It is the ability to project the military instrument of national power in response to operational requirements, encompassing mobilization, deployment, employment, sustainment, and redeployment. It depends on trained forces, pre-positioning, ports and bases, reception, onward movement, integration, and the industrial and logistics systems behind them.[^army-projection]

This project adapts that logic without importing military purpose or culture:

> **Resilience projection** is the ability to deliver and sustain a civilian life-support and stabilization effect at the place and time required, despite damaged infrastructure, constrained access, and simultaneous demand.

Its continuous processes are:

`anticipate → posture → mobilize → deploy → receive and integrate → employ → sustain → transfer → redeploy and regenerate`

The addition of **transfer** is decisive. A disaster force succeeds when it restores or bridges civilian systems and hands them back to legitimate local, public, cooperative, or commercial operators. Permanent substitution is usually failure.

## What changes from the current model

The National Response Framework describes optimal U.S. disaster response as locally executed, state or territorial managed, and federally supported, with businesses, infrastructure owners, nongovernmental organizations, and the public engaged throughout. It states that infrastructure owners and operators normally carry primary responsibility for operating and repairing their systems.[^nrf]

That model is necessary for most incidents and should remain the normal case. It is structurally vulnerable when:

- local government and infrastructure operators are themselves incapacitated;
- multiple lifelines fail together;
- demand exceeds mutual-aid, commercial, or contract capacity;
- transport and communications failures prevent resources from becoming effects;
- multiple disasters compete for the same workforce, warehouse space, generators, aircraft, contractors, or supplies; or
- the needed system does not exist in deployable form.

The new institution does not need to own every resource. It does need to own or control an **assured minimum national capacity** for effects whose absence causes cascading mortality, displacement, or system failure. The research must determine which effects satisfy that test.

## Civilian end states: the service-effect portfolios

FEMA defines community lifelines as the most fundamental services whose stabilization enables the rest of society to function. The current construct includes safety and security; food, hydration, and shelter; health and medical; energy; communications; transportation; hazardous materials; and water systems.[^lifelines]

Those lifelines are a strong outcome architecture, but they are impact categories rather than a force structure. This study expands them into nine portfolios for requirements analysis:

| Service-effect portfolio | Minimum end state the force may need to create |
| --- | --- |
| Safety and civil coordination | Survivors, responders, government, and essential sites can operate under a legitimate common plan. |
| Food, shelter, and essential goods | Affected populations can access safe calories, basic commodities, climate-safe refuge, and dignified support. |
| Health and medical | Time-critical care, routine care, public health, patient movement, medical supply, and fatality management remain available. |
| Energy and thermal safety | Critical loads and population heat/cooling needs have reliable power or direct thermal service. |
| Communications and information | Responders, authorities, infrastructure operators, and the public have trusted local access and survivable backhaul. |
| Mobility and access | People, responders, commodities, heavy equipment, and waste can move through or around damaged networks. |
| Water and sanitation | Potable water, wastewater control, hygiene, drainage, and contamination monitoring meet safe minimums. |
| Hazard and environmental control | Fire, floodwater, unstable structures, debris, hazardous materials, smoke, and contamination are contained or reduced. |
| Housing and economic continuity | Bridging infrastructure allows households, schools, public administration, and markets to resume while permanent recovery proceeds. |

The ninth portfolio makes the rescue-to-recovery boundary explicit. Temporary housing, public services, and market access are often where a short emergency becomes long displacement.

## Operational functions: what the force must repeatedly do

The service portfolios describe **what must work for society**. Operational functions describe **what the force must be able to do** across every portfolio.

| Function | Purpose | Representative measures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anticipate and sense | Detect hazards, exposure, damage, survivors, flows, and system state | coverage, latency, accuracy, persistence |
| Command and integrate | Build a common operating picture, establish objectives, allocate capacity, and coordinate lawful authorities | time to unified plan, data completeness, decision latency |
| Reach and receive | Enter damaged or infrastructure-poor areas and turn arriving resources into operating capability | time to first effect, throughput at damaged nodes, access independence |
| Move and supply | Transport and distribute people, equipment, commodities, fuel, spares, and waste | tonnes or people per day, last-mile reach, stockout probability |
| Generate and connect | Produce water, electricity, heat, cooling, oxygen, connectivity, and other essential services; connect them safely to users | output, quality, setup time, connection rate, energy/fuel intensity |
| Control and protect | Suppress, pump, drain, isolate, cool, shore, ventilate, decontaminate, and defend critical systems | area or volume controlled, rate of spread reduced, protected population |
| Rescue and treat | Locate, extract, triage, move, and care for people and animals | rescue rate, care capacity by acuity, time to definitive care |
| Build and restore | Clear, excavate, bridge, repair, erect, lay, inspect, and certify temporary or permanent works | engineering output per day, restored service connections, material flow |
| Sustain and regenerate | Keep deployed systems and people effective, safe, maintained, rested, and supplied | mission-capable rate, endurance, rotation capacity, maintenance backlog |
| Transfer and recover | Hand services to durable operators, remove temporary systems, replenish stocks, learn, and return to readiness | transfer time, dependency avoided, reset time, lessons closed |

These functions are deliberately closer to joint functions than to an agency directory. The Joint Staff emphasizes that functions reinforce one another and must be integrated for mission accomplishment; the same is true here.[^joint-functions]

## The primary performance measure

Inputs such as dollars obligated, personnel deployed, or tonnes shipped are poor measures of operational success. The proposed primary measure is:

> **Time to stabilized life-support:** elapsed time from impact or mission authorization until a defined population has reliable access to a stated bundle of minimum services at specified quality and duration.

This must be disaggregated. A metropolitan hospital, an isolated dialysis population, a wastewater plant, and a cooling shelter have different clocks and minimum service bundles.

FEMA's THIRA methodology provides a useful grammar: standardized capability targets combine a critical task, the impact to be managed, and a timeframe. Current capability is then compared with the target to expose a gap.[^thira] Disaster Force extends that approach from community preparedness to national force sizing.

## Requirement record

Every proposed force element will trace to a requirement record with the following fields:

| Field | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Consequence chain | What hazard-to-human sequence creates the need? |
| Civilian end state | Which service portfolio must be stabilized, for whom, and to what minimum quality? |
| Operational effect | What must the force physically or organizationally accomplish? |
| Impact quantity | Population, area, load, flow, bed mix, route length, debris volume, fire intensity, or other demand measure? |
| Time | How soon must the effect begin, reach scale, and complete? |
| Duration | How long must it operate before transfer or relief? |
| Environment | What access, terrain, weather, contamination, and infrastructure conditions apply? |
| Concurrency | What other missions may be drawing on the same people, platforms, bases, and supplies? |
| Integration | What local, tribal, state, federal, allied, utility, health, commercial, and NGO interfaces are mandatory? |
| Force package | What complete combination of command, people, payloads, lift, protection, maintenance, and sustainment produces the effect? |
| Alternatives | Can mitigation, fixed infrastructure, stockpiles, mutual aid, contracts, or smaller distributed systems do it better? |
| Transfer | Who receives the capability, under what standards, and what happens to temporary assets? |

## Composition without premature organizational boxes

Before naming services or commands, the force can be described as six necessary layers:

### 1. Persistent local interface

Small embedded or regionally assigned elements maintain plans, site data, infrastructure interfaces, exercises, credentials, and community relationships. They do not replace local emergency management. They make national capacity usable on arrival.

### 2. High-readiness operational force

Permanently staffed, trained, and maintained multi-function units provide command, reconnaissance, access, medical, engineering, logistics, utilities, communications, and hazard-control effects on short notice.

### 3. Regional depth

A distributed network of bases, depots, warehouses, training centers, maintenance facilities, medical nodes, utility connection kits, and pre-positioned stocks reduces time and dependence on intact national transport.

### 4. Strategic projection and scarce capability

National assets provide long-range lift, high-output mobile infrastructure, specialized control, deep medical capacity, major construction, and surge command. These are the capabilities whose economics or rarity prevent broad regional distribution.

### 5. Scalable reserve and civil mobilization

A trained reserve, credentialed professional auxiliary, and pre-negotiated commercial mobilization system expand health, engineering, construction, utilities, transport, logistics, animal care, public administration, and human services. Contracts are not counted as readiness unless capacity, priority, training, integration, and exercise obligations are enforceable.

### 6. Science, acquisition, and industrial system

Laboratories, test centers, program offices, standards bodies, production facilities, shipyards, aviation plants, vehicle manufacturers, software and communications suppliers, and public-interest capital form the production base. They must be designed as part of the force, not treated as background vendors.

## Force-package hierarchy

The eventual force should be sized and assembled through packages, not individual assets:

1. **Capability element:** the smallest trained team and payload able to produce a measured effect.
2. **Mission module:** several elements with command, power, communications, maintenance, safety, and supplies sufficient for independent employment.
3. **Response task group:** a multi-function package tailored to a design reference mission.
4. **Regional response force:** command, basing, lift, and sustainment able to employ several task groups and support local partners.
5. **National response force:** strategic lift, scarce capabilities, replacement flow, industrial mobilization, and command for simultaneous major events.
6. **Global stabilization group:** a legally and diplomatically configured package for allied or humanitarian deployment without reducing the domestic floor below its standard.

This hierarchy will later support an inventory model. A ship count is meaningless without the task groups it completes, the ports it can use, the units it carries, and the second event it leaves uncovered.

## Candidate readiness bands

These bands are initial model categories, not final standards:

| Band | Initial effect | Typical purpose |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| R0 — persistent | already operating | sensing, liaison, plans, pre-positioning, risk reduction |
| R1 — immediate | minutes to 6 hours | warning, command activation, local rescue, remote assessment |
| R2 — rapid | 6 to 24 hours | national incident teams, air-mobile packages, communications, medical and utility bridge capacity |
| R3 — heavy | 24 to 72 hours | major engineering, pumping, distributed power and water, field hospitals, surface logistics |
| R4 — sustained | 3 to 14 days | full task groups, strategic maritime systems, replacement crews, durable temporary networks |
| R5 — enduring | 2 weeks to 12 months | bridging infrastructure, housing, industrial repair, recovery support, transfer |

The research must determine which service effects belong in each band and how value decays with delay. “Fast” is not a universal requirement; the right target is the earliest time at which additional speed no longer changes the civilian outcome enough to justify its cost.

## Design reference mission families

Platforms and force size will be tested against mission families rather than a single worst case:

1. Coastal megastorm with port damage, grid loss, saltwater intrusion, heat, hospital stress, and mass displacement.
2. Inland river-basin flood spanning several states, with levee damage, contaminated water, isolated towns, agricultural loss, and damaged bridges.
3. Simultaneous western fire complexes during drought and extreme heat, producing smoke exposure, evacuations, utility shutdowns, and competition for aircraft and crews.
4. No-notice metropolitan earthquake with port, airport, bridge, hospital, water, gas, and communications damage.
5. Regional extreme-temperature and grid failure event in which water, fuel, health, and communications fail in cascade.
6. Remote island or Arctic catastrophe with little local depth and long external supply lines.
7. Domestic major disaster concurrent with an allied or global stabilization mission.
8. Multi-month public-health event that competes for logistics, medical, communications, shelter, and workforce capacity.

Each will be expressed at several severities and access conditions. The concurrency standard will be selected only after the demand model shows the cost and uncovered risk of competing alternatives.

## What the evidence already rules out

Several design shortcuts are inconsistent with observed failure patterns:

- **A platform without reception and last-mile capacity is not a delivered capability.** Puerto Rico response reviews repeatedly show that distance, damaged access, local incapacity, and logistics slowed the conversion of federal resources into services.[^gao-2017]
- **Medical capacity cannot be defined as beds or trauma teams alone.** HHS requested teams whose trauma and surgical specialization did not match the chronic and primary care needs of isolated communities after Hurricanes Irma and Maria.[^gao-health]
- **Temporary power cannot be separated from grid, fuel, water, and facility interfaces.** Puerto Rico's roughly 11-month restoration and unprecedented federal repair role reflected both physical damage and a local utility unable to lead the task at required scale.[^gao-grid]
- **Communications restoration is an operational function, not merely a regulator's coordination role.** GAO found that unclear federal responsibilities after Maria could contribute to confusion and delay, later prompting clarified guidance.[^gao-comms]
- **Concurrency must be a first-order sizing variable.** Four sequential 2017 disasters overwhelmed FEMA's workforce and produced contractor and debris-removal shortages; FEMA's 2024 preparedness review likewise describes competing demand for generators, supplies, storage, and response resources.[^gao-2017][^npr-2024]
- **Compound hazards must drive the test set.** The Fifth National Climate Assessment finds that connected systems transmit failures across energy, water, transportation, telecommunications, health, and economic activity, and that simultaneous or sequential extremes can strain governance and response capacity beyond the damage from isolated events.[^nca-complex][^nca-compound]

## Next research decisions

Pass 2 must answer five questions before the project can responsibly define the mature institution:

1. Which service effects require assured federal ownership, and which should remain coordination, reserve, contract, or local functions?
2. What population, geographic, and concurrency standards define the domestic readiness floor?
3. Which functions require a common operational profession and chain of command, and which require independent professional governance?
4. How much capability should be regionally distributed versus nationally concentrated?
5. What is the transfer doctrine that prevents emergency capacity from becoming indefinite substitute government?
