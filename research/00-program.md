# Research program

## Purpose

Design a mature United States disaster-response force as a peer to military force projection in funding, institutional value, technological ambition, readiness, and industrial depth. The project assumes full political authorization and does not optimize for near-term legislative or cultural feasibility.

## Audience

Senior national-security, resilience, infrastructure, public-health, humanitarian, industrial-policy, and capital-program leaders. The target standard is a top-tier strategic studies report: empirically grounded, explicit about assumptions, technically literate, and rigorous about uncertainty.

## Central question

If the United States treated disaster prevention, response, and stabilization as a primary instrument of national power, what mission effects would it need to deliver, what force would reliably deliver them, and what economy would be required to build and sustain that force?

## Design boundary

The force is not a larger FEMA, a civil branch of the military, or a collection of converted military platforms. It is a new purpose-built system. Existing institutions, capabilities, and platforms are evidence and possible interfaces—not design constraints.

The project initially holds political feasibility constant. It does not hold physics, engineering, law of armed conflict, safety, workforce formation, supply chains, environmental impact, opportunity cost, or operational friction constant.

## Research questions

### Mission

1. What recurring physical and social effects do fires, floods, droughts, storms, earthquakes, extreme heat, epidemics, infrastructure failures, and compound events produce?
2. Which effects can be prevented, contained, absorbed, bypassed, or rapidly reversed?
3. What outcomes and time thresholds distinguish rescue from stabilization and reconstruction?

### Operating model

4. What must remain continuously ready, what can be mobilized, and what can be contracted?
5. Which capabilities must operate locally, regionally, nationally, and globally?
6. How should command work when events cross jurisdictions, sectors, borders, or response phases?
7. What common logistics, sensing, communications, power, water, medical, engineering, and data layers support every hazard mission?

### Force design

8. Which requirements imply permanent units, distributed stockpiles, specialized crews, autonomous systems, or large purpose-built platforms?
9. Where does modularity improve readiness, and where does it create a false promise because integration, certification, crew proficiency, or transit time dominates?
10. What fleet size, basing pattern, maintenance model, and surge reserve achieve credible coverage?

### Industrial base and economics

11. How should a $1 trillion, 25-year capitalization be phased across research, procurement, construction, workforce, basing, stockpiles, digital infrastructure, and production capacity?
12. What recurring operating and recapitalization cost follows at maturity?
13. What domestic spillovers, export markets, standards, and dual-use industries emerge?
14. What benefits can be credibly quantified, and which strategic benefits should remain qualitative?

### Strategy and risk

15. How would a standing response force change domestic resilience, alliance relationships, development policy, migration pressure, insurance, and the legitimacy of U.S. power?
16. What failure modes arise from centralization, mission creep, technological dependence, nuclear systems, procurement concentration, or displacement of local capacity?

## Analytical sequence

1. **Baseline:** establish hazard, mortality, loss, displacement, response-time, infrastructure, logistics, and climate-risk baselines.
2. **Effects:** decompose disasters into physical effects and human consequences rather than beginning with agency missions.
3. **Design reference missions:** construct representative compound events that stress distinct environments and timelines.
4. **Capability threads:** derive what the force must sense, decide, move, supply, build, treat, protect, and restore.
5. **Force packages:** combine capability threads into deployable units with explicit readiness, lift, endurance, and support requirements.
6. **Institution:** derive command, regions, bases, workforce, training, reserve, acquisition, science, and partner interfaces.
7. **Platforms:** specify systems only after requirements stabilize; identify common families and hazard-specific exceptions.
8. **Economics:** model capitalization, operating cost, industrial capacity, procurement learning, recapitalization, exports, and avoided-loss ranges.
9. **Stress tests:** run the architecture against domestic, allied, global, and simultaneous scenarios.
10. **Synthesis:** reconcile strategic value, opportunity cost, uncertainty, and governance risks.

## Evidence policy

- Consequential factual claims require primary evidence or explicit corroboration.
- Official data, statutes, doctrine, after-action reports, standards, and technical documentation receive priority.
- Peer-reviewed research and transparent independent analysis are used to interpret or challenge official evidence.
- Source facts, derived estimates, and normative design choices are labeled separately.
- No platform illustration is commissioned until its requirement chain is documented.
- Material contradictions remain visible until resolved or bounded.

## Research products

- A modular Markdown report with executive synthesis, doctrine, institution, force structure, platform families, industrial base, economics, scenarios, risks, and appendices.
- A formula-driven workbook in real 2026 U.S. dollars with editable assumptions, force inventories, acquisition schedules, operations and support, workforce, industrial capacity, scenario outputs, sensitivity analysis, and source provenance.
- Requirement sheets and visual briefs for every rendered platform, including mission, environment, payload, endurance, interfaces, crew, safety, and unresolved engineering questions.

## Pass structure and publication discipline

Each pass is developed on a dedicated branch, reviewed for internal consistency, committed with a detailed message, pushed, merged into `main`, and pushed again.

1. **Foundations:** empirical baseline, first-principles framework, terminology, and design reference mission method.
2. **Force architecture:** doctrine, readiness, geographic posture, command, workforce, and common capability threads.
3. **Systems:** purpose-built maritime, aviation, ground, infrastructure, space, communications, medical, and autonomous families.
4. **Economics:** capitalization, recurring cost, industrial base, workforce, exports, and benefits model.
5. **Operations:** compound scenarios, deployment timelines, capacity analysis, and failure modes.
6. **Synthesis and visuals:** strategic implications, final report integration, and research-grounded renderings.

## Modeling conventions

- Monetary values are real 2026 U.S. dollars unless stated otherwise.
- The initial $1 trillion over 25 years is an illustrative capitalization constraint, not a researched optimum.
- Recurring operations, sustainment, pensions, and recapitalization are shown separately from capitalization.
- Ranges replace point estimates when uncertainty is material.
- Every major assumption remains editable and carries a source or a clear design-study label.
- Avoided losses are not treated as cash revenue and are not counted without a stated counterfactual.
