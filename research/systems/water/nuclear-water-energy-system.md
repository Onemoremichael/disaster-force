# Nuclear Water–Energy System

## Executive judgment

The Department of Resilience should not begin by procuring a nuclear desalination ship. It should begin by creating a **Nuclear Water–Energy System (`N-WES`)**: a strategic utility formation that can deliver persistent electric power and potable water to pre-engineered coastal or island receiving nodes after fixed infrastructure fails.

The distinction changes the architecture. A reactor, desalination train, shore cable, water main, mooring, security zone, laboratory, storage field, grid-forming substation, maintenance organization and licensed workforce do not share the same mobility, safety, maintenance or incident-access requirements. Forcing them into one fast-moving hull concentrates failure and turns every process modification into a nuclear or marine configuration question. The preferred research reference therefore separates the formation into:

1. a **protected nuclear energy core** that operates only at licensed or pre-characterized utility anchorages;
2. replaceable **water-process modules** outside the nuclear safety boundary;
3. **shore-interface groups** for grid-forming power, cable, water quality and custody transfer;
4. **rapid utility-corridor groups** that install pipe, cable, boosters and crossings from the receiving node toward demand;
5. storage, district distribution, wastewater and residuals formations; and
6. tugs, salvage, repair, security, radiological protection, crew support and depot infrastructure.

This is less cinematic than a universal nuclear rescue carrier and substantially more ambitious. It treats mobile nuclear power as a national infrastructure system with its own receiving geography, regulatory architecture, serial production line, test facilities and fleet-generation model. It also makes the central negative finding visible: **for drinking-water production alone, nuclear energy is usually not the binding technology**. The strategic case rests on long endurance, fuel-independent firm power, simultaneous grid support, heat or cooling services, and repeated use at nodes where shore distribution already exists or can be rapidly established.

The accompanying [N-WES configuration workbook](../../../models/nuclear-water-energy-system.xlsx) is a design-study model, not a reactor design, safety analysis, licensing application, marine classification package, cost estimate or acquisition recommendation. Its reference case is a 300 MWe-gross floating energy core supporting 100 MW of shore power and 100,000 m³/day of potable water. It keeps all consequential safety, siting, reliability and alternatives gates open.

## 1. Mission before mechanism

The mission is not “bring a reactor to a disaster.” It is:

> At a prepared coastal or island node, establish months-to-years of firm electric service and source-to-user safe water after regional fuel, generation or water infrastructure is impaired, without creating an intolerable nuclear, marine, environmental or civil-authority burden.

That mission excludes several tempting uses.

- It is not the first 72-hour response. Distributed stocks, repair teams, batteries, conventional generation, bottled water, local treatment and evacuation usually arrive earlier.
- It is not an arbitrary-port capability. Reactor operation depends on a licensed design and site envelope, security, emergency planning, cooling, mooring, grid behavior, environmental authorization and competent civil interfaces.
- It is not a hospital ship, aviation carrier, bulk sealift ship or port-opening vessel. Those functions create incompatible access cycles, public flows, contamination boundaries and availability demands.
- It is not automatically the preferred long-duration source. Gas turbines, grid restoration, shore reactors, renewable-storage systems, imported electricity, land desalination and transported water remain explicit competitors.

The system earns a role only where three conditions coincide:

1. **duration** is long enough that repeated fuel delivery and emissions become strategically burdensome;
2. **scale** is large enough that persistent firm power has value beyond the desalination train; and
3. a **receiving node** can accept the nuclear, marine, electrical, water and public-safety interfaces.

This produces a different deployment concept from military force projection. The highest-consequence asset does not push to the most damaged edge. It occupies a prepared, defensible utility node. Modular process and distribution forces extend its effect inland.

## 2. The complete public-effect chain

The useful output is not reactor megawatts or membrane nameplate capacity. The chain is:

```text
licensed energy core
  → safe reactor heat removal and electrical conversion
  → fault-limited marine bus
  → desalination and water-quality release
  → product storage and custody transfer
  → trunk pipeline, boosters, pressure zones, crossings and repair
  → district storage and local distribution
  → people and critical facilities receiving safe service

licensed energy core
  → grid-forming conversion and protection
  → export cable and landing
  → isolated critical-load islands or grid bridge
  → protected facilities and thermal services
```

Every arrow can bind. A platform that reaches anchorage but cannot establish a safe moor has zero mission capacity. A plant that makes water into a full clearwell while its shore main is incomplete has zero additional delivered capacity. A cable energized into an unsurveyed or unstable network can worsen the emergency. A reactor that cannot retain qualified crews through the deployment is not a persistent source.

The system therefore reports two service measures:

- **verified megawatt-hours delivered at the authorized shore bus**, by critical-load class; and
- **quality-released cubic-meter-person-days delivered at user interfaces**, not produced at the membrane.

## 3. Architecture alternatives

Six alternatives remain in the design space.

| Architecture | Strategic advantage | Dominant burden | Current disposition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monolithic nuclear utility ship | One hull can arrive with generation and process plant integrated | Common-mode loss; every process change touches ship/reactor configuration; poor access to damaged ports | Do not select as reference |
| Segmented nuclear utility flotilla | Nuclear core stays protected while replaceable process and shore modules move closer to the effect | More marine interfaces, moorings, cables and formation coordination | Preferred research reference |
| Prelicensed shore reactor with floating process plant | Simplifies nuclear stationkeeping and may reuse brownfield grid/cooling infrastructure | Loses geographic mobility; requires a network of capital-intensive sites | Retain as strong comparator |
| Non-nuclear firm-power flotilla | Faster licensing and broader port access; mature turbine technology | Continuous fuel logistics, emissions, fuel-price and supply vulnerability | Required operational comparator |
| Renewable–storage–grid portfolio | Distributed, scalable and valuable in peacetime | Land, construction time, weather correlation and long-duration storage | Required site comparator |
| Water-only mobile works plus grid repair | Often fastest and least complex where power can be restored | Fails when both water and regional firm power remain unavailable for long duration | Default alternative to beat |

No weighted score can settle this before site and mission data exist. The workbook therefore records discriminants rather than manufacturing a single opaque rank: earliest service, months of endurance, fuel tonnes per day, licensed-site dependence, port dependence, replaceable process capacity, common-mode loss, workforce depth, thermal rejection, lifecycle burden and transferable peacetime utilization.

## 4. Preferred reference: a separated strategic utility flotilla

### 4.1 Nuclear Energy Core (`NEC-300`)

The reference core is a slow-self-propelled or towable floating nuclear power facility with 300 MWe gross installed output. This is an **energy core, not a ship specification**. Reactor technology, number of modules, containment, hull form, subdivision, collision basis, ultimate heat sink, spent-fuel strategy and propulsion remain open.

Its first-order operating rules are more important than its silhouette:

- it operates only within an approved site and mooring envelope;
- it can reach a stable shutdown state without shore power, process barges or network control;
- loss of any non-nuclear module cannot challenge reactivity control, heat removal or radionuclide retention;
- public water never crosses the nuclear primary boundary;
- export buses are fault-limited and electrically separable;
- emergency planning, security, marine inspection and civil command are defined before arrival; and
- departure, storm avoidance, safe harbor and disablement recovery are part of the licensed operating concept.

The NRC states that maritime reactors can use existing Part 50, Part 52 and Part 53 pathways and that NRC–Coast Guard coordination now covers civilian floating plants and nuclear propulsion.[^nrc-maritime] That is evidence of a regulatory path, not evidence that a disaster-entry operating concept is licensable. Coast Guard materials separately identify vessel design, marine engineering, electrical engineering, stability, security and pollution regimes for floating facilities.[^uscg-floating]

### 4.2 Water Process Barge (`WPB-100`)

A process barge carries pretreatment, seawater reverse osmosis, remineralization, disinfection, laboratories, product clearwell, clean-in-place systems, spares and concentrate control for 100,000 m³/day. It is electrically and physically separated from the nuclear core. Multiple smaller trains allow maintenance and feed-specific reconfiguration without opening the reactor plant configuration.

The process family should become more ambitious than today's desalination market. Its research targets include:

- autonomous feed characterization and train configuration;
- chlorine-, oil-, sediment-, harmful-algal-bloom- and smoke-ash-tolerant pretreatment;
- membranes with live integrity sensing and rapid cassette exchange;
- low-chemical or recoverable cleaning systems;
- brine-to-resource or controlled dilution modules with verified environmental fate;
- continuous product-quality assurance tied to automatic diversion; and
- container-neutral power, fluid, control and evidence interfaces.

IAEA work establishes the physical precedent for floating nuclear desalination and for coupling nuclear energy to both thermal and reverse-osmosis processes.[^iaea-floating][^iaea-economics] DOE's seawater-desalination analysis provides an energy-method baseline, but complete delivered-water energy also includes feed, pretreatment, post-treatment, pumping, storage and distribution.[^doe-desal]

### 4.3 Shore Interface Group (`SIG-100`)

The shore group owns the dangerous transition between a stable floating plant and a damaged public network. It includes:

- marine cable handling and protected landing;
- grid-forming conversion, transformation, grounding, protection and synchronization;
- black-start and dead-bus procedures;
- water manifolds, custody metering, laboratory release and backflow control;
- fiber, time, identity and configuration links that can operate offline;
- independent emergency isolation on both sides of every transfer; and
- utility, water, environmental and civil-authority liaison teams.

The reference workbook screens 100 MW at both 34.5 kV and 115 kV. At 0.95 power factor, three-phase current is approximately 1,762 A at 34.5 kV and 528 A at 115 kV. That arithmetic does not select a voltage. Fault duty, reactive power, cable length, landing geometry, insulation, thermal burial, protection coordination, degraded network state and converter strategy determine the actual design. DOE offshore-transmission work demonstrates the complete export chain—offshore station, export cable, landing and onshore substation—and the distance-dependent trade between HVAC and HVDC.[^doe-offshore]

### 4.4 Rapid Utility Corridor Group (`RUCG`)

The corridor group carries the mass toward the user. At 100,000 m³/day, average product flow is 1.157 m³/s. Holding a 2 m/s design velocity requires approximately 0.858 m internal diameter before redundancy and reserve. A single illustrative 30 km, 1.0 m OD, DR17 polyethylene trunk contains about 4,957 tonnes of pipe material before joints, fittings, anchors, crossings, spare sections or parallel capacity.

That result rules out the idea of drones “laying the pipeline” if drones means airborne mass transport. Autonomous aircraft can survey, mark, pull pilots, inspect and coordinate. Ground carriers, reels or continuous-extrusion factories, amphibious haulers, cranes, jointing machines and civil crews move and connect thousands of tonnes.

The technology opportunity is still radical:

- self-surveying corridor swarms that map utilities, ground, contamination and access;
- continuous or very-long-section large-bore polymer/composite pipe production near the route;
- robotic joint preparation, fusion, inspection and proof testing;
- active pressure and water-hammer control at distributed booster nodes;
- recoverable surface crossings and self-anchoring floodplain routes;
- embedded leak, strain, temperature and contamination sensing; and
- automated configuration records that allow safe handover to a local utility.

### 4.5 Distribution and sanitation groups

The formation inherits the lessons of `WRG-50`. Product storage, pressure zones, district interfaces, household access, accessibility, sampling, wastewater, concentrate and cleaning residuals are not externalities. At the reference 45% seawater-recovery assumption, 100,000 m³/day of product requires about 222,222 m³/day of feed and produces about 122,222 m³/day of concentrate. EPA notes that desalination discharges can include concentrated seawater, suspended solids, metals and treatment chemicals.[^epa-discharge]

The intake and outfall are therefore site systems. The platform cannot assume that open-ocean dilution, an existing sewer, or emergency authority makes the mass disappear.

## 5. Reference balances

### 5.1 Electrical balance

The workbook uses these illustrative controls:

| Item | Reference |
| --- | ---: |
| Gross electric output | 300 MW |
| Station service | 8% |
| Operating derate | 10% |
| Sustained available bus | 248.4 MW |
| Authorized shore export | 100 MW |
| RO specific energy | 3.5 kWh/m³ |
| Water auxiliaries | 0.8 kWh/m³ |
| Hotel, security and mission loads | 15 MW |
| Operating reserve on served loads | 15% |

RO load is 14.58 MW and water auxiliaries add 3.33 MW. With 100 MW of shore export and 15 MW of platform/mission load, the served-load total is 132.92 MW. A 15% operating reserve raises the requirement to 152.85 MW, leaving a 95.55 MW screening margin against the sustained bus.

This wide margin is strategically revealing. A 300 MWe core is not justified by a 100,000 m³/day RO plant. It is justified, if at all, by the combined need for large shore export, enduring process energy, operating reserve, future thermal services and fuel-logistics independence.

### 5.2 Heat rejection

At an illustrative 33% gross thermal efficiency, 300 MWe implies roughly 909 MWth of reactor heat and about 609 MWth rejected before useful heat recovery and auxiliary adjustments. Removing 609 MW at a 7°C cooling-water rise requires about 20.8 m³/s of seawater in a simple once-through screen.

That cooling flow is approximately eighteen times the product-water flow. Heat rejection, intake protection, marine growth, thermal plume, storm debris and ultimate heat sink are therefore central configuration drivers. The model deliberately does not credit desalination with consuming the reactor's waste heat: reverse osmosis primarily uses electricity, while a thermal or hybrid process would change both water and heat balances and must be modeled separately.

### 5.3 Water and distribution balance

The reference product supports different public effects depending on service level:

- 6.67 million people at 15 L/person-day of survival water;
- 2 million people at 50 L/person-day of expanded emergency service; or
- a smaller population plus hospitals, cooling, sanitation and economic restart.

These are production equivalents, not delivered populations. Leakage, reserve, industrial quality, pressure, storage, point-of-use access and wastewater reduce the realizable service. The formation must publish product, delivered, consumed and returned flows separately.

## 6. Deployment concept

The system operates through five phases.

### Phase 0 — standing preparation

The Department maintains a network of licensed or pre-characterized receiving nodes. Each node has surveyed approaches, mooring loads, exclusion and security geometry, cable and pipe landings, grid studies, water-intake and discharge envelopes, emergency plans, civil agreements, environmental baselines, spare interfaces and periodic full-scale exercises. Peacetime use supplies electricity, water, district thermal service, grid support, training or strategic industrial loads so the fleet does not exist as idle catastrophe inventory.

### Phase 1 — local bridge

Local utilities, `WRG`, `PRG`, stockpiles and repair forces stabilize survival services. Port-opening and survey teams inspect the receiving node. The nuclear core remains at its home or safe operating site until the destination envelope is confirmed.

### Phase 2 — theater opening

Tugs, salvage, security, marine works, cable, process and corridor advance groups establish moorings, navigation control, landing points and isolation zones. Where possible, the process and shore groups begin on local or conventional bridge power before the nuclear core arrives.

### Phase 3 — strategic utility operation

The core enters the approved envelope, connects to the isolated marine bus, and ramps only after independent nuclear, marine, grid, water and civil release authorities concur. Water and power loads are added in blocks. A failed process train or shore feeder must isolate without challenging the reactor safety case.

### Phase 4 — transfer and regeneration

Local or rebuilt systems progressively assume load. The formation conducts decontamination where required, recovers modules, records the as-operated configuration, restores the site, rotates crews, enters depot and replenishes spares. The mission ends only when service is transferred and the receiving node is safe—not when the platform disconnects.

## 7. Fleet and readiness

The reference fleet uses **eight `NEC-300` energy cores**. At an illustrative independent availability of 65%, the expected available inventory is 5.2. More importantly, the simple binomial probability of at least three available cores is about 97.5%. Six cores would provide only about 88.3% probability under the same assumptions.

This is a screening calculation. Nuclear outages, common suppliers, regulatory holds, storms, homeport dependencies and design defects are correlated. The fleet must therefore include:

- at least two independently qualified reactor or balance-of-plant paths where feasible;
- geographically separated home and safe-harbor nodes;
- staggered refueling and major maintenance;
- spare process modules that do not share the nuclear outage cycle;
- independent shore-interface and corridor groups sized above core count;
- a reserve crew and regulator/inspector pipeline; and
- the ability to serve the same receiving node from non-nuclear sources through common interfaces.

The reference formation inventory is eight energy cores, sixteen water-process barges, twelve shore-interface groups, twenty-four rapid utility-corridor groups, sixteen storage/distribution groups, eight marine support groups, four operating bases and two nuclear depot/training complexes. These quantities are force-design hypotheses, not a procurement plan.

## 8. Workforce and institutional design

The deployed formation is not crewed only by reactor operators and mariners. It requires nuclear operations and maintenance, engineering and chemistry, radiological protection, security, deck and propulsion, electrical protection and grid control, water process and laboratory staff, pipe and civil works, environmental monitoring, communications and cyber, logistics, medical support, public liaison and command.

The workbook's preliminary reference assigns 2,440 people to one complete deployed theater formation and applies a 3.2 force-generation multiplier plus 20% training, depot, regulatory and institutional overhead. Eight deployable cores do not imply eight full formations simultaneously; modular groups cycle separately. The mature workforce screen is approximately 47,000 billets across fleet, bases, depots, schools, independent test, licensing support and command. This is an order-of-magnitude occupational planning hypothesis.

The regulator cannot be inside the operating service's chain of command. Reactor safety, marine safety, water release, grid energization and incident authority remain distinct concurrence decisions. A national emergency may change priority and tempo; it does not make the underlying expertise or independent stop authority optional.

## 9. A $400 billion capital program

The workbook places the reference system inside a **$400B, 25-year capital envelope**:

| Cost object | Reference allocation |
| --- | ---: |
| Eight nuclear energy cores | $128B |
| Sixteen water-process barges | $48B |
| Shore-interface groups | $18B |
| Rapid utility-corridor groups | $30B |
| Storage and distribution groups | $12B |
| Marine support groups | $20B |
| Four operating bases and safe-harbor network | $40B |
| Two nuclear depot, training and refueling complexes | $30B |
| R&D, rival prototypes and independent test | $34B |
| Program reserve | $40B |

The unit allocations are deliberately rounded design-study assumptions. They include neither a vendor quote nor an independent estimate. Operations, fuel-cycle services, decommissioning, host-node construction outside the listed base allocation and broader water/power formations must be modeled separately.

The capital case is ambitious enough to create a new industrial sector: serial floating nuclear construction, marine high-integrity electrical systems, modular desalination, large-bore rapid pipe factories, grid-forming converters, nuclear-qualified shipyards, tugs and salvage, digital configuration systems, and a standing licensed workforce. Export value would more often lie in non-nuclear process, interface and corridor modules than in transferring U.S. nuclear cores. Partner operation requires safeguards, liability, security, fuel-cycle, regulatory and sovereign-command agreements that cannot be treated as commercial paperwork.

## 10. Technology campaigns

The program should fund rival pathways against explicit coefficients.

| Campaign | Coefficient to change | Disproof condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Passive maritime heat rejection | Decay-heat removal time without shore support | Unbounded dependence on active seawater systems or vulnerable intakes |
| Collision- and grounding-tolerant nuclear island | Conditional release and safe-shutdown margin | Marine casualty couples into safety functions beyond licensed bounds |
| Standard marine utility bus | Connection time and fault containment | Cross-vendor fault or protection mismatch propagates to the core |
| Extreme-feed separations | Quality-released water per train-day across feed matrix | Product diversion, consumables or residuals erase availability |
| Brine and thermal-plume control | Receiving-water burden per cubic meter delivered | Ecological or permitting burden prevents strategic-node operation |
| Rapid large-bore corridor factory | Tonnes and crew-hours per commissioned m³/s-km | Joints, crossings, proof tests or transport dominate the claimed rate |
| Self-forming shore grid | Hours to safe authorized 100 MW service | Fault duty, grounding, instability or manual fallback cannot close |
| Autonomous inspection and maintenance | Exposure-hours and outage-hours per service unit | Automation creates correlated blind spots or unrecoverable failures |
| Long-life core and modular overhaul | Available service-months per depot-year | Refueling, component replacement or waste path dominates fleet readiness |
| Digital safety and evidence thread | Time to prove exact as-operated configuration | Offline operation or supplier change breaks provenance |

The National Resilience Proving Network requires a dedicated marine nuclear–utility test program, but not a public-scale reactor accident experiment. Elemental and subsystem facilities should replicate mooring loads, cable faults, pump trips, intake blockage, thermal transients, process contamination, collision energy, flooding, fire, cyber isolation and emergency disconnect. Full-system trials must use licensed facilities and surrogate or non-nuclear heat/power sources where they answer the causal question without creating unnecessary radiological risk.

## 11. Failure architecture

The system must be designed to fail into bounded states.

| Failure | Immediate safe state | Mission consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loss of shore grid | Export breaker opens; reactor transitions to stable island load or shutdown | Water may continue on internal bus; shore power pauses pending network proof |
| Water-process contamination | Product diverts; affected train isolates | Power mission continues; water output degrades by train |
| Intake blockage | Process trains derate; reactor uses qualified alternate/ultimate heat sink | Sustained output may fall; core safety cannot depend on public-water intake |
| Export-cable fault | Differential protection isolates both ends | One shore block lost; alternate feeder or local source restores priority loads |
| Pipeline rupture | Section isolates; pressure zones enter reserve service | Delivered water falls despite unaffected production |
| Mooring casualty | Disconnect or safe shutdown according to envelope; tugs respond | Strategic node may be unavailable through the weather window |
| Common software defect | Freeze/rollback; local manual-safe control | Automation benefit is lost without loss of essential control |
| Security event | Layered access control and reactor safe-state authority | Public interface may close even while the physical plant remains available |
| Fleet regulatory hold | Non-nuclear sources use the same shore/process interfaces | Nuclear availability can fall to zero nationally; common architecture limits service loss |
| Qualified-workforce shortfall | Reduce deployed cores rather than stretch watchbills | Inventory exists without readiness credit |

The last two are strategically decisive. A homogeneous nuclear fleet can lose national availability through a single regulatory or technical finding. Non-nuclear interoperability and design diversity are not procurement niceties; they are part of the safety and continuity case.

## 12. Decision and rendering gates

`N-WES` advances from a held `R2` platform idea to a **segmented `R2+` system architecture**. The workbook closes first-order electrical, thermal, water, pipeline, fleet, workforce and capital arithmetic. It does not close reactor technology, containment, hull and stability, mooring, collision, ultimate heat sink, site licensing, emergency planning, security, environmental impact, solved grid behavior, pipeline transients, maintainability, correlated availability, cost or construction schedule.

Before `R3`, the program must:

1. select at least two reference receiving nodes and complete site, hazard, approach, mooring, grid, intake, discharge and public-interface studies;
2. compare the six architectures on common mission cases using independent cost and schedule ranges;
3. complete reactor and marine preliminary safety cases, including loss of mooring, collision, grounding, flooding, fire, storm avoidance, station blackout and ultimate heat sink;
4. solve the 100 MW shore one-line, load blocks, converters, grounding, protection, harmonics, transients and black start;
5. validate feed-specific water recovery, quality, residuals and material compatibility;
6. survey a 30 km corridor and close pressure, surge, joint, anchor, crossing, construction and repair evidence;
7. demonstrate modular isolation so non-nuclear failures cannot challenge reactor safety;
8. build the occupation, licensing, rotation, refueling, depot and decommissioning model;
9. quantify correlated fleet holds and maintain a non-nuclear continuity path; and
10. obtain independent cost, schedule and industrial-base estimates.

No exterior rendering is authorized. The first eventual visual should be an operating-system plate showing the core's exclusion boundary, process barges, mooring, cooling and discharge, cable landing, shore substation, water trunk, pressure zones, storage, district interfaces, support flotilla and failure-isolation points. A beauty view of a nuclear ship would communicate the wrong architecture.

## 13. Strategic conclusion

The most important innovation is not a floating reactor or a larger desalination membrane. It is the ability to **make a temporary regional utility arrive as a certified formation**: at known nodes, with sovereign endurance, replaceable process capacity, safe public interfaces, a corridor-building force, independent evidence and a trained institution that can operate for years.

Nuclear power may become the superior energy core for some enduring coastal and island missions. The first-principles result is narrower and stronger than the original image. The reactor should normally move among prepared nodes, not improvise access to the disaster edge. The modules that touch damaged infrastructure should be more numerous, more replaceable and more mobile than the reactor. The entire formation should accept non-nuclear power through the same interfaces. That architecture preserves the strategic option without making public service depend on nuclear symbolism.
