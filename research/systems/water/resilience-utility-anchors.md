# Resilience Utility Anchors

## Executive judgment

The Nuclear Water–Energy System cannot be evaluated as a fleet in search of ports. Its real unit of design is a **Resilience Utility Anchor (`RUA`)**: a permanently prepared, repeatedly exercised civil utility base where mobile sources can become safe public service.

An anchor is not a berth, an emergency waiver or a set of connection drawings. It is the resilience equivalent of a strategic airbase or naval support complex, expressed through public infrastructure. It combines a hazard-qualified marine envelope, energy and water landing systems, islandable critical-load networks, source and discharge works, corridor rights, storage, laboratories, independent release authorities, security, maintenance, workforce, environmental baselines and a practiced civil compact. The mobile fleet supplies scarce capacity; the anchor makes that capacity usable.

This pass tests two deliberately different design-study archetypes:

- an **estuary regional anchor (`RUA-300`)** serving a large hurricane- and surge-exposed metropolitan utility region; and
- an **oceanic island anchor (`IUA-60`)** serving a smaller, fuel-constrained grid exposed to cyclone, earthquake and tsunami effects.

The comparison overturns another single-platform intuition. The estuary case requires approximately 260.91 MW at the protected bus for 180 MW of critical shore service, 150,000 m³/day of water, internal loads and reserve. One `NEC-300` supplies only 248.4 MW sustained under the present assumptions, so the reference mission requires two cores or a deliberately smaller public effect. The island case requires only 38.62 MW; a 300 MWe core is not a strategic reserve but a grid-integration problem. A notional `NEC-60` supplies 49.68 MW sustained and better fits the mission.

The resulting architecture is therefore a **utility system family**, not an eight-ship class:

| Layer | Regional family | Island family | Common rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Firm energy core | `NEC-300` | `NEC-60` | May be nuclear or non-nuclear behind the same protected bus contract |
| Water process | `WPB-100` and `WPB-50` | `WPB-20` | Process plant remains outside the nuclear safety boundary |
| Shore interface | `SIG-100` blocks | `SIG-25` blocks | Grid-forming, fault-limited and independently releasable |
| Utility corridor | heavy dual-trunk `RUCG-H` | compact high-head `RUCG-I` | Survey, proof, isolation and custody are part of delivery |
| Storage and distribution | regional pressure-zone network | terrain-distributed reservoirs | Product, delivered and consumed water remain separate measures |

No architecture wins both cases by label. A segmented floating nuclear system is a plausible long-duration option at both anchors only after the anchors exist. Prelicensed shore nuclear can dominate it where local hazard survival and restart are credible. Renewable generation, storage and grid repair can dominate both in normal service and may shrink the scarce firm-power requirement. A gas-turbine flotilla can arrive with fewer licensing constraints but consumes about 2,184 metric tonnes of LNG per day in the estuary case and about 323 tonnes per day in the island case under the workbook assumptions. Imported water and diesel remain bridges, not full-service substitutes.

The policy implication is larger than basing a reactor. A Department of Resilience should build a **National Utility Anchor Network** whose interfaces are source-neutral and whose peacetime operation improves the host system every day. Nuclear mobility is then one high-end plug-in to a durable civil architecture. Without the anchors, the fleet is inventory. With anchors but no mobile fleet, the nation still gains hardened microgrids, water security, emergency logistics, industrial capability and a repeatable place to exercise restoration.

The accompanying [anchor alternatives workbook](../../../models/resilience-utility-anchor-alternatives.xlsx) is a transparent design study. It is not a site-selection model, nuclear safety analysis, environmental review, utility plan or cost estimate. Its two locations are archetypes, not recommendations. Its purpose is to expose constraints and reject false comparability before the program chooses places or shapes.

## 1. The node, not the vessel, is the strategic object

Military power projection is often described through mobile platforms, but its usable power depends on bases, depots, access agreements, communications, ranges, fuel, maintenance and rehearsed command. A peer resilience force requires the same systems seriousness. The difference is that its decisive interface is not a weapons employment chain; it is the transfer from a nationally controlled formation into a locally governed public utility.

That transfer is unusually demanding. Electricity must enter a network with known topology, grounding, fault duty, protection and priority loads. Water must pass through sampled, released and metered interfaces into pressure zones that can retain quality. Marine units must remain inside safe navigation, mooring, cooling, weather and security envelopes. Concentrate, heat, cleaning residuals and wastewater need lawful receiving paths. Operators from several institutions must know who can connect, energize, release, curtail, disconnect and stop.

The U.S. Department of Energy defines a microgrid as a controllable group of interconnected loads and distributed energy resources that can operate either connected to or apart from the wider grid.[^doe-microgrids] Federal guidance on islanding emphasizes isolation, black start, staged load blocks and protection against unsafe backfeed.[^doe-islanding] Those are not emergency accessories; they are the electrical core of an anchor. DOE's microgrid report to Congress also identifies ports, water systems, medical facilities and evacuation infrastructure as strategic locations and notes that networked microgrids can share resources and separate when conditions require.[^doe-report]

The same completeness applies to water. EPA's record for the Doheny Ocean Desalination Project treats subsurface intake, raw-water conveyance, the plant, brine disposal, product storage and distribution, and offsite power as one project system.[^epa-doheny] A mobile membrane train cannot delete those surrounding functions. It can change which parts travel and which parts are prepared.

An `RUA` therefore has four simultaneous identities:

1. **a utility base**, with grid, water, thermal, communications and public-service interfaces;
2. **a licensed operating envelope**, with site hazards, marine limits, environmental conditions, emergency planning and independent stop authority;
3. **a logistics and industrial node**, with approach, laydown, repair, storage, spares, berthing, workforce and corridor access; and
4. **a civic compact**, with host priorities, rate and custody rules, environmental monitoring, public information, tribal and territorial sovereignty where applicable, and a transfer plan.

If any identity is missing, the nominal megawatts and cubic meters do not become a public effect.

## 2. What must be prepared before the catastrophe

### 2.1 Site and hazard envelope

The anchor owns a living, version-controlled site basis covering meteorology, flooding, hydrology, geology, seismology, tsunami, sediment, navigation, collision, grounding, fire, sea-level conditions and correlated infrastructure loss. NRC review practice treats external hazards, security and human factors as material parts of advanced-reactor review, while its post-Fukushima hazard work emphasizes site-specific meteorologic, hydrologic, geologic and seismic information.[^nrc-oar][^nrc-poanhi] A generic mobile license cannot make a destination-specific harbor or grid generic.

Heavy weather creates a further contradiction. A strategic utility core is most valuable during regional storms, yet a port may restrict movement as conditions deteriorate. Coast Guard heavy-weather planning requires vessels or barges that remain in port to identify approved shelter or heavy-weather arrangements and progressively restricts port movement under severe conditions.[^uscg-weather] The anchor therefore needs more than a design storm load. It needs a decision timeline: when to enter, when to ride out, when to depart, where to seek safe harbor, what loads can be shed, and what public service continues after disconnection.

### 2.2 Marine and physical works

The prepared marine envelope includes surveyed approach and turning geometry, bathymetry, under-keel margins, collision controls, fender and mooring design, emergency tow points, protected cable and hose corridors, workboat access, debris management, firefighting, pollution response and alternate safe harbor. Mooring and utility connections must fail independently: a cable fault must not create a mooring casualty, and an emergency disconnect must not defeat reactor safe shutdown.

For a nuclear energy core, the ultimate heat sink cannot silently depend on the same exposed intake used by the water plant. The anchor must characterize cooling-water temperature, sediment, debris, marine growth, harmful blooms, ice where relevant, and thermal-plume behavior. It must provide qualified monitoring and alternate or passive safe-state paths. This is why “floating desalination plant” and “floating nuclear power facility” remain separate safety objects even when they share a mission.

### 2.3 Electric landing and critical-load islands

The anchor's electrical system begins with an isolated landing zone and ends with known public loads. It includes marine export cable, disconnects at both ends, grid-forming conversion, transformers, grounding, protection, sectionalizing, communications, black-start auxiliaries, load banks, spares and at least one safe local source. Priority loads are pre-grouped into blocks that can be energized without improvising a damaged network topology.

No anchor should be source-exclusive. `NEC`, gas turbine, storage, renewable, shore reactor and surviving grid sources meet the same bus contract. This source neutrality has three strategic benefits:

- a regulatory or technical hold on a reactor class does not close the anchor;
- conventional sources can exercise the complete public-effect chain before nuclear deployment; and
- host investments retain value even if one national platform program fails.

### 2.4 Water landing, source and residuals

The water interface includes raw-water source rights, screened intake or verified connection, process feed, product clearwell, continuous quality instrumentation, laboratory release, backflow prevention, custody metering, trunk mains, booster nodes, storage, pressure zones and sampling points. Intake and discharge systems must remain inspectable. EPA reviews note that filter backwash and plant processes can increase intake demand above product flow and that dilution, storage and diffuser geometry matter to disposal.[^epa-intake] Marine growth can reduce effective pipeline diameter and therefore hydraulic margin.[^epa-feasibility]

This makes the anchor's apparently passive civil works active readiness assets. A buried pipe that has not been pigged, sampled or pressure-tested does not count. An outfall whose diffuser is obstructed does not count. A clearwell whose isolation boundary is uncertain does not count.

### 2.5 Corridor, storage and last mile

The anchor needs at least one pre-negotiated utility corridor from the landing toward multiple demand areas. It contains rights of entry, crossing designs, staging points, road and bridge limits, geotechnical information, contamination baselines and alternate alignments. Permanent sleeves, pull paths, valve vaults, transformer pads and booster foundations can greatly reduce response time without requiring the full emergency system to sit idle.

Storage separates production from public distribution. It absorbs train trips, pump interruptions and demand variability; creates time for quality release; and permits the national formation to withdraw without an instantaneous service cliff. The required storage architecture differs sharply between an estuary and a steep island, which is one reason the receiving node must shape the mobile system family.

### 2.6 Authority and evidence

The anchor has five distinct release decisions:

1. nuclear or prime-source safe operation;
2. marine entry, mooring and port operation;
3. electrical energization;
4. water-quality release; and
5. civil allocation of scarce service.

No single incident commander should manufacture technical concurrence outside their competence. The Department owns integration and tempo; qualified independent authorities retain stop power. Every exercise must produce configuration evidence, outage histories, samples, protection records, environmental measurements and a public after-action record. Readiness is a demonstrated condition, not a memorandum stating that interfaces are compatible.

## 3. Paired design-reference anchors

The two archetypes are intentionally abstract. They represent different constraint regimes and are not proxies for named cities.

### 3.1 `RUA-300`: estuary regional utility anchor

The regional anchor sits in a large industrial estuary exposed to hurricane wind, surge, river flooding, compound precipitation, sediment and disrupted fuel logistics. It must bridge an urban critical-load island while damaged water and power networks are repaired.

Its design-study public effect is:

| Parameter | Reference assumption |
| --- | ---: |
| Critical shore power | 180 MW |
| Potable-water production | 150,000 m³/day |
| Water/process energy | 26.875 MW |
| Internal mission load | 20 MW |
| Served-load reserve | 15% |
| Required protected bus | 260.906 MW |
| Corridor | 40 km, two parallel trunks |
| Static head | 20 m |

Two `NEC-300` cores provide 496.8 MW sustained under the present 8% station-service and 10% derate assumptions. This leaves large nominal margin after the second unit arrives, but one core is 12.51 MW short of the declared protected-bus requirement. That discontinuity matters: the mission is not “roughly a 300 MW problem.” It is either a two-core problem, a reduced public-effect problem, or a hybrid problem in which storage and local sources cover the gap and contingencies.

At 45% recovery, 150,000 m³/day of product requires approximately 333,333 m³/day of feed and produces 183,333 m³/day of concentrate. Two illustrative 0.9 m OD, DR17 polymer trunks over 40 km contain about 10,707 tonnes of pipe material before fittings, anchors, crossings, spare stock or deployment equipment. Under the stated geometry, first-order steady pumping is 3.14 MW and installed pumping 3.93 MW. The approximately 118 m of modeled friction head is a warning: route, diameter and parallelization are strategic decisions, not downstream civil details.

The regional anchor should therefore be substantially permanent. It needs high-capacity utility landings, industrial laydown, two or more critical-load corridors, regional storage and a marine environment able to separate energy-core safety from process and port casualties. The mobile components are energy scarcity, process surge and corridor repair—not every piece of concrete and pipe.

### 3.2 `IUA-60`: oceanic island utility anchor

The island anchor represents a smaller isolated grid exposed to cyclone, earthquake and tsunami effects, imported-fuel dependence, sea spray, unstable frequency, steep terrain and limited land. DOE uses these factors in its description of American Samoa's energy-resilience challenge.[^doe-samoa] The archetype uses that problem class without selecting American Samoa or any other jurisdiction as a deployment site.

Its design-study public effect is:

| Parameter | Reference assumption |
| --- | ---: |
| Critical shore power | 25 MW |
| Potable-water production | 20,000 m³/day |
| Water/process energy | 3.583 MW |
| Internal mission load | 5 MW |
| Served-load reserve | 15% |
| Required protected bus | 38.621 MW |
| Corridor | 10 km, one trunk |
| Static head | 120 m |

A notional 60 MWe-gross core provides 49.68 MW sustained under the same factors, leaving 11.06 MW screening margin. This is not a reactor selection. It is a requirement discovery: the program needs a smaller energy-core class or a radically different operating concept. Connecting a 300 MWe unit to a small island system could make source dominance, minimum stable load, reserve behavior, fault duty and casualty consequence worse, not better.

At 45% recovery, the water system requires 44,444 m³/day of feed and creates 24,444 m³/day of concentrate. The illustrative 0.56 m OD, DR17, 10 km trunk contains about 518 tonnes of pipe. Because terrain dominates, the model produces 142.55 m total dynamic head and 0.54 MW installed pumping even at the smaller flow. Distributed elevated reservoirs and multiple isolation zones may be more valuable than a larger central plant.

The island anchor should be integrated into ordinary service. Renewable generation, storage, synchronous support, demand flexibility and efficient water systems reduce imported fuel and improve daily grid quality. A 50 MW generation-and-microgrid project in Hawaiʻi demonstrates the scale of firm local resilience investment and its emphasis on black start; its reported fuel storage also illustrates that even robust conventional generation remains tied to finite fuel endurance.[^doe-hawaii] The nuclear option, if proven, should be a long-duration firm layer within that portfolio—not a reason to underbuild the portfolio.

## 4. Constraint screen across six architectures

The workbook compares six architectures without a weighted score. A score would hide vetoes and allow an attractive total to compensate for a failed safety, fuel, access or service constraint. The screen instead asks whether each alternative clears the same mission boundary and records the reason when it does not.

### 4.1 Segmented floating nuclear system

This architecture offers fuel-independent endurance and geographic reuse across prepared anchors. It carries the heaviest licensing, security, marine-safety, workforce and correlated-hold burdens. In the estuary case, two `NEC-300` units or hybrid support are required. In the island case, the 300 MWe class is rejected by scale and a smaller core becomes a separate development program.

Its strategic advantage is not fast incident entry. It is the ability to rotate firm capacity among a known network after initial stabilization while common process and shore modules are already operating. Its disproof conditions include inability to maintain safe shutdown through site hazards, inability to disconnect or depart within the weather decision timeline, and lifecycle utilization too low to sustain its specialized workforce and capital base.

### 4.2 Prelicensed shore nuclear

A shore plant can reuse permanent cooling, security, grid and workforce infrastructure and may avoid marine stationkeeping risk. It also concentrates capital in one hazard geography and loses mobile substitution across regions. Where the plant and its grid island survive and restart, it may dominate the floating system. Where storm, seismic, flooding or cooling hazards disable the site, nominal permanence is a common-mode exposure.

The comparison therefore turns on conditional availability after the same event—not generic capacity factor. A plant that performs excellently in normal years but shares the disaster's failure mode cannot automatically receive resilience credit.

### 4.3 LNG or gas-turbine utility flotilla

The conventional flotilla can use mature turbines, broader ports and a more familiar operating regime. At the modeled protected-bus requirement and 7.5 MMBtu/MWh heat rate, however, it consumes about 2,184 tonnes of LNG per day in the regional case and 323 tonnes per day in the island case. The question is not whether global fuel exists; it is whether the damaged receiving system can repeatedly berth, unload, store, regasify and secure it while also serving other needs.

This option is powerful as a bridge and as a continuity source behind common interfaces. It becomes weak when its logistics chain is the same chain the disaster has broken.

### 4.4 Renewable generation, storage and firm backup

A distributed portfolio creates strong peacetime value, avoids a single mobile source and can reduce the size of every emergency energy core. Its constraints are land, construction time, weather correlation, grid-forming behavior, seasonal energy and the duration of storage required to survive a compound event. On an island, land scarcity can bind; in a regional estuary, transmission and damaged distribution can bind.

This is not the “soft” alternative. At peer scale it implies advanced long-duration storage, corrosion-resistant generation, autonomous sectionalizing, power electronics, forecast-coupled control and modular firm backup. It should be tested as a rival technology campaign, not treated as an ideological baseline.

### 4.5 Water works plus grid repair

Permanent water investments, emergency interties, mobile treatment and an elite grid-repair force may restore service earlier and at lower complexity where the bulk grid or neighboring regions remain available. This is the default alternative for many continental cases. Its weakness appears when generation, fuel, transmission and source water are simultaneously unavailable for months.

The Department should prefer this path whenever it closes the same service-time deficit. A spectacular platform does not earn priority over faster repair.

### 4.6 Imported water and diesel bridge

Packaged water, tankers, diesel generation and temporary distribution are essential survival tools. They cannot economically or physically replace the full regional reference mission for months. The architecture remains in the model because it defines the bridge that every slower system must beat and the residual support that every supposedly complete system still needs.

## 5. From fleet program to National Utility Anchor Network

The network should be organized by **constraint class**, not congressional geography or platform homeport. A national planning hypothesis would contain:

- major estuary and coastal metropolitan anchors able to receive regional energy cores and heavy process/corridor groups;
- island and territorial anchors sized around small-grid stability, imported-fuel exposure, steep terrain and limited land;
- inland waterway or Great Lakes anchors for large freshwater, heat, grid and industrial restoration missions;
- training and proving anchors with non-nuclear surrogate sources that exercise the full interfaces continuously; and
- partner-compatible anchors whose civil interfaces can accept U.S. process, power and corridor modules without transferring nuclear custody.

The program should not set the count before network optimization. Candidate nodes must be screened against population-service deficits, hazard covariance, approach and port survivability, alternate-source access, grid and water topology, workforce, environmental burden, sovereignty, host benefit, travel time and the probability that several anchors are impaired together. A single node may serve several roles, but no “national network” exists if one storm, fault, supplier or regulatory finding closes all of them.

Each anchor should have an ordinary mission. It may provide grid-forming reserve, black-start service, potable-water security, industrial process water, district cooling, research, training, energy storage, port electrification or regional mutual aid. Utilization keeps interfaces live, creates operator judgment, finds corrosion and configuration drift, and gives the host community value before catastrophe.

## 6. Industrial and technological program

Treating the anchor as infrastructure does not reduce technological ambition. It directs invention toward the coefficients that prevent service.

| Technology campaign | Operational coefficient | Required proof |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source-neutral protected utility bus | Time and risk to accept unlike sources | Fault, grounding, harmonic, black-start and disconnect trials across vendors |
| Self-forming critical-load islands | Time from damaged network to authorized service | Topology discovery, protection synthesis, human override and safe rollback |
| Extreme-hazard utility landings | Probability the interface survives when the host network fails | Surge, debris, earthquake, scour, fire, corrosion and replacement trials |
| Rapid large-bore corridor manufacturing | Commissioned m³/s-km per crew-day | Full-route production, joint proof, crossings, pressure transients and repair |
| Autonomous intake/outfall stewardship | Available flow under debris and biofouling | Long-duration feed matrix, inspection, cleaning and environmental evidence |
| Modular grid-forming energy cores | Useful firm power across 25–300 MW receiving systems | Stable minimum load, block pickup, source sharing, casualty and black start |
| Distributed water-quality assurance | Time from treatment to safe release at multiple zones | Continuous sensing, laboratory correlation, diversion and evidence custody |
| Reconfigurable high-head water networks | Energy and failure burden across terrain | Surge control, booster isolation, storage coordination and degraded operation |
| Weather-aware marine disconnect | Time to safe separation or ride-out | Full-scale mooring, cable, hose, tug, shutdown and decision exercises |
| Anchor digital twin and evidence thread | Time to prove exact site and as-operated state | Offline provenance, survey change detection, cyber isolation and audit |

This program creates an industrial base broader than floating nuclear construction: power-electronics firms, modular substations, marine cable and hose systems, utility robotics, pipe factories, intake and discharge technology, high-integrity instrumentation, resilient ports, laboratories, storage, grid controls and specialized civil works. These products have domestic infrastructure and export markets even where the nuclear element is never adopted.

The acquisition object should be an **anchor service package** with verified interfaces, not a collection of unrelated construction contracts. The Department can fund rival source vendors while owning the bus, water, safety, data and evidence contracts that make substitution possible. Independent test must be able to fail the whole mission thread: source arrival, connection, service block pickup, water release, corridor delivery, degraded operation, environmental control and transfer.

## 7. Economics and lifecycle logic

This pass deliberately does not offer a national cost total. A credible total requires candidate geography, construction class, hazard basis, local network state, land and marine rights, source portfolio, environmental mitigation, workforce and utilization. Instead, the workbook keeps capital and 25-year lifecycle ranges by architecture and node so the missing evidence cannot hide behind a precise point estimate.

Several accounting rules follow:

1. **Count the anchor.** Mooring, landings, grid islands, water works, corridors, storage, laboratories, security, bases and exercises are not host-provided free goods.
2. **Count bridge systems.** A nuclear or renewable end state does not erase the first weeks of diesel, batteries, repair, tankers and packaged water.
3. **Count duplicated resilience.** Alternate intakes, routes, buses, safe harbors and spares are the capability, not inefficiency around it.
4. **Count workforce continuity.** Training, licensing, watch rotations, inspectors, maintainers and host operators continue in years without catastrophe.
5. **Credit peacetime service explicitly.** Grid support, water production or port electrification can offset lifecycle burden, but only at verified utilization and transfer prices.
6. **Do not add unrelated envelopes.** The `N-WES` $400B design-study frame and the future anchor-network program overlap in bases, interfaces, R&D and process modules. They require a work breakdown structure before aggregation.

The strategic comparison is cost per **verified service-month under common-mode stress**, not cost per installed megawatt. That denominator forces the alternatives to disclose whether the same event disables fuel, cooling, port access, grid, people or environmental authority.

## 8. Failure architecture

| Failure | Required bounded state | Design consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anchor unavailable after event | Divert to alternate anchor or local-source plan | Network travel time and spare receiving capacity become readiness variables |
| Energy-core class hold | Conventional and renewable sources use the same bus | Common interfaces must be independent of nuclear certification |
| Safe harbor inaccessible | Core reaches safe shutdown outside the threatened port | Entry/departure timing and alternate mooring are licensed operating cases |
| Shore network topology unknown | Energization remains blocked while local islands are proved | Mobile generation does not receive readiness credit by arrival alone |
| Intake/outfall impaired | Process isolates; safe core cooling remains independent | Water and nuclear seawater systems cannot share an unqualified failure path |
| Product trunk ruptures | Section isolates; storage supplies priority zones | Storage, valves and parallel routes determine delivered service |
| Island source dominates grid | Export limits or trips without destabilizing critical loads | Core family and converter design follow minimum-system size |
| LNG receipt interrupted | Load transfers or sheds before fuel exhaustion | Fuel days and berth throughput are explicit mission constraints |
| Host authority disputes allocation | Technical operation continues only within agreed civil priorities | Allocation and custody rules must exist before deployment |
| Environmental limit exceeded | Affected process derates or stops with public reporting | Emergency value does not erase discharge and plume control |
| Cyber/configuration compromise | Local verified configuration and manual-safe control persist | Network disconnection must not destroy essential evidence or control |
| Anchor workforce lost or displaced | Reserve team assumes defined functions; unsafe work stops | Host and national staffing are part of site survivability |

The network itself can fail through concentration. If every regional anchor uses one vendor's converter, one digital control stack, one pipe joint or one nuclear module, a common defect can remove national capacity. The Department must measure substitutability at the interface and maintain diversity where correlated failure dominates the cost of variety.

## 9. Research result and next gates

This pass advances the receiving-node concept from a phrase to an `R2` functional architecture and an `R2+` paired constraint model. It does not select a site or close a platform at `R3`.

The estuary and island cases establish four durable findings:

1. **The anchor is a strategic base.** Port access, utility interfaces, corridors, environmental systems, authority and exercise are part of force projection.
2. **The energy core must be a family.** The regional mission exceeds one `NEC-300` sustained bus, while the island mission makes that core badly oversized.
3. **Source neutrality is a resilience requirement.** Nuclear, conventional, renewable, storage and surviving-grid sources must be able to substitute behind common safety contracts.
4. **The alternatives are site-conditional.** Nuclear endurance, shore-plant survivability, renewable land and duration, gas receipt, grid repair and water sources must be tested against the same event and public-effect clock.

Before any anchor or associated vehicle can reach `R3`, the program must:

1. choose two real candidate sites through an open screening process and publish the rejection logic;
2. complete external-hazard, approach, mooring, safe-harbor, cooling, intake, discharge and environmental baselines;
3. solve regional and island electrical one-lines, load blocks, grounding, fault, stability, harmonic, black-start and source-sharing cases;
4. survey and physically trial representative 40 km dual-trunk and 10 km high-head corridors;
5. validate product quality, intake availability, concentrate and thermal-plume behavior across the site feed matrix;
6. compare all six architectures with independent capital, operating, schedule, outage, fuel, construction and decommissioning evidence;
7. model correlated hazard, supplier, workforce, port and regulatory outages across the proposed network;
8. demonstrate safe source substitution and emergency disconnect at a non-nuclear proving anchor;
9. establish host governance, environmental monitoring, public priorities and transfer rules; and
10. close an industrial and occupational plan for both `NEC-60` and `NEC-300` scale classes before selecting either.

Rendering remains blocked. The first authorized anchor visual should eventually be a systems plate, not a hero view: hazard and exclusion envelopes, safe harbor, energy core, process modules, intake and discharge, protected bus, critical-load islands, water trunks, storage, pressure zones, maintenance, security, public interfaces and every disconnect point must appear together. Until the site and interface balances close, form would be decoration masquerading as design.

## 10. Strategic conclusion

The mature Department of Resilience should project **temporary sovereignty over essential service**, not merely move equipment. A Resilience Utility Anchor is the territorial and technical place where that promise becomes measurable: known hazards, known interfaces, known authorities, practiced people and multiple sources able to create water and power under failure.

This reframes the original nuclear desalination idea without shrinking it. The ambitious program is not one miraculous ship. It is a national network of hardened civil utility bases, a family of mobile energy and process cores, rapid corridor forces, new licensing and evidence institutions, and an industrial ecosystem capable of replacing regional infrastructure as an organized formation. Nuclear technology may provide the unmatched enduring layer at some nodes. The anchor architecture ensures that the public mission survives even if it does not.
