# Force composition: the capabilities, echelons, and people

## Composition thesis

The Resilience Force should be composed as a **national and globally deployable system of complete mission packages**, not a warehouse of equipment and not a collection of hazard agencies. Each package must contain the people, tools, transport interface, command, data, consumables, maintenance, protection, and endurance needed to deliver a measured civilian service effect.

The planning assumption is parity of seriousness with national defense. The force therefore includes mass as well as elite teams: full-time formations in every region; air, land, maritime, infrastructure, health, and community-stabilization services; strategic and tactical mobility; months of rotational depth; a very large reserve; overseas access and pre-positioning; national depots and production; and enough residual capacity to respond while another major operation is underway.

It is entirely plausible that quantitative design will produce a mature establishment of several hundred thousand active members, a reserve of comparable or greater size, hundreds of major aircraft and vessels, tens of thousands of specialized ground systems, and a budget measured in multiple percentage points of federal spending. This pass does not select those numbers by intuition. It makes clear that a boutique force is outside the design space.

Hazards still matter: fire behavior differs from flood hydraulics, an earthquake creates different access problems than a hurricane, and contaminated debris differs from ordinary debris. But hazard categories should shape training and equipment variants **inside** a force organized around recurring effects. Otherwise every new hazard creates another silo while the same dependencies—access, power, water, communications, medical care, distribution, and shelter—are rebuilt repeatedly.

The composition problem has five axes:

1. **effect portfolio**—what civilian service is restored or substituted;
2. **operational function**—what work is performed;
3. **echelon**—how much capacity and command depth is aggregated;
4. **component**—active, reserve, auxiliary, partner, or contracted; and
5. **readiness state**—how soon the complete package can deliver a sustainable effect.

No unit label is meaningful without all five.

## Services generate; joint task forces deliver

The Department of Resilience's six services provide durable professional and industrial homes:

| Generating service | Primary force-generation responsibility | Recurring joint dependencies |
|---|---|---|
| Land Engineering Service | Ground access, heavy construction, flood works, debris, temporary infrastructure, ground fire operations | Air and maritime lift, infrastructure technicians, health protection, community transfer |
| Air Response Service | Lift, aerial fire control, sensing, relay, evacuation, airborne delivery, uncrewed systems | Ground reception, fuel, medical regulation, weather and airspace coordination |
| Maritime Response Service | Sealift, coastal and inland-water access, offshore production, port opening, salvage, floating support | Port and ground distribution, infrastructure connection, medical and community support |
| Infrastructure Restoration Service | Power, water, wastewater, communications, fuel systems, grid and utility restoration | Engineering works, strategic logistics, public health, owner transfer |
| Health Protection Service | Field and definitive care, public health, patient movement, health workforce replacement | Power, water, oxygen, waste, communications, transport, shelter |
| Community Stabilization Service | Shelter, food, essential goods, information, accessible services, temporary civil support, housing transition | All producing services plus local institutions and recovery finance |

No service deploys a self-contained campaign. A coastal megastorm task force, for example, might contain Maritime port-opening and production groups, Land access and debris groups, Infrastructure power-water-communications groups, Health continuity groups, Air lift and sensing groups, and Community shelter and transition groups under one geographic commander.

## The force is built from capability records

Every deployable element should have a published capability record with at least these fields:

| Field | Required statement |
|---|---|
| Effect | The service outcome delivered, expressed in civilian units |
| Capacity | Quantity delivered per hour or day and total sustainable output |
| Time | Alert, movement, setup, initial effect, full effect, and transfer times |
| Conditions | Terrain, weather, contamination, infrastructure, access, and security envelope |
| Inputs | Power, fuel, water source, feedstock, data, labor, spare parts, and host support |
| Outputs | Useful service plus waste, emissions, heat, traffic, noise, and other burdens |
| Footprint | Personnel, weight, volume, vehicles, berths, staging area, and transport class |
| Endurance | Unresupplied endurance and sustainable duration with defined supply chain |
| Interfaces | Mechanical, electrical, hydraulic, data, command, medical, and legal interfaces |
| Reliability | Availability target, mean time to repair, redundancy, and degraded modes |
| Safety | Principal hazards, exclusion zones, licensing, clinical or technical supervision |
| Transfer | Conditions and process for handoff to local, utility, commercial, or recovery owner |

This record is the operational equivalent of a product specification and the accounting unit for readiness. A generator without a qualified operator, switchgear, cable, grounding, fuel plan, maintenance, and a validated load is not a power capability. A water-treatment plant without an intake, pretreatment, quality laboratory, storage, distribution, wastewater plan, and licensed operation is not a water capability.

## Force-building blocks

### Capability element

The smallest independently accountable technical output. An element may be a survey team, a pumping set with crew, a surgical team, a communications node, or a debris-processing line. It need not be independently deployable or sustainable.

### Mission module

A capability element plus the enabling functions required to produce an effect at a stated rate. A mission module has command, communications, basic maintenance, safety, data reporting, and an explicit resupply interface. This is the smallest unit that should appear in a national readiness inventory.

### Response task group

Several mission modules combined under a deployable headquarters and sustainment unit for a specific consequence chain. Examples include an isolated-community stabilization group, a hospital-continuity group, or a flooded-urban-access group. The name describes the civilian outcome, not the equipment.

### Regional response force

A standing command with multiple task groups, organic reception and logistics, planning depth, and enough capacity to support several incidents or one major regional incident. It trains with states, tribes, territories, utilities, health systems, ports, and mutual-aid networks in its area.

### National response force

An assembled formation for a catastrophic or multi-region mission. It includes strategic transport, national logistics, scarce technical capabilities, reserve mobilization, replacement crews, and a national-level headquarters. It is task-organized rather than permanently concentrated.

### Global stabilization group

A self-contained, internationally certified formation designed for host-nation invitation, austere entry, independent sustainment, and deliberate transfer. International employability is a separate certification; domestic readiness does not automatically confer it.

## Nine service-effect portfolios

The portfolios are enduring communities of doctrine, training, acquisition, and professional practice. They are not isolated chains of command. An incident task group draws modules from several portfolios.

## 1. Civil coordination, safety, and public information

**Purpose:** establish legitimate, safe, intelligible operations when institutions, data, and communications are degraded.

Required module families include:

- integration and effects teams that translate civil objectives into executable service packages;
- deployable incident-support and planning cells;
- public warning, multilingual risk communication, rumor control, and accessible communications teams;
- responder safety, industrial hygiene, occupational health, and environmental monitoring;
- geospatial survey, damage assessment, infrastructure reconnaissance, and remote-sensing exploitation;
- civil-rights, disability-integration, tribal-affairs, legal, and community-liaison teams;
- fatality-management coordination and dignified family-assistance support; and
- accountability, credentialing, resource tracking, and public-cost reporting.

This portfolio deploys first and remains through transfer. It does not replace local incident command. It gives the operating force enough contextual understanding to avoid technically efficient but socially or legally destructive action.

## 2. Food, shelter, and essential-goods continuity

**Purpose:** keep displaced or service-isolated populations alive, safe, and connected to basic goods without creating unmanaged camps or brittle commodity lines.

Required module families include:

- bulk and special-diet feeding systems;
- cold-chain and medication-support logistics;
- accessible congregate shelter and non-congregate lodging support;
- emergency sanitation, laundry, bathing, and solid-waste services;
- infant, elder, disability, and companion-animal support modules;
- commodity reception, break-bulk, packaging, and last-mile distribution;
- household essentials and emergency cash/digital-access support; and
- shelter-to-housing transition teams.

The design standard is not beds or meals shipped. It is **people safely sustained per day**, disaggregated by access and functional need, with a traceable path out of emergency shelter.

## 3. Health and medical continuity

**Purpose:** prevent avoidable mortality by maintaining the care pathway from field triage through definitive care, chronic-care continuity, public health, and patient return.

Required module families include:

- prehospital triage, stabilization, and ambulance coordination;
- emergency, surgical, critical-care, burn, pediatric, obstetric, behavioral-health, dialysis, and chronic-care modules;
- hospital decompression and facility continuity teams;
- patient regulation, tracking, ground and air movement, reception, and return;
- pharmacy, blood, oxygen, laboratory, imaging, sterilization, and medical-waste support;
- epidemiology, infection prevention, vaccination, vector control, and environmental health;
- mortuary surge and family-assistance support; and
- medical logistics and clinical staff replacement pools.

HHS's all-hazards plan describes NDMS patient movement as a linked system of evacuation, medical regulation, en-route care, tracking, and return, with definitive care provided through a network of roughly 1,800–2,000 participating civilian hospitals.[^hhs-ahp] That architecture is sound. The new Force must add an assured capacity floor for situations in which receiving hospitals, their utilities, and their workforces are themselves impaired.

Medical modules must publish **licensed and staffed bed-equivalents by acuity**, not nominal cots. Their inputs must include oxygen, power quality, water, sterile processing, pharmaceuticals, blood, diagnostics, waste, cybersecurity, and replacement clinicians.

## 4. Energy and thermal continuity

**Purpose:** restore usable electrical and thermal service to critical loads, not merely deliver generators.

Required module families include:

- critical-load assessment and electrical safety;
- rapid generation across household, facility, campus, and district scales;
- mobile storage, power conditioning, switchgear, protection, and microgrid control;
- temporary distribution and service-drop construction;
- transformer, substation, and grid-component replacement support;
- fuel reception, quality control, storage, and distribution;
- district heating or cooling, mobile cooling centers, and building thermal stabilization;
- renewable and hybrid systems where they reduce fuel dependency; and
- utility restoration liaison, testing, synchronization, and transfer.

USACE's temporary-power mission includes assessing critical facilities and hauling, installing, operating, and maintaining generators for validated requirements.[^usace-power] That complete verb chain should be the minimum definition of power capability. The mature Force extends it upstream to fuel and grid components and downstream to safe building loads and utility transfer.

The principal metric is **critical-load megawatts continuously served at required power quality**, accompanied by population or service outcomes—not aggregate generator nameplate capacity.

## 5. Communications, computation, and public-service access

**Purpose:** recreate the minimum information environment required for rescue, command, family contact, commerce, benefits, health care, and infrastructure restoration.

Required module families include:

- interoperable responder voice and data;
- emergency 911 and dispatch continuity;
- rapidly deployable cellular, satellite, microwave, mesh, and high-frequency systems;
- local internet and public charging access;
- edge computing, identity, cybersecurity, and data-exchange services;
- broadcast, alert, and accessible public-information distribution;
- infrastructure telemetry and remote-control restoration; and
- repair support for fiber, towers, cable landings, and critical network facilities.

Communications modules should be evaluated by **priority users and geographic area served at defined availability, latency, and throughput**, not by radios or terminals issued. They require energy modules by design and must operate in degraded spectrum, cyber, and backhaul conditions.

## 6. Mobility, access, and distribution

**Purpose:** open and sustain paths for people, responders, goods, utilities, and waste across damaged terrain and infrastructure.

Required module families include:

- route reconnaissance, hydrographic survey, airfield and port assessment;
- emergency road and channel clearance;
- bridging, causeways, ramps, ferries, and temporary landing facilities;
- high-water, shallow-water, off-road, snow, and debris-field mobility;
- airfield, heliport, railhead, port, and beach reception;
- cargo handling, cross-docking, container management, and material-handling equipment;
- evacuation and accessible mass transport;
- last-mile delivery by crewed and uncrewed systems; and
- movement-control centers that allocate scarce routes, slots, and lift.

The unit of effect is **sustainable throughput from origin to point of use**. Tons arriving at a regional airport do not count if local roads, unloading equipment, storage, manifests, recipients, or return flows are absent.

## 7. Water, sanitation, and hygiene

**Purpose:** provide safe water and sanitation across the entire chain from source to user and from waste generation to safe discharge or disposal.

Required module families include:

- source assessment, intake, raw-water transfer, and well support;
- desalination and treatment for saline, brackish, fresh, polluted, and biologically compromised sources;
- water-quality laboratories and continuous monitoring;
- bulk storage, pressure management, pumping, and temporary distribution;
- household and facility dispensing;
- wastewater collection, lift, treatment, and bypass;
- latrines, bathing, hygiene, and health-promotion support;
- sludge, concentrate, and residuals management; and
- repair teams for treatment plants, pumps, controls, and distribution networks.

The metric is **liters of compliant water delivered per person or critical user per day at the point of use**, plus wastewater safely managed. Plant-gate production is an intermediate measure.

## 8. Hazard and environmental control

**Purpose:** reduce the active physical process that continues to threaten people, infrastructure, ecosystems, or response operations.

Required module families include:

- structural and wildland fire detection, initial attack, extended attack, and protective-line construction;
- high-volume flood pumping, temporary barriers, drainage restoration, and controlled dewatering;
- slope, avalanche, ice, dam, levee, and unstable-structure monitoring and intervention;
- hazardous-material detection, containment, decontamination, and waste handling;
- oil and chemical spill control on land and water;
- smoke, ash, dust, and air-quality mitigation;
- vector, carcass, contaminated-soil, and environmental-health control; and
- precision forecasting and decision support for changing hazard behavior.

The UK's national-resilience fire program provides a useful capability taxonomy—high-volume pumps, urban search and rescue, enhanced logistics, flood rescue, and CBRN detection and decontamination—hosted locally but standardized and deployable nationally.[^uk-resilience] The proposed Force applies that modular logic at much greater scale and with full sustainment.

## 9. Housing, public works, and economic continuity

**Purpose:** cross the gap between emergency survival and a functioning community.

Required module families include:

- rapid structural and habitability assessment;
- emergency weatherproofing and safe temporary repair;
- debris characterization, clearance, separation, processing, recycling, and disposal;
- temporary public facilities, schools, clinics, government offices, and commercial-service nodes;
- modular interim housing and site infrastructure;
- water, sewer, power, and communications repair support;
- local-contractor integration, permitting, inspection, and technical assistance;
- business-reopening logistics and market access; and
- recovery transfer, documentation, and demobilization.

USACE notes that debris work ranges from emergency clearance to removal, demolition, contaminated debris, waterways, and disposal, and that disposal sites are central to mission scope.[^usace-debris] This illustrates why an earthmover fleet is not a debris capability. Characterization, routing, contracting, environmental controls, reduction, disposal, records, and community consent determine whether material movement produces recovery or another hazard.

## Cross-cutting enabling commands

The service-effect portfolios cannot deploy themselves. Five enabling systems should be designed as first-class operational capabilities.

### Strategic mobility and reception

This command controls common-user air, sea, rail, and road movement; movement priorities; load planning; hazardous-material documentation; reception hubs; and onward movement. It does not need to own every carrier, but it must own enough assured lift and handling capacity to establish a credible deployment floor.

### Sustainment and maintenance

This command provides supply, maintenance, calibration, fuel, food, responder shelter, medical support, sanitation, waste, and replacement crews. Every operational module must include an initial support slice; the command provides depth and theater-level distribution.

### Engineering standards and technical authority

Chief engineers, medical directors, aviation and maritime authorities, environmental officers, and data-security authorities control professional standards and high-consequence release decisions. Incident urgency does not eliminate the need for licensed or certified judgment.

### Training, exercises, and lessons

The Force needs schools and field centers for common doctrine, technical qualifications, command, and joint exercises. Lessons must link incident observations to requirement changes, acquisition priorities, and revised training—not end as unassigned after-action findings.

### Personnel replacement and family support

Long-duration response fails if the institution treats the first crew as the mission. A replacement system tracks qualifications, exposure, fatigue, travel, family support, mental health, and return-to-readiness. It is a force component, not an administrative afterthought.

## Components of the force

### Active component

The active component provides capabilities whose response time, technical complexity, maintenance burden, or continuous public mission requires full-time teams. It should dominate:

- national and regional command;
- forecasting and readiness management;
- strategic logistics and depot maintenance;
- aviation, maritime, nuclear, medical, and high-hazard technical authority;
- rapidly deployable integration teams;
- scarce engineering and infrastructure restoration;
- instructor, evaluator, acquisition, and test cadres; and
- the first rotations of high-readiness task groups.

### Federal reserve component

The reserve provides trained units and individuals for surge, endurance, and specialized civilian skills. It must be organized around complete units where collective performance matters and individual augmentation only where it does not.

Reserve design requires:

- protected civilian employment and credential portability;
- predictable training and activation rules;
- assigned equipment or guaranteed access to a maintained cache;
- mobilization centers and transport plans;
- recurring collective exercises;
- medical and physical readiness appropriate to role;
- promotion and qualification paths equal in seriousness to the active component; and
- family, income, and employer support during long activations.

The German THW shows the scale that a standardized volunteer technical system can achieve: hundreds of local sections, nationally consistent training and equipment, thousands of vehicles, and specialist modules that can combine across regions.[^thw-overview] The lesson is standardization and local presence, not that volunteers can replace the full-time core required for this much larger readiness promise.

### State and territorial component

States and territories could sponsor federally funded, nationally typed units available first to their governors and second to the national system under compact or federal activation. This resembles the useful dual character of the National Guard without making the force military.

The component would provide persistent local knowledge, distribute capability geographically, and give governors a meaningful stake in force design. National funding would be conditional on common readiness, inspection, data, and interoperability standards.

### Tribal resilience component

Tribal nations should be eligible to host and command typed units through direct federal compacts. These units would combine general capabilities with expertise in local infrastructure, geography, languages, governance, and cultural resources. They must not be subordinated administratively to states.

### Civil auxiliary

An auxiliary would enroll individuals and organizations that can contribute defined skills, facilities, data, or equipment without becoming deployable reserve units. Examples include amateur radio, mapping, veterinary support, translators, disability organizations, community health workers, mariners, pilots, heavy-equipment operators, and technical societies.

Auxiliary status should never be used to relabel spontaneous volunteers as ready capacity. Each contribution requires credentialing, supervision, liability, data protection, and a supported place in the incident organization.

### Contracted and industrial capacity

Contracts are essential for scale, especially transport, construction, lodging, commodities, maintenance, and production surge. But a contract is not readiness unless the government has verified:

- asset and workforce availability under concurrent demand;
- priority relative to commercial customers;
- mobilization and delivery times;
- fuel, material, and subcontractor dependencies;
- interoperability and data requirements;
- performance under damaged infrastructure;
- price and liability terms; and
- exercise participation.

The Force should maintain a **certified industrial reserve** analogous to a unit readiness system. Vendors would report complete deployable packages, not catalog quantities.

## Workforce professions

The Force needs a professional identity broad enough to include operators, caregivers, engineers, logisticians, scientists, and community specialists without flattening their standards. Career fields should include:

- incident leadership and civil integration;
- logistics, contracting, movement control, and supply-chain engineering;
- civil, electrical, mechanical, environmental, coastal, geotechnical, and systems engineering;
- utilities and skilled trades;
- fire, rescue, hazardous materials, and environmental control;
- medicine, nursing, pharmacy, behavioral health, public health, veterinary medicine, and mortuary affairs;
- aviation, maritime, rail, and ground operations;
- communications, computing, cybersecurity, data, geospatial, and forecasting;
- shelter, mass care, disability integration, social services, housing, and economic recovery;
- acquisition, test, manufacturing, cost analysis, and quality assurance; and
- safety, law, civil rights, public information, tribal affairs, and international coordination.

### Three qualification layers

Every member should possess:

1. a **service qualification**—common doctrine, safety, ethics, incident systems, field living, and deployment;
2. a **professional qualification**—license, trade, technical, command, or analytical competence; and
3. a **position qualification**—demonstrated ability to perform a specific job in a typed module.

GAO has documented recurrent FEMA challenges in training access, on-the-job development, mentoring, evaluation, and skill availability.[^gao-fema-training] The proposed Force therefore cannot treat an employee count as a workforce measure. It must report qualified positions by module and readiness state.

### Workforce design principles

- **Crews, not résumés:** maintain collective proficiency for work that must be performed as a team.
- **Licensure survives deployment:** compact or federal rules must preserve professional scope across states while maintaining accountability.
- **No invisible care labor:** workforce plans include responder lodging, food, health, family support, and recovery.
- **Exposure is cumulative:** heat, smoke, trauma, pathogens, noise, radiation, and fatigue are tracked across a career.
- **Experience must regenerate:** active incidents cannot be the only path to qualification; simulation and large exercises must generate reps safely.
- **Civilian expertise is a strength:** promotion should not force a master electrician, ICU nurse, hydrologist, or heavy-equipment operator into generic management to advance.

## Unit design rules

### Rule 1: design for the broken environment

Modules must assume that the same event may remove commercial power, fuel, communications, lodging, potable water, repair shops, and local labor. Self-sufficiency is not binary; every module states what it can do at 0, 24, 72, and 168 hours without external support.

### Rule 2: separate entry mass from sustaining mass

The equipment needed to begin work is usually much smaller than the mass required to sustain it. Plans must identify both. A six-hour advance package that creates a six-day logistics debt is not rapid response.

### Rule 3: modularity has a transaction cost

Modularity permits tailoring and distributed procurement, but every interface adds planning, compatibility, testing, and command burden. Modules should split where demand varies independently or transport requires it—not merely to create a larger catalog.

### Rule 4: every producer has a distribution tail

Generation, treatment, or bulk delivery creates no civilian effect without connection and last-mile distribution. Power modules include electrical distribution; water modules include storage and delivery; medical modules include patient flow; communications modules include user access.

### Rule 5: every mission has a waste stream

Water treatment produces concentrate and sludge. Medical care produces infectious waste. shelters produce sewage and solid waste. Fire suppression produces contaminated runoff. Debris operations produce dust and disposal burdens. Waste handling belongs in the requirement record.

### Rule 6: units must combine without redesign

Power, water, medical, communications, and logistics modules should use controlled interface standards, common data definitions, interchangeable connectors where safe, and published load envelopes. Standardization should focus on interfaces, not force every mission into identical equipment.

### Rule 7: transfer begins at design

Temporary systems should be compatible with responsible owners' infrastructure, licensing, data, and maintenance where possible. The Force measures success when the emergency system can be removed without another service collapse.

## Composition decisions deferred to quantitative design

This pass does not set final headcount, base locations, fleet quantities, or platform designs. Those require the concurrency and network models in later passes. It does establish what those models must size:

- complete mission modules rather than assets;
- qualified crews and replacement rotations rather than billets;
- end-to-end delivered effect rather than production at the platform;
- simultaneous domestic incidents plus a residual national reserve;
- territorial and remote access as distinct deployment cases;
- maintenance, training, and depot pipelines;
- activation and employer constraints for reserves;
- industrial production and repair surge; and
- transfer conditions and demobilization capacity.

The next document defines the readiness and posture system that turns this composition into a credible national promise.
