# National Utility Projection System

## Executive judgment

If the Department of Resilience is to project water and power with the seriousness that the Department of Defense projects force, it needs more than a fleet and more than a collection of hardened ports. It needs a **National Utility Projection System (`NUPS`)**: a unified command, a continental and territorial network of prepared utility-anchor complexes, permanently assigned formations, source-neutral interfaces, strategic mobility, deep maintenance, and an industrial base sized against a declared concurrency standard.

The system's promise is direct:

> Within a damaged region, establish an authorized island of essential electric and water service at strategic scale; extend it through temporary or repaired networks; sustain it through rotation and industrial replenishment; and transfer it without a second collapse.

This pass converts that promise into a mature-force hypothesis. It proposes:

- a **Utility Projection Command (`UPCOM`)** as a unified functional command inside the Department of Resilience;
- twenty-six **Resilience Utility Anchor complexes** across four classes, each comprising more than a waterfront point;
- a source family containing large and small nuclear energy cores, non-nuclear firm-energy units, water-process modules, shore-interface groups, corridor formations, storage/distribution groups and marine support;
- a utility-specific interpretation of the Department's `3C + 5M + 2G + 20R` concurrency standard;
- approximately 198,000 mature billets in the utility projection enterprise under the workbook assumptions; and
- a **$1.698 trillion, 25-year capital program** before recurring operations, of which $344B constructs anchor complexes, $571B buys the mobile force, $500B builds production, depots, research, training, common systems and strategic spares, and $283B remains explicit program reserve.

The capital total is deliberately larger than the preceding $400B Nuclear Water–Energy System frame. Pass 20 asked what one strategic system might contain. Pass 22 asks what a national force capable of simultaneous regional, territorial and international utility campaigns would require. The new figure is still a design-study hypothesis, not an estimate, but the scale change is real: eight `NEC-300` cores can provide a 97.47% independent screen probability of at least three available; they provide only a 16.91% probability of at least seven. The utility concurrency case therefore points to fifteen `NEC-300` and twelve `NEC-60` cores if each has 65% independent availability and the planning target is at least 95% probability of meeting simultaneous demand.

That result is not a recommendation to order twenty-seven reactors. It is a warning against designing a national institution around a dramatic demonstration fleet. The program must keep non-nuclear firm sources behind the same interfaces, fund rival pathways, model correlated holds, and allow nuclear to lose where a shore plant, renewable-storage network, repaired grid or conventional utility flotilla closes the same public-effect deficit more safely or cheaply.

The accompanying [utility projection workbook](../../models/national-utility-projection-system.xlsx) makes the network, concurrency, reliability, workforce, capital and recurring-cost assumptions editable. The twenty-six geographic areas are **candidate screening zones**, not selected ports, parcels, licensed sites or funding recommendations. No rendering is authorized.

## 1. Why a unified utility projection system

Water and electricity are not simply two capability portfolios. They are upstream conditions for hospitals, cooling, communications, shelter, food, wastewater, fuel handling, industrial restart and legitimate civil administration. Their restoration requires several operating services at once:

- the Infrastructure Restoration Service generates electrical and water-process units;
- the Maritime Response Service generates energy-core, process-barge, port-opening, tow, salvage and marine-safety units;
- the Land Engineering Service generates corridor, crossing, heavy-work and distribution formations;
- the Air Response Service moves urgent people, controls, laboratories, spares and high-value modules;
- the Health Protection Service defines and operates protected clinical loads and water-quality consequences; and
- the Community Stabilization Service connects technical output to accessible household and public service.

Leaving integration to an ad hoc campaign headquarters would recreate the existing coordination problem at much larger scale. The Department therefore needs one command that continuously plans the end-to-end utility mission, controls the anchor network, certifies source-neutral interfaces, allocates nationally scarce modules, and exercises complete service threads.

The institutional separation is important. `UPCOM` is not a national super-utility and does not displace the civil owner. It is a force-employment and readiness command. Host utilities, governments, tribal and territorial authorities remain the civil principals for priorities, ordinary operation and transfer. Independent nuclear, marine, grid, water-quality and environmental authorities retain technical stop power. The command integrates capacity; it does not erase professional or democratic authority.

DOE's microgrid program describes standardized, adaptive control, automation and protection; networked microgrids; advanced power-electronics interfaces; and port electrification as parts of a future grid architecture.[^doe-microgrid-strategy] `NUPS` takes that logic to the level of national force design. It links grid-forming service to bulk water, marine access, corridors, workforce, strategic reserve and public authority.

## 2. Utility Projection Command

### Command position

`UPCOM` should be a unified functional command reporting through the Chief of the Resilience Staff. It receives formations from the six operating services and assigns them to domestic geographic commands or Global Resilience Command. In routine conditions, it operates the anchor network and controls strategic readiness. During a campaign, the affected geographic commander normally becomes the supported commander for civilian outcomes, while `UPCOM` remains the supporting command for scarce utility forces, network rerouting and national continuity.

When a failure spans several geographic commands—such as a multiregional grid event, fuel-system collapse or simultaneous drought and heat emergency—the Secretary may designate `UPCOM` as supported for the national utility campaign. Civil priority remains with the Civil Resilience Authority and affected governments; operational unity does not confer power to set social allocation without them.

### Permanent subordinate commands

The mature headquarters should contain five permanent operational systems rather than a large coordinating staff.

**Anchor Network Command** owns the federal portion of every utility-anchor complex, its paired dry or inland counterpart, site basis, common landings, exercises, local agreements and alternate-source plan. It maintains the network as operating infrastructure rather than emergency real estate.

**Energy and Water Projection Command** controls the national pools of `NEC-300`, `NEC-60`, hybrid firm-energy units, water-process barges, shore-interface groups and specialist utility crews. It also maintains the source-neutral bus and fluid-interface configuration authority.

**Utility Corridor Command** controls rapid pipeline, cable, substation, pumping, storage, network-repair, crossing and heavy-construction formations. Its performance measure is commissioned service-distance through a real route, not tonnes of equipment dispatched.

**Utility Assurance Center** maintains national models of critical-load islands, water systems, hazard effects, source availability, fuel, environmental conditions and exact as-operated configuration. It hosts independent grid, water-quality and environmental release cells but cannot overrule their stop decisions.

**Utility Sustainment and Industrial Command** owns depots, nuclear and non-nuclear energy-core maintenance, membrane and power-electronics overhaul, large-bore pipe production, transformer and cable reserves, configuration-controlled spares, and emergency production authorities.

The National Resilience Proving Network remains institutionally separate enough to evaluate `UPCOM` claims. A command that owns a readiness promise should not be the sole judge of whether it has met it.

## 3. The anchor is a complex, not a dot

Pass 21 defined the Resilience Utility Anchor as a strategic base. At network scale, each anchor becomes a **four-part complex**:

```text
wet or source node
    ↕ protected energy, water and data landings
hazard-dissimilar back node
    ↕ two or more pre-engineered utility corridors
critical-load cells and regional storage
    ↕ temporary districts, repaired networks and public distribution
national fleet, depots and alternate anchors
```

The **wet or source node** receives marine energy and process units, or connects other bulk sources. It owns approach, mooring, cooling, intake, discharge, cable and hose systems.

The **back node** is physically separated from the waterfront hazard and contains grid-forming substations, control, laboratories, storage, spares, crew support and the inland end of protected landings. A single surge, port fire or collision should not remove both.

The **service cells** are pre-modeled islands of hospitals, water and wastewater works, communications, cooling sites, fuel terminals, shelters and other civil priorities. They can separate from a damaged wider grid and accept staged blocks of service.

The **corridors** connect these parts through surveyed, rights-cleared routes with known crossings, foundations, sleeves, valve locations, cable paths, alternate alignments and repair plans. A mobile source does not receive readiness credit for reaching the wet node if the corridor cannot deliver its effect.

This architecture explains why the system is expensive. It buys deliberate redundancy and interfaces where civilian infrastructure normally optimizes them away. That redundancy is not an overhead around the capability. It is the mechanism that makes service survive a correlated event.

## 4. Network design obligations

The national network should be selected through constraints before optimization. A low-cost portfolio that leaves a territory, coast, major population-service basin or inland logistics system outside the required time band is infeasible. A high-scoring node whose wet and back sites share one floodplain or fault system is infeasible. No weighted national score should compensate for those failures.

The initial obligations are:

1. **continental coverage:** at least three Atlantic, three Gulf and three Pacific regional complexes, with no coast dependent on one approach, shipyard, control system or hazard family;
2. **territorial assurance:** resident anchor and bridge capability for Puerto Rico, the U.S. Virgin Islands, Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands and American Samoa, plus remote-state coverage for Hawaiʻi and Alaska;
3. **inland reach:** at least six Great Lakes or inland-waterway complexes able to receive, fabricate and route major utility systems without coastal port access;
4. **paired survivability:** every wet node has a separated back node and alternate reception plan;
5. **source diversity:** every operational anchor can accept at least two unlike firm-source families and operate critical islands without the national mobile source;
6. **time bands:** rapid modules can begin a bounded bridge inside 72 hours where prepositioned; mass utility service is planned in days to weeks and enduring service in weeks to years;
7. **strategic reserve:** national allocation preserves at least 20% immediately employable reserve in each scarce effect after declared campaigns;
8. **evidence capacity:** at least two integration complexes can test complete utility missions without consuming operational-node availability; and
9. **covariance control:** no single hurricane, earthquake, tsunami, river system, supplier, software release, regulator action or fuel route is allowed to remove the national promise.

FEMA's National Risk Index provides a common national hazard taxonomy and modeled relative-risk framework, but it does not select an engineering site or describe post-event utility access by itself.[^fema-nri] USGS's 50-state seismic model similarly supplies probabilistic ground-shaking science used in public policy and engineering, including basin and subduction effects, not a yes/no siting answer.[^usgs-nshm] The network model must retain those source definitions rather than compressing all hazards into one generic risk score.

## 5. A 26-zone planning portfolio

The workbook instantiates twenty-six screening zones. They are geographic obligations used to test force scale, not chosen facilities.

| Class | Count | Mission | Candidate-zone families |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| Regional coastal `RUA-300` complex | 10 | Two-core or hybrid regional utility campaigns; heavy water and corridor throughput | Northeast gateway, Mid-Atlantic, South Atlantic, Florida peninsula, central and western Gulf, Southern California, California Bay–Delta, Columbia–Willamette, Puget Sound |
| Island and remote `IUA-60` complex | 8 | Small-grid firm power, water, terrain distribution and resident bridge capability | Two Puerto Rico sectors, U.S. Virgin Islands, Hawaiʻi, Guam, Northern Marianas, American Samoa, Southcentral Alaska |
| Inland `LUA-150` complex | 6 | Great Lakes/river reception, freshwater processing, inland grid bridge, fabrication and cross-coast substitution | Chicago–Calumet, Detroit–Toledo, Cleveland–Erie, Buffalo–Niagara, mid-Mississippi, lower-Ohio confluence |
| National utility integration complex | 2 | Full-scale non-nuclear surrogate testing, independent evaluation, fleet certification and alternate command | One Atlantic–Gulf system and one Great Lakes–inland system, exact locations open |

The coastal zones correspond to nationally significant population and freight systems, but commercial throughput is only one input. The Bureau of Transportation Statistics reports that U.S. ports accounted for 41% of U.S. imports and exports by value in 2024, more than $2.1T, and that waterborne transportation and support employed 164,900 people.[^bts-ports] USACE collects vessel, commodity, origin and destination data for ports, waterways and canals, including U.S. territories.[^usace-wcsc] These sources establish why ports matter and provide a data spine for screening. They do not establish that a busy terminal is a safe nuclear or utility anchor.

The inland class is not secondary. USACE's inland-waterway reporting describes roughly 465 million tonnes and more than $158B moving on the system annually and notes the age of lock and dam infrastructure.[^usace-inland] Great Lakes and river complexes create freshwater access, heavy-cargo movement, manufacturing depth and alternatives when coastal approaches fail. They also prevent the anchor concept from becoming synonymous with a nuclear ship.

Territorial assurance cannot be an afterthought to continental optimization. Census maintains separate population-estimate and projection methods for Puerto Rico and the U.S. island areas because their data systems and release cycles differ.[^census-islands] The workbook therefore assigns a coverage obligation rather than allowing small population alone to eliminate a remote node. Two Puerto Rico sectors are included because one island-wide facility would reproduce the common-mode problem. DOE's PR100 work also illustrates that restoring and transforming an island grid is a system-scale problem: its summary identifies 300–800 MW of grid-forming battery storage as important for large frequency deviations, black start and recovery in the short term.[^doe-pr100]

Exact parcels remain open because each zone must still pass approach, land, hazard, environmental, grid, water, workforce, community, sovereign-authority and lifecycle screens. The current federal Port Infrastructure Development Program recognizes coastal, inland-river and Great Lakes capital projects, including port microgrids and electrification.[^marad-pidp] Its FY2026 discretionary scale—about $488.6M—also shows the order-of-magnitude difference between ordinary port improvement and the proposed $344B base anchor network. That comparison is descriptive, not an argument to redirect the existing program.

## 6. Concurrency changes the fleet

The utility interpretation of `3C + 5M + 2G + 20R` assumes:

- two simultaneous catastrophic regional utility campaigns;
- one simultaneous catastrophic island or remote campaign;
- five major operations, split between regional and island/inland requirements;
- two global operations, one heavy and one compact; and
- 20% residual available capacity after those commitments.

The reference allocation creates a simultaneous demand for seven `NEC-300`-class firm-energy cores and five `NEC-60` cores. These are **source-equivalent mission slots**. They can be filled by a qualified nuclear core, unlike firm source or hybrid portfolio if the anchor contract and mission duration permit.

The deterministic inventory screen divides simultaneous demand by `(1 − reserve)` and assumed material availability. At 65% availability, this gives fourteen large cores and ten small cores after rounding. That calculation does not meet the declared reliability target. The binomial independent-availability screen requires fifteen large cores for a 95.78% probability of at least seven available and twelve small cores for a 97.45% probability of at least five.

The distinction changes the earlier fleet result:

| Question | Eight `NEC-300` cores | Fifteen `NEC-300` cores |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Probability at least three available at 65% independent availability | 97.47% | greater than 99.99% |
| Probability at least seven available | 16.91% | 95.78% |
| National utility concurrency implication | demonstration or limited campaign fleet | reference force-level inventory |

Independence is an optimistic screen. Shared designs, suppliers, digital systems, ports, weather, regulators, workforce and maintenance can create correlated outages. `UPCOM` therefore cannot treat fifteen identical reactors as fifteen independent service promises. The reference force also contains fourteen 150 MW non-nuclear hybrid firm-energy units and mandates compatible local, shore and renewable-storage sources. Source diversity is part of readiness, not an environmental or procurement preference.

The process and corridor force scales separately. The workbook's 95% independent-availability thresholds yield seventeen `WPB-100` units for ten simultaneous requirements, twenty `WPB-20` units for twelve, seventeen `SIG-100` groups, twenty `SIG-25` groups, thirty-two heavy corridor groups for eighteen demands, twenty-five island/high-head corridor groups for fourteen demands, twenty-five storage/distribution groups for fifteen demands and nineteen marine-support groups for ten demands. Each coefficient remains a force-design assumption pending mission-level queueing and repair evidence.

## 7. Posture and movement

The fleet should be distributed by operating basin, not gathered in one nuclear homeport. The reference allocation places large cores across Atlantic, Gulf and Pacific pools with two national test or reserve units. Small cores are distributed across Caribbean, central-Pacific, western-Pacific and south-Pacific/remote pools, with a national reserve unit. Non-nuclear firm units are more numerous and can circulate through inland and partner nodes.

This posture accepts a hard fact: geography cannot be conquered by platform speed alone. A core on the wrong ocean may require weeks, canal access, safe transit and a confirmed destination. Island utility response inside days therefore needs resident bridge generation, storage, process modules, shore interfaces, spares and crews even if the enduring core is mobile. The anchor's permanent systems should begin service before the nationally scarce source arrives.

Movement promises should be reported as distributions with separate terms for alert, source shutdown and preparation, transit, port or node opening, site confirmation, connection, technical release and staged load pickup. “Sailing time” is not time to public effect. The workbook uses planning bands only; real routing and weather studies remain a required next pass.

## 8. Workforce and profession

The reference utility projection enterprise reaches approximately **198,200 mature billets**:

- about 113,600 in rotating mobile formations;
- 33,200 resident at the twenty-six anchor complexes; and
- roughly 51,400 in command, education, independent safety, science, acquisition, depots and industrial mobilization.

This is a workforce hypothesis, not a staffing table. It demonstrates that a utility force cannot be crewed from reactor operators and linemen alone. The system needs mariners, nuclear and conventional plant operators, power engineers, protection technicians, water-process operators, laboratory scientists, hydrographers, environmental specialists, pipe and cable construction crews, welders and fusion technicians, heavy-equipment operators, logisticians, cyber and control engineers, health specialists, public administrators, trainers, inspectors and maintainers.

The occupation is national and local at once. Anchor operators must know the host system over years. Mobile crews must substitute across regions under a common technical constitution. Independent authorities must retain expertise and career paths outside the command whose readiness they judge. The Department's academy, technical-college network, apprenticeships, utility exchanges and reserve components should treat these as one utility-projection profession with several licensed branches.

## 9. A $1.698 trillion capital program

The 25-year capital hypothesis is an integrated work breakdown structure rather than an addition of prior study envelopes.

| Capital line | Reference |
| --- | ---: |
| Ten `RUA-300` complexes | $180B |
| Eight `IUA-60` complexes | $64B |
| Six `LUA-150` complexes | $60B |
| Two national integration complexes | $40B |
| Mobile energy, process, interface, corridor, storage and marine force | $571B |
| Shipyards, depots and production capacity | $160B |
| Research, rival prototypes and independent test | $140B |
| Education and workforce infrastructure | $80B |
| Common digital, standards and assurance systems | $40B |
| Strategic materials and configuration-controlled spares | $80B |
| Explicit 20% program reserve | $283B |
| **Total** | **$1.698T** |

The program averages $67.92B per year if divided evenly, but a rational build does not divide evenly. The workbook allocates 20% to years 1–5, 35% to years 6–10, 25% to years 11–15, 15% to years 16–20 and 5% to years 21–25. Capital therefore peaks near $118.86B per year during serial anchor construction and fleet production in years 6–10.

At maturity, the recurring operating screen is approximately **$119B per year**: $57.5B for fully burdened personnel at $290,000 per billet; $17.1B for mobile-force maintenance at 3% of modeled capital; $8.6B for anchor operation at 2.5%; and $36B for fuel and consumables, exercises, continuous research, global operations and reconstitution. This excludes host utility purchases, disaster-specific reconstruction and the rest of the Department of Resilience.

The scale is the point. A force that promises to replace regional water and power cannot be financed as a grant program that becomes visible after an event. At the same time, the $1.698T figure is not evidence of affordability or benefit. Independent reference-class estimates, construction schedules, land and environmental costs, decommissioning, cost covariance, operating revenue and failure cases remain open.

## 10. Industrial economy

`NUPS` would create a durable utility-projection sector rather than a handful of bespoke ship programs. Its major markets include:

- serial nuclear and non-nuclear energy cores across several scales;
- grid-forming converters, mobile substations, protection and black-start systems;
- modular desalination, advanced pretreatment, water-quality assurance and residuals control;
- marine cables, hoses, disconnects, moorings and extreme-hazard landings;
- large-bore pipe factories, robotic jointing, boosters, valves and recoverable crossings;
- storage, district distribution, cooling and sanitation systems;
- digital site twins, configuration evidence, offline control and utility cyber protection;
- tugs, salvage, workboats, heavy transporters and field maintenance systems; and
- full-scale test equipment for faults, contamination, waves, sediment, debris, corrosion and grid instability.

The government should own interface standards, reference designs where competition fails, strategic tooling, test data and enough production fallback to prevent a nominal supplier market from becoming one corporate failure point. Private primes should compete for system integration and serial production. Second sources should be qualified before the first source is indispensable.

Export potential is strongest in non-nuclear anchors, process modules, microgrids, corridors, environmental systems and training. Nuclear transfer adds safeguards, liability, security, fuel-cycle and sovereign-control questions. The Department can deploy U.S.-controlled energy cores to partner-compatible anchors without assuming that reactor ownership itself becomes a commodity.

## 11. Peacetime employment

A $119B annual operating system cannot wait for disasters. Every node and formation needs ordinary work that generates the same readiness evidence:

- grid-forming reserve, black-start and networked-microgrid service;
- water-security production, drought support and distribution exercises;
- port electrification and marine utility service;
- district cooling and critical-facility thermal protection;
- transmission, substation, water-main and crossing replacement;
- allied and territorial utility exercises;
- full-scale feed-water, grid-fault, weather-disconnect and corridor tests;
- strategic industrial loads that can curtail during emergency allocation; and
- regional mutual aid for ordinary outages and infrastructure failures.

NOAA's coastal data tools combine demographic, economic, hazard and infrastructure information while warning that jurisdictional boundaries and source datasets are not automatically compatible.[^noaa-coastal] `UPCOM` needs the same epistemic discipline in daily operations: service, exercise, maintenance and incident records retain their definitions and provenance rather than being converted into one self-congratulatory readiness score.

## 12. Failure architecture

The system must be designed against national failure, not only node failure.

| Failure | Bounded state | Institutional design consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One anchor complex lost | Alternate anchor and local bridge assume declared load blocks | Coverage includes spare receiving capacity and movement time |
| Wet and back nodes share hazard | Complex receives no readiness credit | Dissimilar pairing is a gate, not a preference |
| Nuclear fleet regulatory hold | Hybrid, shore and local sources use the same interfaces | Source-neutral contracts and non-nuclear inventory are mandatory |
| Common converter or control defect | Affected profile isolates; alternate certified profile operates manually | Supplier and software diversity are national readiness variables |
| Two basins close simultaneously | National and inland pools reroute without exhausting reserve | Fleet is allocated by covariance, not average utilization |
| Island route interrupted | Resident bridge and process systems sustain survival services | Remote posture includes people, spares and fuel, not paper access |
| Corridor construction slower than plan | Storage protects critical cells; public effect is reduced transparently | Commissioned route rate, not dispatched mass, controls claims |
| Workforce mobilization fails | Fewer safe systems operate; watchbills are not stretched | Personnel availability is coequal with equipment inventory |
| Host rejects allocation or operation | Technical system remains safe but does not impose service | Civil compact and consent precede deployment |
| Environmental limit binds | Process derates or stops; alternate source/discharge activates | Emergency mission does not erase environmental mass balance |
| Industrial surge misses schedule | Strategic spares and second sources preserve bounded operations | Factories, tooling and supplier health are part of force readiness |
| Cost growth consumes reserve | Rival pathway, quantity or mission requirement is reconsidered openly | Reserve cannot conceal an unreconciled baseline |

The most dangerous failure is institutional self-deception: counting a node, core, crew or agreement in several simultaneous promises. `UPCOM` must maintain one authoritative allocation ledger in which an asset belongs to one readiness pool, a source-equivalent slot names its actual technology, and substitute capacity carries its own movement, fuel, safety and availability constraints.

## 13. Research gates and continuation

This pass advances the national utility projection idea to an `R1/R2` institutional and posture architecture with `R2+` force, reliability, workforce and capital arithmetic. It does not select a port, authorize a reactor, solve a grid, demonstrate a corridor, validate a workforce pipeline or produce an independent estimate.

The next evidence program must:

1. replace candidate zones with transparent parcel alternatives and explicit rejection records;
2. build population-service-time catchments from compatible Census, utility, NOAA, FEMA, USGS, BTS and USACE data;
3. model travel and node-opening time under damaged road, rail, waterway, port and grid conditions;
4. quantify hazard covariance among wet nodes, back nodes, corridors, bases, suppliers and safe harbors;
5. solve representative regional, island and inland electrical and water networks;
6. run source-neutral full mission trials at a national integration complex;
7. model fleet queues, repair distributions, scheduled outages and correlated class holds rather than independent Bernoulli availability alone;
8. derive occupation and training pipelines from task and watch requirements;
9. obtain reference-class capital and recurring-cost ranges with schedule and industrial constraints;
10. reconcile `NUPS` with the Department-wide capital, technology and proving-network work breakdown structures; and
11. compare the system with a distributed permanent-infrastructure strategy that uses little or no mobile nuclear capacity.

Rendering remains blocked. The first eventual network visual should be a rigorous operations map showing wet/back pairs, corridors, source classes, home basins, damaged-route travel bands, hazard covariance, alternate anchors, service catchments and the exact assets allocated under simultaneous campaigns. A map of attractive dots or a fleet portrait would overstate knowledge the research has not yet earned.

## 14. Strategic conclusion

The Department of Resilience becomes a peer instrument of national power when it can make and keep promises that ordinary emergency coordination cannot: not that help will be requested, but that essential service will be established at a stated scale, in a stated time, through a known receiving system, while other catastrophes are already consuming the force.

The National Utility Projection System is one mature expression of that ambition. Its twenty-six anchor complexes are civil strategic bases. Its fleets are mobile utilities, not heroic vehicles. Its command joins six professional services without displacing local authority. Its industrial base produces interfaces, energy, water and corridors at a scale large enough to change both domestic resilience and global stabilization.

Most importantly, its size is generated by simultaneous public effects rather than by analogy to a navy. That logic can reject a nuclear platform, add an inland node, expand a corridor force or reduce a mission without diminishing the ambition. The ambition is the assured public effect.
