# Institutional architecture: from coordination system to operating force

## Interim judgment

The proposal is not a reform of emergency management. It is the creation of a **second great instrument of American state capacity**, deliberately comparable to national defense in political standing, industrial depth, professional identity, strategic mobility, technological ambition, and sustained public investment.

Its mission is broader than disaster response:

> **Project resilience: prevent the collapse of essential civilian systems; overwhelm catastrophic service failures with organized capacity; stabilize affected societies; and transfer restored functions to legitimate civil owners.**

This requires a cabinet department, a large full-time operating force, reserve and state components, service academies and technical colleges, national laboratories, proving grounds, arsenals, shipyards, aviation and maritime fleets, strategic lift, regional and overseas posture, a mobilization base, and a generation of purpose-built industrial firms. A trillion-dollar research and capital program would be a beginning, not the mature annual operating model. If “equal” is taken seriously, long-run funding must be indexed to national-defense effort rather than confined to the fiscal scale of a conventional civilian agency.

The existing United States system is useful here mainly as **boundary evidence**. It reveals recurrent missions, interfaces, failure modes, authorities, and skills. It is not the template. Today’s organizations may be absorbed, split, transferred, or retired during the build. No inherited agency, emergency-support function, regional map, contracting habit, or declaration sequence is presumed to survive simply because it exists.

The mature design still needs two complementary systems:

1. a **civil sovereignty system** that determines legitimate priorities, protects constitutional and community authority, and defines the conditions of employment; and
2. a **national resilience establishment** that owns, generates, deploys, sustains, and regenerates the capacity needed to deliver water, power, access, care, communications, shelter, environmental control, and public works at strategic scale.

The ambition is radical centralization of **capacity**, not of **community rule**. A governor, tribal government, mayor, health authority, or infrastructure owner should not need to own a strategic airlift fleet or mobile grid architecture. The national force should not decide a community’s political future merely because it brought those capabilities.

## What exists now—and why it is insufficient

The present system contains useful design evidence, but no current institution approximates the proposed scale or mandate.

| Existing element | Evidence it provides | What the new establishment must supersede |
|---|---|---|
| FEMA and the National Response Framework | Civil coordination, common language, lifeline framing, and federal mission assignment are necessary | A system that mainly coordinates and reimburses becomes one administrative component inside a department that also owns decisive operating capacity |
| National Incident Management System and Incident Command System | Modular command, common terminology, and unified authority are valid interface principles | Incident organization remains, but national units arrive as complete forces rather than requests assembled from many agencies |
| U.S. Army Corps of Engineers emergency missions | Power, debris, water, roofing, and infrastructure work can be organized as technical mission packages | Part-time mission teams and contract-management cells give way to standing engineering formations with organic plant, crews, lift, maintenance, and depth |
| FEMA Urban Search and Rescue | Typed multidisciplinary teams, equipment caches, rapid activation, and self-sufficiency work | A narrow 72-hour rescue construct expands into weeks-to-months stabilization formations across all lifelines |
| National Disaster Medical System | Patient movement and definitive care must form one pathway | Intermittent teams and borrowed civilian beds are supplemented by a major federal health force with deployable definitive-care and workforce-replacement capacity |
| National Interagency Fire Center system | Predictive posture, local–regional–national mobilization, and explicit scarcity arbitration work | A hazard-specific allocation system becomes an all-hazard national readiness and deployment command with large owned fleets and crews |
| National Guard and dual-status command | State–federal command interfaces and locally rooted reserve forces can coexist | Civil resilience receives its own state and reserve components rather than depending on force availability generated for defense |
| Coast Guard incident-management and deployable specialized forces | A standing service can combine daily operations, technical authority, regional basing, and surge | The resilience establishment builds independent air, land, maritime, health, infrastructure, and community-stabilization services at far greater scale |

FEMA doctrine explicitly states that businesses and infrastructure owners have primary responsibility for operating and repairing their systems, while federal structures coordinate support and stabilization.[^nrf-private] The Stafford Act likewise makes major-disaster assistance supplemental and normally predicates a declaration on a governor or tribal chief executive finding that the event exceeds local and state capability.[^stafford-401] Those are legitimate governance rules. They also explain why the current system cannot be treated as an inventory of assured national capacity.

Current operational fragments further demonstrate the difference between a system that can summon capacity and one that owns it. USACE planning and response teams cover debris, emergency power, temporary housing, infrastructure assessment, commodities, and water, but are commonly staffed by trained volunteers from the Corps workforce.[^usace-prt] NDMS relies substantially on intermittent federal responders who normally work elsewhere; GAO found that its prior enrollment target did not adequately account for nationwide or concurrent events, at-risk populations, or the availability of other medical responders.[^gao-ndms] FEMA's own disaster workforce mixes permanent staff, temporary full-time staff, local hires, and intermittent reservists; after two major 2024 disasters, only 4 percent of its incident-management workforce was reportedly available to deploy.[^gao-fema-workforce]

This is not an argument that those organizations have failed. It is evidence that the United States optimized them for **coordination, augmentation, reimbursement, and supplementation**. The proposed establishment is optimized for **assurance, mass, speed, endurance, concurrency, technological overmatch, and geopolitical reach**.

## Institutional alternatives

### Alternative A: enlarge FEMA into an operating service

This appears administratively simple, but it would combine grant administration, insurance, policy, declaration support, recovery adjudication, incident coordination, acquisition, and field-force command inside one organization. Those functions have different time horizons, incentives, and professional cultures. A grant-and-coordination agency can purchase assets, but owning a permanently ready technical force would become one mission among many and would compete with statutory program administration.

### Alternative B: assign the mission to the Department of Defense

The armed forces possess strategic mobility, planning depth, logistics, engineering, communications, and command systems. Yet making disaster stabilization a defense mission would preserve the very asymmetry this project is intended to change: civil protection would remain a dependent use of capacity designed and scheduled for another purpose. It would also militarize routine domestic support and make readiness contingent on defense availability.

### Alternative C: federate existing agencies under a standing council

This would improve plans and coordination but leave asset ownership, readiness standards, personnel systems, procurement, and budgets fragmented. GAO has repeatedly identified fragmentation across disaster assistance and recovery; more than 30 federal entities participate through programs with different requirements and timeframes.[^gao-fragmentation] A council can arbitrate. It cannot make an unavailable crew, aircraft, substation, or water plant become available.

### Alternative D: create an independent national resilience establishment

This is the recommended mature-state structure. It separates resilience from defense but makes it a peer rather than an adjunct: cabinet-level budget authority, multiple operating services, unified geographic and functional commands, a professional and reserve personnel system, dedicated strategic mobility, a science and acquisition complex, an industrial mobilization base, global posture, and a permanent constitutional interface with civil authorities.

The proposal assumes the political greenlight specified for this project. The test is therefore not near-term legislative plausibility; it is whether the architecture would be coherent if the nation chose to make the mission real.

## Recommended mature structure

Create a **Department of Resilience** as a cabinet department and establish a statutory **National Resilience Establishment** comparable in permanence to the national-defense establishment. Names are provisional; scale and functional separation are not.

The name **Department of Resilience** is now retained as the canonical cabinet-level identity. Pass 8 distinguishes it from the **National Resilience Establishment**—the services, commands, schools, science, acquisition, depots, reserves and industrial base that generate capacity—and the **Resilience Force**, meaning the certified formations actually available for employment. This prevents the existence of a bureaucracy, an equipment inventory or a contract from being mistaken for operational readiness. See [Mature force design](../force/mature-force-design.md).

```text
President and Congress
└── Secretary of Resilience
    ├── Civil Resilience Authority             civil outcomes, activation, assistance, recovery
    ├── Chief of the Resilience Staff          joint requirements, plans, readiness, doctrine
    │   ├── Six operating services             organize, train, equip, sustain
    │   ├── Domestic geographic commands       employ joint forces in the United States
    │   ├── Global Resilience Command          overseas posture and operations
    │   └── Unified functional commands        mobility, sustainment, medical, information,
    │                                          industry, training and doctrine
    ├── Science, Engineering and Acquisition   laboratories, prototypes, programs, test, standards
    ├── Education and Profession               academy, technical colleges, graduate schools
    └── Independent oversight                  safety, civil rights, cost, test, audit, inspector general
```

### Department leadership and Resilience Staff

The Secretary of Resilience would be responsible for national resilience policy, force employment, acquisition, international engagement, and preparedness. A civilian Deputy Secretary would manage the department. A **Chief of the Resilience Staff**, supported by the chiefs of six operating services, would be the principal professional adviser on force readiness and employment.

The Resilience Staff would conduct joint planning, force design, requirements integration, global posture, readiness assessment, and lessons. It would prevent each service from building a self-contained response system that cannot combine with the others.

### Six operating services

The services organize, train, equip, and sustain forces. Joint commands employ them.

1. **Land Engineering Service** — access, earthmoving, bridging, debris, flood control, temporary works, housing infrastructure, ground transport, and large-scale fire ground operations.
2. **Air Response Service** — strategic and tactical lift, aerial fire control, sensing, communications relay, evacuation, medical movement, airborne delivery, and uncrewed aviation.
3. **Maritime Response Service** — ocean, coastal, riverine, and lake access; sealift; offshore life-support production; port restoration; salvage; evacuation; and floating bases.
4. **Infrastructure Restoration Service** — power, water, wastewater, communications, grid components, industrial controls, fuel systems, utility repair, and deployable public works.
5. **Health Protection Service** — field and definitive care, public health, medical logistics, patient movement, chronic-care continuity, workforce replacement, veterinary support, and fatality management.
6. **Community Stabilization Service** — shelter, feeding, essential goods, accessible services, public information, civil liaison, education continuity, temporary administration, housing transition, and local economic restart.

This division is not a prediction that every occupation fits neatly inside one service. It establishes durable professional homes and acquisition sponsors while requiring cross-service response task forces for operations.

### Unified geographic commands

Standing geographic commands would plan and employ joint forces. Domestic command boundaries would be designed from travel time, hazard covariance, infrastructure networks, watersheds, population, ports, and territorial obligations—not inherited agency regions. A separate **Global Resilience Command** would maintain overseas access agreements, liaison networks, forward stocks, partner training, and rapidly deployable stabilization groups.

Geographic commanders would control assigned active forces and receive additional units during surge. They would maintain standing campaign plans for heat, fire, drought, flood, storm, seismic, public-health, and infrastructure-failure risk—not merely incident plans.

### Unified functional commands

Some capabilities are too scarce, interconnected, or global to divide regionally:

- **Strategic Mobility Command** would own or control long-range airlift, sealift, rail mobilization, common-user trucking, reception hubs, aerial refueling where justified, and global movement control.
- **Strategic Sustainment Command** would control depots, arsenals, shipyards, aviation maintenance centers, strategic stocks, fuel, medical supply, and the national distribution network.
- **Resilience Medical Command** would regulate patients and scarce clinical capacity across regions while the Health Protection Service generates the units.
- **Information and Forecast Command** would fuse hazards, infrastructure status, vulnerability, supply chains, public health, and readiness into operational forecasts and allocation recommendations.
- **Joint Campaign Assurance Command** would maintain dependency and common-mode registers, allocate correlation budgets, run adaptive force-closure models, certify substitution rules, and direct blind loss-of-node and loss-of-enabler trials. It would advise rather than absorb the Civil Resilience Authority's public-priority power or the independent technical authorities' safety-release power.
- **Industrial Mobilization Command** would maintain supplier maps, surge contracts, government-owned production capability, critical-material reserves, repair capacity, and emergency production plans.
- **Training and Doctrine Command** would run common education, collective exercises, certification, doctrine, and the lessons system.

The [National Resilience Deployment and Distribution Enterprise](../force/joint-deployment-and-distribution-enterprise.md) makes the mobility–sustainment boundary explicit. Strategic Mobility Command owns the origin-to-theater movement and damaged-entry architecture; the supported Theater-Opening and Theater Sustainment Commands clear the force to operating locations; Strategic Sustainment Command owns national depots, replacement and reconstitution. This prevents “transport delivered” from being confused with “public service operating” without creating duplicate owners for the same supply chain.

The wildfire system demonstrates the value of predictive posture, explicit preparedness levels, and national allocation of scarce resources.[^nifc-mobilization][^nifc-levels] The new establishment would apply that logic continuously across the entire force, backed by owned fleets and formations.

### Civil Resilience Authority

A separate authority inside the department would handle intergovernmental legitimacy, preparedness standards, declarations or successor activation mechanisms, grants, insurance, individual assistance, recovery finance, and public accountability. FEMA's present functions would be divided among this authority, the operating establishment, and other departmental components; FEMA would not persist as a coequal legacy organization.

The authority would act as **civil principal and outcome governor**. It would identify the affected governments' priorities, validate force employment, define acceptable transfer conditions, and independently evaluate whether operations improved civilian outcomes.

### Resilience Science, Engineering, and Acquisition System

The department would need an ecosystem, not a procurement office:

- an **Advanced Resilience Projects Agency** for high-risk, high-payoff research;
- mission laboratories for water, power, fire, climate control, medicine, materials, autonomy, logistics, and human systems;
- national proving grounds for coastal storm, wildfire, flood, extreme heat and cold, seismic, contaminated, remote, and urban environments;
- service acquisition executives and program offices;
- independent cost assessment and operational test agencies;
- a Resilience Standards Institute controlling open physical and data interfaces;
- public design bureaus able to act as technically sophisticated buyers; and
- industrial finance, export credit, and allied co-development offices.

The operating services define effects and provide test units; acquisition professionals manage baselines; independent evaluators determine whether a system delivers the promised service under realistic failure conditions.

### National production, depot, and logistics base

The establishment would own a minimum sovereign production and repair base for capabilities that markets cannot guarantee under correlated demand. This includes strategic depots, maintenance centers, mobile-equipment arsenals, water and power system production lines, medical-equipment reserves, aviation overhaul, and access to purpose-built ship construction and repair.

Private primes and suppliers would carry much of the production load, but multi-year demand, government reference designs, open interfaces, second sources, surge tooling, and public fallback capacity would prevent a nominal industrial base from becoming a single-point failure.

FEMA's logistics and Mobile Emergency Response Support systems demonstrate only the first layer of the problem.[^gao-logistics][^fema-mers] The new command must source, move, receive, maintain, refuel, repair, rotate, replace, reproduce, and regenerate entire forces.

### Education and profession

The department would operate:

- a four-year **United States Resilience Academy**;
- a national network of technical colleges and apprenticeship centers;
- graduate schools for resilience strategy, engineering, logistics, public health, and civil command;
- regional exercise centers; and
- a national lessons and professional-journal system.

The Academy is not required to make the institution look military. It is required because a peer instrument of national power needs a durable profession capable of integrating engineering, medicine, logistics, climate science, public administration, ethics, and command.

### Force components

The establishment would include a large active component, a federal reserve, federally funded state and territorial resilience guards, direct tribal compacts, a civil auxiliary, and a certified industrial reserve. The active force provides the immediate and technically demanding core; the other components provide local presence, concurrency, endurance, specialist depth, and national mobilization.

### Funding parity rule

The institution should not depend on episodic supplemental appropriations after catastrophe. A mature funding rule would establish:

- a protected annual readiness and operations appropriation;
- multi-year procurement and construction accounts;
- a national resilience research-and-engineering share at least comparable to defense R&D intensity;
- a revolving industrial-mobilization and export-finance authority;
- a separately transparent disaster-benefits and recovery account; and
- a long-run target tied to a stated fraction of national-defense expenditure or GDP.

Separating the operating establishment from benefits and recovery transfers matters. Otherwise a major disaster can make the department appear better funded while its deployable force remains hollow.

### Transition from the inherited system

The current system should be mapped function by function, then deliberately dismantled or transferred:

- FEMA's coordination and assistance functions move to the Civil Resilience Authority; operational logistics, MERS, US&R, and incident workforce capabilities become force or command nuclei.
- HHS emergency medical response and NDMS become the initial cadre of the Health Protection Service, while ordinary health policy remains outside the department.
- Federal wildland-fire mobilization, aviation, and national incident-management capabilities seed Land Engineering and Air Response formations, while land-management agencies retain stewardship responsibilities.
- USACE emergency power, debris, water, infrastructure, and temporary-facility missions transfer into standing Land and Infrastructure formations; the Corps' navigation, civil works, and defense-engineering missions require separate decisions.
- USAID/BHA deployable disaster capabilities provide a nucleus for Global Resilience Command, under foreign-policy direction.
- The National Guard, Coast Guard, public-health agencies, utilities, nonprofits, and local responders remain powerful partners, but they are no longer the hidden capacity base on which the national promise depends.

The end state is not a better interagency committee. It is an establishment able to field and sustain its own national-scale response while integrating partners by choice and comparative advantage rather than by necessity.

## Civil command and operational control

The architecture must distinguish **incident authority**, **mission assignment**, and **force command**.

- The affected jurisdiction and unified command determine incident objectives, legal priorities, and acceptable outcomes.
- The federal civil coordinating authority validates national support missions and resolves interagency priorities.
- The Resilience Force commander exercises operational control over assigned Force units, selects tactics, enforces safety and professional standards, and reports delivered effects.
- Infrastructure owners retain their legal responsibilities and technical authority over their systems unless emergency law provides otherwise.
- Independent safety, civil-rights, environmental, and inspector-general channels remain outside the operational chain.

NIMS already provides the core grammar. Under unified command, participating authorities jointly determine priorities and objectives while each agency retains its statutory authority; organizations without jurisdictional authority support through coordinating and liaison structures.[^nims] The proposed Force should arrive fluent in that system. Its scale should not give it unilateral control over a community.

### The two-key rule

For major domestic operations, field employment should normally require two keys:

1. a **civil key**—a valid request, declaration, compact activation, or defined catastrophic authority; and
2. an **operational key**—a certified mission package with adequate safety, sustainment, and command arrangements.

The civil key prevents self-deployment into a jurisdiction. The operational key prevents leaders from ordering symbolic but unsustainable missions that consume scarce capacity or place responders at unacceptable risk.

### Anticipatory posture without anticipatory takeover

The Force should have standing authority to move within federal facilities, contract transport, load equipment, alert reserves, and pre-position at agreed staging areas before impact. Crossing into incident employment would still require the civil key. This separates **movement time**, which can be purchased in advance, from **authority to act**, which remains politically accountable.

### Catastrophic incident authority

A mature system also needs a narrow rule for events in which the requesting authority is incapacitated or communications are destroyed. The rule should authorize temporary life-saving and life-support action when four conditions are met:

1. credible evidence of catastrophic service failure;
2. inability to establish timely contact with competent civil authority;
3. a reversible, least-intrusive mission limited to life safety and service stabilization; and
4. mandatory transfer to civil direction as soon as contact is restored.

This is an emergency bridge, not a general federal preemption doctrine.

## Employment architecture

### National level

A **National Resilience Operations Center** would maintain the common operating picture, recommend preparedness levels, allocate scarce strategic assets, and coordinate multiple geographic commands. The Secretary or a Senate-confirmed civilian deputy would adjudicate cross-sector and cross-jurisdiction priorities during national scarcity. The Chief of the Resilience Staff would convert those decisions into joint operational direction.

Service chiefs generate ready forces; geographic and functional commanders employ them. That distinction—adapted because it solves a real accountability problem, not because the structure is military—reduces the temptation to hide unready capacity or to let a service optimize deployments around its own equipment.

### Regional level

Domestic geographic Resilience Commands should be standing joint civil headquarters with permanently assigned forces, not headquarters assembled after impact. Each would contain:

- an intergovernmental coordination cell;
- operations, planning, logistics, finance, safety, legal, and public-information functions compatible with ICS;
- embedded liaisons from health, energy, transportation, environmental, communications, agriculture, housing, defense, and law-enforcement agencies;
- tribal and territorial liaison capacity appropriate to the region;
- infrastructure-owner coordination cells with pre-negotiated data and restoration protocols;
- a regional force-allocation board; and
- a deployable command element capable of becoming part of a unified coordination group.

Command areas should remain stable enough to build relationships. Operational force packages should cross them when travel time, watersheds, power interconnections, port access, smoke transport, evacuation flows, or national allocation dictate a different geometry.

### Incident level

The Force would deploy an **Integration and Effects Team** before heavy capability. Its task is to translate incident objectives into measurable effect packages, identify receiving and sustainment constraints, and prevent an asset push unsupported by local access, fuel, labor, or distribution.

This team would replace the need to assemble an ad hoc federal operating headquarters from legacy agencies. It would be the joint force's forward headquarters and technical requirements cell, working inside the larger civil command structure.

## Domestic, territorial, tribal, and international employment

### States and local jurisdictions

States would retain primary police power and incident leadership. Standing agreements would define alert, staging, cost, credentialing, data, workers' compensation, liability, and transfer conditions in advance. The objective is to turn a bespoke request chain into a pre-engineered interface.

### Tribal nations

Tribal governments must be treated as sovereign partners with direct request and command interfaces, not as local subdivisions. Regional commands would maintain permanent tribal liaison offices, culturally and legally competent planning teams, and pre-negotiated rules for operations on tribal lands.

### Territories, islands, and remote communities

These environments require a different readiness promise because strategic lift, port restoration, fuel, water, and communications can all fail together. Forward stocks and organic access capability are not optional conveniences; they are part of the effect chain. Territorial representation must therefore exist in force design and allocation, not only in incident liaison.

### International operations

International deployment would be a separate legal mode undertaken at the request of the host nation and under U.S. foreign-policy direction. The Force should be compatible with United Nations and international civil-protection standards, capable of supporting rather than overwhelming host systems, and designed to transfer operations to local or multilateral actors.

The European Union's rescEU reserve shows that supranational ownership can complement national systems: it fully finances strategically hosted firefighting, medical, shelter, energy, CBRN, transport, and logistics capacities for use when national resources are insufficient.[^resceu] Germany's THW demonstrates another model—standardized technical units distributed through local sections, supported by a small professional core, national logistics and training, and specialized deployable modules.[^thw] Japan's Emergency Fire Response Teams demonstrate nationally typed local units that can be mobilized across jurisdictional boundaries under national direction while operating under affected-area leadership.[^japan-efrt]

These are not scale analogues for the proposed U.S. force. They are evidence that distributed ownership, common standards, central financing, national allocation, and local incident authority can coexist.

## Innovation establishment

The Department is not mature if it can only buy what today's emergency market already sells. Pass 16 adds a standing invention-to-field system to the National Resilience Establishment:

- the **Advanced Resilience Projects Agency (`ARPA-R`)** frames measured high-risk opportunities, funds rival mechanisms and can terminate or redirect them;
- **National Resilience Laboratories** own long-lived mission science, models, reference data, metrology and shared instruments;
- a **National Resilience Proving Network** supplies representative, destructive and complete-mission environments at sovereign scale;
- the independent **Resilience Test and Evaluation Authority (`RTEA`)** owns test plans, configuration lock, data custody, replication, readiness findings and operational acceptance;
- **Service Technology Commands** integrate mature prototypes with formations, doctrine, training, maintainers, logistics and civil command;
- a **Resilience Manufacturing and Mobilization Administration** owns manufacturing readiness, supplier depth, surge tooling, digital production baselines, repair and depots;
- **National Resilience University Consortia** protect long-horizon science and workforce formation; and
- a **Standards, Data and Public Assurance Office** maintains open interfaces, configuration provenance, privacy, accessibility, environmental evidence and public-effect accounting.

Creation, proof, production, employment and civil authority remain separate decisions. A research sponsor cannot certify its own system; a technology command cannot create incident authority; a vendor cannot define the public-effect metric by which its product is accepted. See [The resilience technology frontier](../innovation/technology-frontier.md).

## Functions the new institution should not absorb

An equal national investment does not justify an unlimited mandate. The Department and Force should not become:

- a national police or border force;
- the routine operator of local utilities;
- a substitute for public-health departments or civilian hospitals;
- a general construction agency for ordinary infrastructure;
- an insurer of every private loss;
- a permanent displacement-camp authority;
- the default owner of recovery policy, housing benefits, or land-use decisions; or
- a mechanism for bypassing environmental, civil-rights, labor, tribal-consultation, or procurement law without defined emergency necessity.

Its endpoint is **stable transfer**: the point at which essential services can be sustained by responsible civil, community, commercial, or recovery actors without emergency force support.

## Institutional success tests

The mature architecture should be judged against eight tests:

1. **Assurance:** Can leaders identify what capacity is actually available, qualified, maintained, transportable, and sustainable now?
2. **Legitimacy:** Does the affected civil authority retain control of public priorities and outcomes?
3. **Speed:** Can anticipatory decisions remove avoidable time without creating unauthorized action?
4. **Concurrency:** Can the system meet more than one regional catastrophe without stripping all residual national capacity?
5. **Interoperability:** Can units from different regions and components combine without improvised command, data, fuel, or mechanical interfaces?
6. **Endurance:** Can initial response rotate into stabilization without a collapse in staffing, spares, or supply?
7. **Transfer:** Is every mission designed to hand service back to a responsible owner at a defined condition?
8. **Accountability:** Can Congress and the public distinguish inventory, readiness, deployment, delivered effect, cost, and outcome?

These tests form the institutional requirements for the force-composition and readiness design that follows.
