# F36 `CISA-40` `SA6` Root A drainage, firewater and recovery technology constitution

## Executive judgment

The [internal cell and source-boundary constitution](f36-cisa40-sa6-internal-cell-and-source-boundary-design-constitution.md) gave every Root A source compartment its own 150 mm reference liquid route, 15 m³ source sump, 40 m³ firewater vault, recovery handoff and native evidence path. It also left the most important Root A question unresolved: whether the emergency system actually reduces consequence when liquid release, pool growth, ignition, cooling water, damaged terrain, debris, neighboring sources, environmental retention and remote recovery interact.

This pass creates `RFA-1`, the Root A drainage, firewater and recovery technology constitution. Its primary judgment is:

> Do not treat drainage as plumbing or firewater as an unqualified benefit. Compete complete source–surface–drain–fire–water–runoff–recovery architectures, measure every intervention as part of the causal system, and require a closed source, water, heat and environmental ledger before Root A can earn physical form.

Three unlike candidates enter without selection. `AF-1` is a passive gravity cascade: a fixed refractory grade, two passive source slots, separately graded firewater return and minimal dependence on active energy. `AF-2` is a cellular closed-loop deck: distributed underdeck source cartridges, local capture cells and recirculating impingement cooling intended to keep most water out of the source path. `AF-3` is a robotic capture field: deployable refractory grade, autonomous drain clearing, remote skimming, localized cooling and modular source–water–agent fractionation designed to operate after the prepared surface is damaged.

The candidates are complete-chain hypotheses, not equipment sketches. Each includes source isolation, surface behavior, liquid transfer, sump or cartridge capacity, thermal protection, water control, environmental custody, remote intervention, evidence and restoration. Each receives two unlike builders. Forty-eight root-native articles preserve hydraulic, fire, destructive, recovery, regeneration and calibration roles. Six public rigs across three proving campuses support a 306-event campaign: 72 development, 192 paired, 24 destructive, 12 reset and six outside-site reconstruction events. The 192 paired events form 96 matched control/challenge pairs across six candidate–builder roots, two operational sites and eight Root A challenges.

The inherited one-compartment source is 2,000 kg of a declared planning liquid at 800 kg/m³ and 42.588 MJ/kg. It occupies 2.500 m³ and contains 85.176 GJ. The inherited 25 m² pool-fire screen at 0.055 kg/m²/s yields 1.375 kg/s burning, approximately 58.559 MW heat release and 20.495 MW radiant power at the declared 0.35 radiative fraction. ## 2. The coupled Root A problem

### 2.1 Five inventories, not one fire

A Root A event carries at least five inventories:

1. source liquid remaining in the vessel and route;
2. source liquid collected in the sump, cartridges or casualty vessel;
3. source liquid spread on the surface, burned, volatilized or lost;
4. suppression water and agent applied, recirculated, retained or released; and
5. water–source–agent–solid mixtures requiring separation and environmental disposition.

An ordinary fire metric can improve while the source ledger deteriorates. Flame can disappear because liquid is driven into a drain. Surface temperature can fall because water spreads the pool. A vault can remain below its geometric capacity while foam, debris or an unmeasured heel eliminates usable capacity. `RFA-1` therefore denies single-metric success.

### 2.2 The terminal-source clock

The inherited Root A target is thirty seconds. The clock stops only when independent observation shows that new source flow has ended or entered a bounded recovery path, pool area is no longer growing, no neighboring compartment is recruited, controlled inventories remain inside their accepted envelopes and evidence remains live.

The thirty-second target is much shorter than complete fire suppression or product recovery. It demands rapid isolation and diversion first. Cooling, fire control and recovery continue on their own clocks. This separation prevents a long fire response from disguising failure to terminate the source.

### 2.3 Water is a state-changing intervention

Water can remove heat, shield a neighboring source, cool structure and reduce responder exposure. It can also increase total mobile volume, carry source through damaged drainage, overflow retention, alter fuel-layer geometry, hide mass in an emulsion, spread burning liquid or make later recovery harder.

The program therefore records gross application, recirculated fraction, net retained volume, inlet and return temperature, vaporized fraction, source entrainment, agent identity, solids and every controlled discharge. A statement such as “firewater operated” is not evidence of benefit.

## 3. Three unlike complete-chain candidates

### 3.1 `AF-1` passive gravity cascade

`AF-1` uses a fixed refractory surface with positive grade toward two physically independent source slots. Firewater follows a separately graded surface return. Passive diverters and thermal shutters place the source in its sump without normal power. Local agent application can blanket the source path after isolation.

Its strength is transparent passive behavior. Grade, slot identity and liquid direction can be surveyed before an event. Its weakness is sensitivity to damaged terrain, debris, thermal distortion and long open-loop water demand. Its preliminary design assumes 0.03534 m² aggregate drain area, 0.50 m hydraulic head, 0.62 discharge coefficient, 2.4 m³/min water application, no recirculation and no required deployment interval.

`AF-1` must prove that surface separation remains real during fire. A clean survey before release cannot establish that fuel, water and agent remain separate after boiling, debris and barrier motion.

### 3.2 `AF-2` cellular closed-loop deck

`AF-2` divides the active surface into replaceable capture cells. Each cell drains source into a locally identified underdeck cartridge rather than a single long open route. A separate closed cooling loop impinges on protected surfaces and returns to a heat-rejection system; it is designed not to enter the source cells.

The architecture's strength is low net water demand and local source ancestry. Its 85-percent planning recirculation holds thirty-minute retained water to 7.2 m³. Its weakness is hidden volume, cleaning complexity, cartridge interaction, underdeck fire, fragment vulnerability and active energy dependence. A closed-loop label earns no credit if seals, drains or heat exchangers allow the source and water paths to mix.

### 3.3 `AF-3` robotic capture field

`AF-3` assumes that fixed terrain may be damaged or absent. Robots deploy refractory grading mats and barriers, clear drains, place localized cooling, skim a stable pool and connect modular fractionation units. The architecture can reconfigure around debris and may export well beyond one vehicle or test cell.

Its strength is adaptability and distance between hazard and people. Its weakness is a four-minute planning deployment interval, dependence on robot energy and localization, hose and cable routing, exposure of actuators to heat, and the risk that active recovery begins before the source is stable. The passive source-isolation path must therefore remain independent. Robotics may improve consequence after isolation; it cannot justify waiting four minutes to stop source flow.

### 3.4 Why all three remain alive

The coarse screens do not select among them. `AF-1` offers the strongest passive story but collides with long-duration water retention. `AF-2` controls retained water but creates a more complex hidden boundary. `AF-3` can repair terrain function but has active-system and time dependencies. The competition exists because these are different causal bets.

## 4. Planning physics

### 4.1 Source energy and pool fire

The one-compartment source is:

\[
V_s=\frac{2{,}000\ \mathrm{kg}}{800\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}}=2.500\ \mathrm{m^3},
\]

\[
E_s=2{,}000\ \mathrm{kg}\times42.588\ \mathrm{MJ/kg}=85.176\ \mathrm{GJ}.
\]

For pool area (A), declared burning flux \(\dot m''=0.055\ \mathrm{kg/m^2/s}\) and lower heating value (H), the screening heat-release rate is:

\[
\dot Q=A\dot m''H.
\]

At 25 m², \(\dot m=1.375\ \mathrm{kg/s}\) and \(\dot Q\approx58.559\ \mathrm{MW}\). With radiative fraction 0.35, the screen assigns approximately 20.495 MW to radiation. If all 2,000 kg were available to the constant-flux pool, the notional burn duration would be approximately 24.24 minutes.

The simple point-source distance to a target radiant flux (q) is:

\[
r=\sqrt{\frac{\chi_r\dot Q}{4\pi q}}.
\]

At 1.6 kW/m², the 25 m² screen gives approximately 31.927 m. A 100 m² area screen gives approximately 63.855 m. Neither is a consequence boundary. The equations omit flame shape, smoke, atmospheric transmission, shielding, wind, fuel depth, surface heat transfer and transient burning.

### 4.2 Gravity-drain screen

The preliminary flow screen is:

\[
Q=C_dA_e\sqrt{2gh},
\]

where (A_e) is effective drain area and (h) the liquid head. The source-rate comparator remains 2,000 kg over 120 seconds, or 16.667 kg/s. This is intentionally different from the thirty-second terminal-source target: a source may take longer to finish transferring while new uncontrolled release has already stopped.

All candidates retain calculated positive margin under the compound half-area and 0.75-head screen. `AF-1`, `AF-2` and `AF-3` retain approximately 7.106, 5.148 and 7.233 kg/s. That result only admits the architectures into experiment. Real capacity may be governed by entrance loss, surface depth, vapor, air binding, debris, thermal distortion or route damage.

### 4.3 Water heat-transfer screen

For applied water rate \(\dot V_w\), heat capacity per volume (c_v), temperature rise \(\Delta T\), vaporized fraction (f_v), latent heat (L_v) and transfer efficiency \(\eta\), the screen is:

\[
\dot Q_w=\frac{\dot V_w}{60}\left(c_v\Delta T+f_vL_v\right)\eta.
\]

The declared hypotheses are 4.186 MJ/m³-K, 55 K, 0.15 vaporized fraction, 2,257 MJ/m³ latent heat and 0.65 efficiency. These yield approximately 14.788 MW for `AF-1`, 9.859 MW for `AF-2` and 7.394 MW for `AF-3` at their respective gross rates.

These values do not subtract directly from the 58.559 MW fire to predict extinction. Heat may be transferred from a protected surface rather than the flame, vaporization may alter mixing, efficiency may vary rapidly and open-loop water may change pool geometry. The screen sizes measurement and heat rejection.

### 4.4 Retention contradiction

Net retained water is:

\[
V_{ret}=\dot V_w(1-f_r)t,
\]

where (f_r) is the physically recovered recirculated fraction. `AF-1` has only 4 m³ of margin after fifteen minutes and fails the 40 m³ geometric screen after twenty minutes. `AF-2` and `AF-3` remain inside at thirty minutes.

This contradiction creates a real research question. The program must discover whether `AF-1` can terminate or reduce water earlier without increasing protected heat, whether `AF-2` can preserve loop separation under damage and whether `AF-3` can deploy reliably before fixed retention becomes inadequate.

## 11. Causal estimator and missing data

### 11.1 Hierarchy

For feature (Y), each accepted pair produces:

\[
\Delta Y_{r,s,c}=Y_{challenge,r,s,c}-Y_{control,r,s,c}.
\]

Both events must be valid. The two operational-site contrasts produce a root mean only after both are accepted; site spread remains visible. The two unlike-builder root means produce a candidate mean only after both are accepted; builder spread remains visible.

This hierarchy prevents a strong result at one site from hiding a weak result at the other and prevents one familiar builder from defining the architecture. Candidate selection remains conjunctive across all eight challenges and governing features.

### 11.2 Missing-data law

Twelve rules prohibit convenience reconstruction. Missing decisive source, water or heat functions are not imputed. An incomplete pool-depth field may support only a bounded area interval; if the interval crosses the decision limit, the event cannot pass. Source and water paths may not be combined into one volume. Summary products cannot replace both native originals. Post-event clock shifting cannot cure time skew. A command cannot prove challenge realization.

Robot telemetry may be bounded by an independent geometry witness only when robot state is diagnostic. If the robot action itself is decisive, missing telemetry prevents the required causal claim. A missing builder-proprietary signal does not penalize a candidate when the public functions remain sufficient.

### 11.3 Uncertainty

Each feature carries an expanded allocation and a physical acceptance field. Contributors include calibration, spatial representation, temporal alignment and transformation. Root-sum-square is the default only in the absence of registered covariance. A point result just inside a bound is not automatically a pass when its accepted interval crosses the limit.

## 12. Recovery and environmental custody

Recovery proceeds through source isolation, cessation of pool growth, known fire state, stable inventories, stable environmental boundary, limited-entry authorization, closed source/water/waste ledgers and restoration of the serialized root.

Human entry is not granted by a thirty-minute minimum alone. It also requires accepted thermal, gas, structural, route and medical conditions. Pumping, skimming or media handling before evidence release would change the ledger and may invalidate the event.

The final evidence packet contains frozen configuration; media and sample custody; initial and challenge histories; two native originals; command and intervention history; source–water–heat reconstruction; recovery and environmental disposition; and independent validity, outcome, minority and reserve decisions. An outside site must be able to reconstruct the event without builder narration.

## 16. Stocktake and next research obligation

### 16.1 What is now stronger

Root A now has the same basic institutional seriousness as Root B: unlike candidate architectures, root-native articles, an exact event population, matched challenges, public methods, causal estimation, missing-data law, recovery, evidence, workforce and finance. The pass also corrects a conceptual error: water is not outside the source system merely because it is applied for protection.

The broader force concept gains a reusable design rule. Disaster-response equipment should own the harmful secondary inventories created by rescue action. Firewater, medical waste, dredged sediment, contaminated floodwater, battery debris and desalination brine are mission outputs requiring capacity, custody and recovery—not externalities left to another institution.

### 16.2 What remains weak

The six public rigs are roles, not yet buildable physical specifications. The program has not fixed replaceable terrain geometry, source dispenser, ignition system, drainage interfaces, vault structure, closed-loop heat rejection, environmental perimeter, robot corridors, casualty capture, protected recorder locations or native bandwidth. Candidate parameters remain screening hypotheses. No physical evidence exists.

The three candidates also need sharper refusal criteria. `AF-1` must not solve thermal control by exporting water. `AF-2` must not conceal source below deck. `AF-3` must not substitute active recovery for rapid source termination.

### 16.3 Recommended next pass

Pass 124 should create `RFP-1`, the Root A full-scale fire/hydraulic public-rig physical specification. It should define a candidate-neutral source dispenser; modular grade and damaged-surface field; swappable drain, sump, cartridge and vault interfaces; controlled ignition and radiation boundary; open- and closed-loop water systems; environmental collection perimeter; known debris and fragment insertion; independent safety paths; robot corridors and denied-navigation cases; consequence and recovery geometry; 480-channel placement; native data rate; workforce; schedule and fiscal reconciliation.

The rig must accept all three architectures without embedding a fixed gravity, underdeck or robotic answer. It should be able to create known adverse grade, half area, partial fill, debris, bounded pool, retention shock, wind, barrier loss and service denial. Exterior rendering must remain blocked. Internal rig sections and measurement diagrams may proceed because they are required to make the experiment buildable.
