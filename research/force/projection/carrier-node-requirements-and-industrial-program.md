# Carrier–Node Requirements and the 25-Year Mobility Program

## The industrial scale of making canonical civil power arrive on time

### Research state

Pass 62 converts the [canonical cargo and transport compiler](canonical-cargo-and-transport-closure.md) into rival carrier and transfer-node requirement envelopes. It asks a narrower and more demanding question than “how much lift should the Department own?”: **what gross carrier potential, activation curve, node release capacity, route performance, reserve and lifecycle program would be required to place 95 percent of every cumulative cargo claim inside its 24-, 48- or 72-hour deadline?**

The accompanying [`carrier-node-requirement-and-program-model.xlsx`](../../../models/force/projection/carrier-node-requirement-and-program-model.xlsx) is the arithmetic authority. It imports the twelve mode–deadline demand buckets from Pass 61, preserves their 7.048-million-tonne chargeable burden, and sizes three rival architectures against the same service target and 20-percent follow-on reserve. It then translates the gross envelopes into four provisional carrier-group families, four transfer-node families, owned inventories, comparative capital, mature operations and a five-epoch 25-year program.

The model is deliberately ambitious and deliberately pre-acquisition. Carrier payloads, turns, technical availability, load factors, group costs, node rates, activation, route survival, release factors and RDT&E are declared design hypotheses. They do not come from vendor offers or existing fleets. “Platform” counts combine unlike aircraft, hulls, craft and land carrier-position equivalents and must never be treated as a common unit. No serial cargo, origin, route, crew, site, receiver or public effect is admitted. All fifteen evidence gates and the rendering gate remain open.

Pass 62 therefore establishes a **requirement and industrial-scale comparison**, not a preferred architecture or vehicle specification.

## Executive judgment

The strategic mobility problem is much larger than the 2.463-million-tonne inherited 72-hour capacity screen suggests. Capacity must be available in the right mode before the cargo deadline, survive node and route losses, preserve a second-event reserve, and release complete cargo positions into onward movement. When those requirements are made explicit, the Department needs between **10.048 million and 23.077 million tonnes of gross 72-hour carrier potential**, depending on how quickly and reliably the system activates.

The result produces three distinct futures:

| Architecture | Gross 72-hour requirement | Multiple of inherited capacity | Carrier groups | Transfer nodes | 25-year program |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `P1` scaled legacy pattern | 23.077M t | 9.37× | 1,119 | 367 | $9.693T |
| `P2` ready carrier–node force | 13.008M t | 5.28× | 652 | 312 | $6.013T |
| `P3` distributed high-activation network | 10.048M t | 4.08× | 517 | 296 | $5.222T |

All three are sized to the same notional 95-percent deadline target. Their difference is not a weaker public promise. It is the value of activation, distribution, node release and route survival. `P1` preserves the inherited activation pattern and compensates by buying enormous late capacity. `P2` assumes purpose-built ready carriers and nodes. `P3` assumes regional origins, more autonomous handling and still stronger early availability. The third case is the smallest declared envelope, but it is not a winner: its 93-percent node release and 94-percent route survival are unproved, and correlated infrastructure damage could erase the advantage.

The dominant requirement is surface movement at 48 hours. Even `P3` needs 5.749 million gross land/rail tonnes and 3.246 million gross ocean tonnes to satisfy cumulative 48-hour demand after reserve and loss factors. The air carrier envelope also binds at 48 hours, while the air-node inventory binds at 24 hours because the earliest 221,999-tonne chargeable claim must cross transfer surfaces immediately. This split matters. The fleet and node are different acquisition problems with different limiting times.

The cost screen is equally consequential. The `P3` comparison carries **$4.168 trillion of capital** and **$1.054 trillion of ramped 25-year operations**, for **$5.222 trillion** in real 2026-dollar program resources. The original $1 trillion ambition would cover about **19.2 percent** of this transport-and-node comparator before receiver systems, full formation acquisition or public-service infrastructure are added. That does not make the trillion-dollar frame trivial. It makes it a constitutional first installment rather than a mature-force budget.

Most importantly, Pass 62 shows that **activation speed is capital**. Reducing only `P3` air activation at 48 hours from 92 to 70 percent raises its gross system requirement from 10.048 to 10.225 million tonnes but increases the lumpy owned fleet and 25-year program from $5.222 trillion to **$5.966 trillion**. Roughly $744 billion is the modeled penalty for losing one readiness coefficient. A coequal Department must acquire no-notice activation, dispersed loading, maintenance currency and node readiness with the same seriousness that it acquires payload.

## 1. The requirement equation

### 1.1 From chargeable claim to gross fleet potential

For architecture `a`, mode `q` and deadline `d`, the model derives the gross 72-hour carrier potential required to satisfy the cumulative chargeable claim:

\[
G_{a,q,d}=\frac{\tau D_{q,d}}
{\alpha_{a,q,d}\,n_{a,q}\,r_{a,q}\,(1-\rho)},
\]

where:

- `D` is cumulative chargeable cargo demand by the deadline;
- `τ = 0.95` is the service target;
- `α` is the share of nominal 72-hour carrier potential activated by the deadline;
- `n` is the node release factor;
- `r` is route survival and completion;
- `ρ = 0.20` is the protected follow-on reserve; and
- `G` is the fleet's gross 72-hour potential before those losses.

The mode's required fleet envelope is the maximum across the three deadlines:

\[
G^{*}_{a,q}=\max_{d\in\{24,48,72\}}G_{a,q,d}.
\]

This equation prevents four forms of false closure.

First, it prevents late capacity from satisfying early service. Second, it prevents a carrier from receiving credit for cargo that the node cannot release. Third, it prevents a nominal route from being treated as a completed path. Fourth, it prevents the first campaign set from consuming the national follow-on reserve.

The equation is not a reliability estimate. Its factors are deterministic architecture hypotheses. A mature model will replace each coefficient with item, carrier, node, path, time and correlated-state records. For now, the method exposes the scale and direction of the design problem.

### 1.2 The rival architectures

`P1`, the scaled legacy pattern, retains Pass 61's inherited activation fractions: air reaches 35 percent at 24 hours and 70 percent at 48; ocean reaches only 5 and 35 percent; land and rail reach 20 and 60 percent; inland water reaches 10 and 45 percent. It adds a 75-percent node release factor and 85-percent route survival screen. This is not a claim about today's system. It is the cost of attempting to meet the new guarantee while leaving the activation shape substantially unchanged.

`P2`, the ready carrier–node force, raises activation through purpose-built design and standing readiness. Air reaches 55 percent at 24 hours and 85 percent at 48; ocean 15 and 60 percent; land 35 and 75 percent; inland water 20 and 65 percent. Node release rises to 88 percent and route survival to 90 percent. This architecture still assumes nationally concentrated strategic fleets but acquires the full carrier–node system rather than payload alone.

`P3`, the distributed high-activation network, treats geography and readiness as design variables. Regional origins, preconfigured cargo, autonomous handling, dispersed maintenance and damage-aware routing raise air activation to 70 and 92 percent; ocean to 30 and 75 percent; land to 50 and 85 percent; and inland water to 35 and 80 percent. Node release reaches 93 percent and route survival 94 percent. These values are technology and organizational targets. The case exists to identify what such performance would be worth, not to assume it into procurement.

All architectures reach 100 percent of nominal gross potential at 72 hours. That convention makes them comparable and also optimistic. A real overseas ocean movement may have no meaningful 72-hour arrival opportunity from a continental origin. A damaged rail network may continue losing capacity after activation. A future model must replace the generic horizon with origin–destination travel distributions.

### 1.3 Why cumulative deadlines govern

Pass 61 contains bucket demand and cumulative demand. Fleet capacity is sized against the cumulative requirement because early cargo consumes the same finite fleet and node capacity that later cargo needs. A system cannot satisfy 95 percent of the 24-hour bucket and then reuse the same capacity without respecting time, cycle and release.

The 48-hour surface demands dominate:

- air: 382,687 cumulative chargeable tonnes;
- ocean: 1,792,242 tonnes;
- land and rail: 3,597,190 tonnes; and
- inland water: 287,344 tonnes.

By 72 hours, the totals rise to 382,687, 2,188,436, 4,126,727 and 349,842 tonnes. The final totals are larger, but their full activation fraction often makes the earlier deadline more demanding in gross fleet terms. A platform program optimized for day-three payload could therefore fail the day-two civil requirement even when its total tonnage looks adequate.

## 2. What the envelopes reveal

### 2.1 Scaling a slow system is extraordinarily expensive

`P1` requires 1.182 million gross air tonnes, 9.539 million ocean tonnes, 11.168 million land/rail tonnes and 1.189 million inland-water tonnes. Ocean must reach **15.99 times** the inherited gross screen because only 35 percent of nominal potential activates by its binding 48-hour deadline and only 63.75 percent of that activated potential survives the node and route factors before reserve. Land/rail reaches 9.44 times inherited capacity. Air binds at 24 hours rather than 48 because its early activation is too shallow.

This is the industrial penalty of treating readiness as an operating detail. A force can compensate for low availability with excess inventory, but every idle or late carrier still requires capital, maintenance, bases, crews, spares and eventual replacement. The Department should therefore report **capacity-by-deadline**, not a single owned tonnage, and should price lost activation as a capital liability.

### 2.2 Purpose-built readiness halves the gross problem

`P2` reduces total gross requirement from 23.077 to 13.008 million tonnes. Its air envelope is 675,046 tonnes, ocean 4.479 million, land/rail 7.191 million and inland water 662,822. Better activation, node release and route completion remove roughly 10.1 million tonnes of required gross potential without reducing the cargo target or reserve.

That reduction is not free. The case carries $380 billion of declared development, and its performance would require new carriers, nodes, training, maintenance, digital configuration and access systems. Yet the comparison establishes the proper strategic trade: investment in readiness and system completion can avoid trillions of capital otherwise spent buying capacity that arrives too late or remains trapped at a node.

### 2.3 Distribution lowers the envelope further

`P3` reduces the total to 10.048 million gross tonnes: 565,040 air, 3.246 million ocean, 5.749 million land/rail and 487,904 inland-water. Its inland-water requirement is below the inherited 522,450-tonne gross screen, although a different activation and node architecture is still required. This is the only mode where current aggregate quantity might be sufficient while readiness and geography are not.

Distribution helps by shortening activation and increasing the probability that a usable origin, node and route exists near a campaign. It could include permanently packed regional formations, forward maritime stations, cargo-ready aircraft detachments, assigned rail and road groups, inland craft stored near interoperable launch systems, and prepared nodes with exercised civil authority.

But geographic distribution creates its own burdens: more bases and depots, duplicated technical teams, corrosion and storage control, local political relationships, inventory rotation, security, configuration drift and exposure to the same regional hazard. `P3` can be admitted only if those tails are counted and if distributed sites remain sufficiently independent. The model's 25-percent base-and-depot capital tail is a coarse placeholder, not closure.

### 2.4 Reserve is not optional surplus

Removing the 20-percent follow-on reserve reduces `P3` gross requirement from 10.048 to 8.038 million tonnes and its 25-year program from $5.222 to **$4.292 trillion**. The apparent saving is $930 billion.

That is not a free efficiency. It is the value of the second-event claim being deleted. A mature force should show this number publicly: a decision to consume reserve converts a financial saving into a precise loss of national capacity under a follow-on catastrophe. Reserve calibration must be based on joint hazard, geography, activation, repair and public consequence—not an optimizer's desire to improve first-event utilization.

## 3. Provisional carrier-group system

### 3.1 Why group equivalents are used

The model does not invent four final vehicles. It introduces four **carrier-group equivalents** so the industrial consequences of the capacity envelopes become visible. Each group includes an operating cohort and the immediate handling or command tail necessary to generate a 72-hour gross yield. The unit is still incomplete: base, depot, node, route, receiver and full crew generation are priced elsewhere or remain open.

The group yield is:

\[
Y_q = u_q p_q c_q a_q l_q,
\]

where `u` is platforms per group, `p` payload per platform-turn, `c` 72-hour turns, `a` technical availability and `l` load factor. Required groups are the smallest whole number whose yield meets the gross envelope.

| Mode | Provisional group | Platforms | Payload per platform-turn | 72-hour turns | Availability | Load factor | Group yield |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Air | Strategic resilience air group | 12 | 180 t | 2.8 | 78% | 88% | 4,151 t |
| Ocean | High-readiness self-discharge squadron | 3 | 20,000 t | 1.0 | 78% | 90% | 42,120 t |
| Land/rail | Continental mobility brigade | 300 position-equivalents | 40 t | 2.5 | 82% | 90% | 22,140 t |
| Inland water | Shallow-water distribution group | 12 | 800 t | 1.8 | 80% | 85% | 11,750 t |

These values are targets chosen to make the requirement falsifiable. The air row does not select propulsion, fuselage, range or runway requirement. The ocean row does not select monohull, multihull, nuclear or conventional power. The land row combines road and rail position-equivalents that must be separated in a later route model. The inland row does not determine draft, beam, lock compatibility or inter-basin transport.

### 3.2 `P3` fleet scale

The distributed rival implies:

| Mode | Owned groups | Physical platform or position equivalents | Procurement screen |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Air | 137 | 1,644 aircraft | $1.151T |
| Ocean | 78 | 234 hulls | $374.4B |
| Land/rail | 260 | 78,000 carrier-position equivalents | $195.0B |
| Inland water | 42 | 504 craft | $37.8B |
| **Total** | **517** | **80,382 unlike units** | **$1.758T** |

The total platform count is dominated by the land row and has no cross-mode engineering meaning. Its value is institutional: the Department is not buying a small elite fleet. It is establishing a mass mobility economy with thousands of aircraft and craft, hundreds of ships and tens of thousands of surface carrier positions, all tied to common cargo and node interfaces.

The air result is particularly demanding. A 1,644-aircraft purpose-built heavy fleet would exceed the scale of an ordinary specialty service. It would require multiple production lines, dispersed bases, engine and structural depots, simulator and crew pipelines, cargo automation and a standing mission-command system. If further work shows that such an air burden is industrially or energetically irrational, the answer is not to erase the cargo. The force must shift the service design through preparation, modularity, alternate modes, slower accepted deadlines or a narrower guarantee and record the consequence.

The ocean result similarly implies a fleet, not a handful of iconic response ships. Its 234 hulls would need differentiated classes for self-discharge, rolling cargo, heavy works, cold and hazardous zones, outsize movement, lighterage, port opening and sustainment. Common mission interfaces can unify the family; a universal ship should not.

### 3.3 Clean-sheet air requirements

The air family should be competed on complete positions released by time, not nominal maximum payload. Its requirement set includes:

- no-notice activation and dispersed alert maintenance;
- high-rate pallet, powered-cold, battery and bounded-outsize handling;
- organic or paired loading systems when commercial equipment is unavailable;
- austere runway and pavement compatibility, or an explicit airfield-opening package;
- rapid reconfiguration without losing restraint, fire separation or center-of-gravity evidence;
- disconnected cargo identity, load health and receiver instructions;
- maintainable propulsion and structures at sustained disaster-cycle tempo;
- crew generation, relief and safe automation; and
- a clear division between strategic trunk, short-field and vertical-access families.

Novel technology should compete where it changes the equation. Autonomous ground handling can increase node release. Optionally crewed ferry and cargo operations may improve sustained cycle rates. New structures or propulsion may improve payload and availability. Distributed additive repair could reduce depot return. None earns credit if it shifts labor, energy or safety burdens to the receiver.

### 3.4 Clean-sheet ocean requirements

The ocean family should maximize useful early arrival and no-pier release. Requirements include:

- high readiness at dispersed regional stations;
- self-discharge across intact berth, damaged berth, lighterage and temporary shore interfaces;
- separated rolling, flatrack, tank, cold, battery, long-load and outsize zones;
- cranes and ramps whose failure does not immobilize the complete package;
- cargo power, monitoring, fire isolation and environmental custody;
- shallow-access companions rather than forcing the strategic hull into every littoral geometry;
- repair, tow, salvage and port-opening support; and
- crew habitability and maintenance for prolonged stabilization campaigns.

Nuclear propulsion may be valuable for some water–energy or sustained-support platforms, but the carrier requirement cannot assume it. A reactor does not make a ship arrive by 48 hours, unload without a port, or connect cargo to a receiver. Nuclear and nonnuclear rivals should face the same position, node, safety, lifecycle and availability trials.

### 3.5 Clean-sheet land and rail requirements

The land/rail envelope is the largest. Its provisional 78,000 `P3` position-equivalents should become a portfolio, not one truck design:

- optionally crewed road-train families for sustained high-rate line haul;
- disaster rail consists with independent power, communications and rapid formation loading;
- common powered trailers and rail platforms accepting governed cargo positions;
- bridge-, axle-, clearance- and pavement-aware route control;
- rapid road/rail transload with minimal bespoke equipment;
- convoy maintenance, recovery, fuel or electric-energy support and crew relief;
- autonomous inspection and localized repair of routes; and
- public-road operating modes that preserve evacuation, commerce and emergency access.

Road and rail must separate in the next model. A rail tonne requires a train path, track class, control system, siding and transload. A road tonne requires a legal axle path, driver or autonomy authority, fuel or charging and road release. Treating both as one capacity hides the most important infrastructure choices.

### 3.6 Clean-sheet inland-water requirements

The inland force is a continental access system. It should include shallow-draft carriers, modular barges, tow and recovery craft, road/rail-deployable small systems, bank-transfer nodes and rapid mooring. Its design must account for current, debris, locks, bridge clearances, bank strength, contamination and the inability to move between watersheds without land transport.

The apparent aggregate sufficiency in `P3` should be used as a proving hypothesis: can the Department transform existing waterways into reliable distribution paths by investing in activation, terminals and interfaces rather than simply adding hull tonnage? Only a geographically explicit network can answer.

## 4. Transfer nodes as major platforms

### 4.1 Node inventory

The model sizes four node families against the cumulative deadline demand, technical availability, architecture release factor and reserve. In `P3` it requires:

| Node family | Rated throughput | Technical availability | Owned nodes | Binding deadline | Capital screen |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Austere air entry | 300 t/h | 85% | 47 | 24 h | $56.4B |
| No-pier maritime transfer | 600 t/h | 82% | 97 | 48 h | $232.8B |
| Road–rail intermodal release | 1,000 t/h | 85% | 113 | 48 h | $84.8B |
| Inland-water bank transfer | 250 t/h | 80% | 39 | 48 h | $19.5B |
| **Total** | — | — | **296** | — | **$393.45B** |

The air carrier fleet binds at 48 hours, but the air-node count binds at 24. That means more aircraft alone cannot close the first-day claim; the force needs many parallel surfaces capable of receiving, inspecting, handling and releasing mixed cargo immediately. Ocean node capital exceeds its carrier-node siblings because no-pier heavy transfer is treated as a complete specialized system.

### 4.2 What a node owns

A transfer node is not a parking area. It owns:

1. damage and surface inspection;
2. traffic sequencing and safe separation;
3. cargo identity and receiver compatibility checks;
4. cold, battery, fuel, process and hazardous-energy zones;
5. heavy, rolling, long-load and outsize handling;
6. temporary power, lighting, communications, fire protection and weather control;
7. queue, dwell and protected storage;
8. onward carrier assignment and route release;
9. workforce shift, relief, shelter and clinical support;
10. environmental and waste custody; and
11. a lawful handoff record to the next carrier or receiver.

Clean-sheet nodes should be modular but not imaginary. High-rate robotic handling, autonomous dollies, self-leveling heavy surfaces, rapidly deployed cranes, machine-readable restraint and universal power/fluid/data adapters could change the release factor. Their complete support systems—energy, maintenance, safety zones, manual fallback and cyber isolation—must be counted.

### 4.3 Node creation as force projection

The Department should possess the ability to create a node where no node survived. Airfield-opening, no-pier port, road–rail transload and bank-transfer formations should be standing units with equipment, trained authorities and exercised sites. Prepared domestic and allied interfaces should be maintained during ordinary years, while expeditionary variants create temporary access after catastrophe.

This capability is a visible form of national power. A country able to arrive with a working port, cold chain, energy pad, fluid manifold, heavy apron and onward distribution command can stabilize a partner without assuming functioning host logistics. It also creates exportable standards and industrial demand beyond the carrier itself.

## 5. Twenty-five-year industrial program

### 5.1 Capital constitution

The `P3` comparative capital is:

| Element | Real 2026 $B |
|---|---:|
| Carrier procurement | 1,758.0 |
| Transfer nodes | 393.5 |
| RDT&E | 520.0 |
| Bases and depots | 537.9 |
| Initial spares and training sets | 263.7 |
| Pre-reserve capital | 3,473.0 |
| Program reserve | 694.6 |
| **Total capital** | **4,167.6** |

Carrier and node costs alone do not constitute the program. Bases, depots, spares, training sets, configuration systems, test facilities and industrial development must be visible. The 20-percent program reserve is separate from the 20-percent operational follow-on reserve: one protects acquisition uncertainty, the other protects national readiness.

Mature annual operations screen at $116.5 billion: $80.7 billion for carriers, $19.7 billion for nodes and $16.1 billion for bases and depots. The declared ramp produces 9.05 full-inventory-equivalent operating years over the first twenty-five years, or $1.054 trillion. Total capital and ramped operations reach $5.222 trillion. Personnel benefits, incident consumables, receiver systems and the wider force may increase that amount; the model should not be read as a Department total.

### 5.2 Five epochs

The `P3` path allocates the program as follows:

| Epoch | Central purpose | Epoch resources |
|---|---|---:|
| Years 1–5 | Constitution, interfaces, test infrastructure, bases, first nodes and 3% fleet | $634.0B |
| Years 6–10 | Rival prototypes, production qualification, 12% fleet and 28% nodes | $856.6B |
| Years 11–15 | National scaling, 25% fleet and 28% nodes | $1.123T |
| Years 16–20 | Maximum production and operating overlap, 33% fleet | $1.352T |
| Years 21–25 | Final depth, replacement learning and 27% fleet | $1.248T |
| **Total** | — | **$5.222T** |

Full authorization does not remove the industrial learning problem. The early program must build design bureaus, independent test, suppliers, schools, depots, tooling and common interfaces before peak production. The heaviest combined capital and operating burden occurs in years 16–20, when the fleet is already active while production remains near its maximum.

The original $1 trillion could fund the first epoch and a portion of the second, or a different deliberately bounded capability tranche. It cannot truthfully be labeled a complete 25-year strategic mobility force under these assumptions. The mature fiscal constitution should separate RDT&E, formation and infrastructure capital, standing operations, industrial reserve and incident employment so annual disasters do not consume modernization.

### 5.3 The resilience mobility industrial base

The program would require a deliberately created civil industrial base:

- at least two competing large-aircraft design and integration lines plus propulsion, structures, avionics and cargo-system suppliers;
- multiple shipyards capable of series construction, repair and common-module integration rather than episodic prestige hulls;
- a national road/rail production and conversion ecosystem for carrier platforms, powered trailers, rail consists, transload and control systems;
- inland-craft yards and distributed repair capacity;
- heavy-handling, crane, surface, cold-chain, battery-safety and fluid-interface industries;
- digital configuration, scheduling and evidence systems that remain interoperable and operable offline;
- technical schools and licensed professions sized for operations, maintenance, inspection and regeneration; and
- public fallback tooling and data rights so a failed prime cannot immobilize a national service.

Stable procurement should be paired with commercial and allied use where compatible, but commercial utilization cannot replace cold readiness. The Department pays for activation evidence, maintained tooling, surge labor and reserved production because those are strategic outputs.

### 5.4 Export and alliance opportunity

The most valuable export may be the carrier–node interface ecosystem rather than a flagship vehicle. Allies, states and cities could purchase different scales of compatible cargo positions, nodes, carriers, software and training. A cold module built by one supplier should be inspectable at another country's node and movable by several carriers without surrendering safety or sovereignty.

Partner capacity should be credited only under explicit availability, authority and domestic-reserve terms. Interoperability expands the graph; it does not allow the United States to count another country's asset as its own. Joint exercises should prove cargo identity, hazardous release, customs and border authority, node operation, public priority, receiver transfer and regeneration.

## 6. Authority and command

### 6.1 Strategic Mobility Command

Strategic Mobility Command should own fleet readiness, activation, carrier assignment and the common movement picture. It should maintain the gross-potential and deadline ledgers, operate purpose-built national fleets, certify contracted capacity and recommend reserve movements. It should not change formation configuration to make loads easier or decide public priority by itself.

Generating services own serial cargo and complete-package readiness. Entry and distribution commands own node creation, transfer and onward movement. Receiving commands and civil authorities admit commissioned cargo into public service. Technical authorities retain safety release. The Joint Capacity Authority arbitrates conflicts and protects reserve. Independent RTE authorities reproduce performance and cost.

### 6.2 Activation as an acquisition key performance parameter

Every carrier and node program should carry activation commitments at 24, 48 and 72 hours, not one nominal availability percentage. Those commitments should include:

- alert and decision latency;
- crew and maintainer arrival;
- cargo readiness and configuration validation;
- loading and departure queues;
- weather and damage effects;
- travel and transfer time;
- node opening and release; and
- the remaining ready reserve.

Payments, readiness reporting and production decisions should reflect demonstrated activation. A platform delivered to inventory but unable to generate its required early curve has not met the acquisition contract.

### 6.3 Denied service and democratic control

Scarce mobility determines which populations receive water, power, cooling, medical support and safe movement first. That allocation cannot be hidden inside fleet scheduling. Civil leadership must publish priority floors, rights constraints and appeals; the Joint Capacity Authority must show which complete formation or service is denied when capacity is reallocated. Technical release cannot be overridden by a numerical priority score.

## 7. Technology competitions

The model creates a portfolio of competitions whose value can be measured against reduced gross requirement, node inventory, program cost and public-service time.

### 7.1 No-notice activation system

Compete predictive maintenance, autonomous inspections, distributed crew readiness, prevalidated load plans, robot loading and cargo-ready regional packages. The objective is not an impressive individual demonstration; it is a statistically defensible shift in the 24- and 48-hour activation distribution without increased hidden failure or workforce burden.

### 7.2 High-rate mixed-cargo node

Compete rapid surfaces, cranes, autonomous dollies, cold and battery zones, machine-readable cargo, outsize handling and disconnected traffic control. Measure complete positions safely released per hour under concurrent arrivals, damaged surfaces and manual fallback.

### 7.3 Distributed readiness network

Compete concentrated fleets with regional basing, afloat or inland prepositioning, modular depots and allied access. Measure joint hazard covariance, activation, cost, configuration drift and regeneration. `P3` should fail if its many locations share power, workforce, supplier, digital or weather common modes.

### 7.4 Shape and interface engineering

Compete foldable low-density systems, standardized powered positions, modular outsize decomposition and universal utility adapters. The objective is lower chargeable demand and faster receiver commissioning without moving assembly labor or safety risk downstream.

### 7.5 Damage-aware routing and recomposition

Compete sensing, graph inference and explainable scheduling that reroute exact cargo positions as networks fail. Systems must function with stale data and disconnected nodes, preserve protected reserve, reject incompatible modes and expose every substitution. Mission-aware recomposition may combine surviving positions into a bounded alternate service only when a validated degradation rule exists.

### 7.6 Regeneration technology

Compete rapid inspection, modular repair, corrosion and contamination control, battery recovery, engine or propulsion exchange, digital work packages and distributed spares. The measure is time to verified next-event readiness, not time to leave the incident.

## 8. What is not yet known

The model's precision should not be confused with evidence maturity.

There is still no nationally serial cargo manifest. The ten position classes are an external grammar built from profiles. There is no observed distribution of origins or travel times. Road and rail are combined. Ocean movement assumes a 72-hour opportunity that may be impossible for many origins. Carrier groups omit detailed range, energy, crew, weather, maintenance and depot constraints. Node rates omit discrete queues, incompatible simultaneous cargo, surface construction and public traffic. Route survival is not fitted to any campaign. Costs are declared comparisons without learning curves, inflation, schedule risk or independent estimates. Receiver capacity remains zero.

The 95-percent target is applied to cumulative chargeable demand, not to serial complete formations. Passing the envelope by construction therefore does not overturn Pass 61's 13-percent formation closure; it identifies the carrier potential that would be required before a serial allocation trial. Nor does it establish the public effect. A position released from a node still must reach, connect, commission and operate at a lawful receiver.

The `P3` cost advantage is particularly conditional. Its high activation, node release and route survival may require capital not fully represented by the base/depot factor. Its distributed sites may be correlated. Its autonomy may create certification, cyber and maintenance costs. A later model may find `P2` more robust or require a hybrid by mode.

## 9. Decisions and next proof

Pass 62 supports six decisions.

First, future mobility programs must report gross potential by 24-, 48- and 72-hour activation, not owned payload alone. Second, node release and route completion belong in the capacity equation. Third, transfer nodes are major platforms and acquisition programs. Fourth, the Department should carry at least three rival architecture families until representative trials establish the value of distribution and activation. Fifth, the original $1 trillion frame is a partial investment under every declared architecture, not a complete program. Sixth, no carrier or node form may be rendered from these group equivalents.

The next proof should return to the item boundary that Pass 61 exposed. It should select one information-rich formation—preferably an engineering, utility or theater-opening family with pallet, container, battery, tank, flatrack, rolling, long-load, bulk and outsize positions—and construct a **serial manifest protocol** from its service contract. Every item must receive mass, cube, geometry, restraint, hazard, power, handling, origin, complement, carrier and receiver identity. Unknown fields must remain visibly unquantified rather than filled by expert intuition.

That manifest should then face two rival carrier–node systems in a discrete schedule with seeded carrier, node and route losses. The trial must report complete formation release, not average tonnes.
