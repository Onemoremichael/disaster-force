# `GCH-340R` complete-system and transient closure

## Purpose and decision

Pass 83 established the first useful atmospheric-carrier hypothesis for `DCP-2`: two vehicles, each carrying 317.5 tonnes of assigned service cargo across a 4,000-kilometre standoff, could provide a formation-scale alternative to four terrain carriers. That pass was intentionally shallow. It reserved 663 tonnes for an empty vehicle, 1,575 megawatt-hours for onboard energy, sixty square metres for contact and an unspecified sixty-tonne variable-buoyancy system. It did not yet force structure, containment, propulsion, heat rejection, landing, mooring, unloading, crew survival and recovery into one mass and spatial account.

Pass 87 does. The earlier 1,003-tonne `GCH-340` does not survive.

The selected successor is **`GCH-340R`**, a 300-metre cellular hydrogen-conversion lifting body. It has twenty-four isolated lift cells arranged in six longitudinal bays, six lift-gas conversion trains, six energy buses, twelve vectoring propulsion pods, sixteen distributed contact feet, two serial cargo keels, two occupied refuges and four independent exits. It is not a conventional airship with a cargo hold. It is a flying service plant that deliberately combines partial static lift with aerodynamic lift, then changes its own support state during unloading.

The complete system has **918.000 tonnes of fixed non-fuel mass**, **131.236 tonnes of mission fuel**, **72.180 tonnes of mission-fuel tankage**, **340 tonnes of rated payload** and a **1,461.415-tonne rated departure gross**. A closed mass–energy loop produces **2,185.075 megawatt-hours of deliverable onboard energy**. In the minimum-lift hot-high atmosphere, 862.600 tonnes of static lift leave 598.815 tonnes for aerodynamic support at rated departure. The declared 675-tonne dynamic-lift capacity leaves a 5.21-percent total-lift reserve and a 40.243-tonne margin after one lift cell is isolated.

This heavier and more complete vehicle reverses the unloading problem. Hot-high air is not the governing offload atmosphere. After fuel burn and full cargo removal, the carrier remains positively coupled to the ground in hot-high conditions. In cold-dense air, however, the same empty-arrival vehicle is 65.104 tonnes lighter than its static lift. To finish unloading while retaining 120 tonnes of controlled ground reaction, it must change effective support by 185.104 tonnes.

The provisional `GCH-340R` answer is deliberately ambitious: convert selected hydrogen lift gas with atmospheric oxygen into contained water. Each tonne of hydrogen converted removes its net lifting contribution and captures eight tonnes of oxygen mass. Under the declared densities, one tonne of hydrogen therefore changes vehicle support by 20.611 tonnes and produces nine tonnes of water. A complete cold-dense unload requires **8.981 tonnes of lift hydrogen conversion** and produces **80.827 tonnes of contained water**. Five of six trains can complete the conversion in 0.998 hour, inside the 1.25-hour placement-and-transfer window, while producing 149.530 megawatt-hours of electrical energy.

This is not a claim that such a system is mature, safe or even ultimately preferable. It is a clean-sheet acquisition target whose value is that it makes the hidden variable-support requirement physical, measurable and falsifiable. A program that cannot build, isolate, cool, control and recover this system does not possess the modeled aircraft.

The correction propagates well beyond the airframe. Landing contact grows from sixty to 160 square metres. No-anchor station keeping requires 5.955 meganewtons of combined N-2 vector thrust and controlled friction against a 5.631-meganewton factored broadside gust. Structural reset becomes the 162-hour critical path. The atmospheric fleet grows from 112 to 118 total articles. Complete atmospheric specialties replace the shallow workforce allowance, revising `DCP-2` to 758 deployed people, 1,699 affiliated positions and a 244-person minimum watch.

The twenty-five-year `GCH-340R` research comparator is **$1.7297432 trillion**, $797.48575 billion above the superseded atmospheric account. With the unchanged `SAT-170R` and federated receiver accounts, `DCP-2` becomes **$3.7773011 trillion** and the present national boundary becomes **$11.16044265 trillion**. These are constant-dollar architecture comparators, not appropriation requests or benefit-cost conclusions. A nested $600 billion technology and proving portfolio makes the industrial burden visible rather than additive.

The companion [executable workbook](../../../models/force/projection/gch340-complete-system-and-transient-closure.xlsx) contains nineteen sheets covering configuration competition, lift-cell geometry, coupled mass and energy, heat rejection, offload transients, landing, mooring, payload balance, crew casualty states, receiver and mission clocks, reset, workforce, production, cost, technology campaigns and 150 evidence gates. Sixty model checks and 150 evidence-state controls produce a 210-of-210 terminal audit; the formula-error scan is empty. Every representative evidence gate remains open. Strict readiness credit is zero.

Exterior renderings remain blocked. Pass 87 supplies a behavior reference and a proving order, not an industrial design.

## 1. The aircraft is a controlled support-state machine

The wrong mental model is a very large cargo aircraft that happens to obtain part of its lift from gas. That description treats cruise as the mission and landing as an endpoint. `GCH-340R` instead performs four distinct physical acts:

1. it accepts two complete service blocks at a governed origin;
2. it moves them through a 4,000-kilometre atmospheric route without a host grid;
3. it arrests on a weak, unprepared receiver while resisting gust and local support loss; and
4. it removes 317.5 tonnes of payload while actively changing lift, mass, heat and ground reaction.

The last act determines the architecture. Cargo removal changes vehicle mass much faster than conventional fuel burn. Atmospheric density changes static lift independently. Gust resistance partly depends on ground reaction, while ground bearing limits the same reaction. Propulsion used for station keeping is also the source of aerodynamic control and placement power. The conversion process that reduces lift is an energy source and a heat source. Water created during conversion is useful ballast but also a sloshing, structural, containment and recovery burden.

No subsystem can therefore be optimized in isolation. The carrier must be treated as a **controlled support-state machine** whose flight, contact and public-service states are explicitly connected.

The governing design rule is:

> At every mission state, vehicle mass, static lift, aerodynamic lift, ground reaction, vector thrust, energy, heat rejection, cargo restraint, occupied refuge, failure boundary and civil release authority must be simultaneously defined.

A vehicle that can cruise but cannot unload in cold air is not a carrier. A vehicle that can unload only by borrowing receiver ballast is not an independent access mode. A vehicle that uses prepared anchors to survive the declared gust has converted a vehicle requirement into unregistered local infrastructure. A vehicle whose lift correction cannot stop safely after a payload actuator fault is a chemical plant with no valid transfer state.

## 2. Why the predecessor fails

The Pass 83 vehicle carried three simplifying assumptions that no longer hold.

First, its 663-tonne empty mass omitted the full consequence of energy containment, protected distribution, multi-cell structure, contact, heat rejection, crew refuge, environmental protection and field recovery. Once those functions receive even provisional mass relationships, fixed non-fuel mass rises to 918 tonnes before mission-fuel tankage.

Second, its 1,575-megawatt-hour energy allowance was not solved against the mass it created. Energy increased fuel and tank mass; added gross increased dynamic-lift demand; dynamic lift increased propulsion energy; the additional energy then increased fuel again. The correct answer requires a fixed point, not a larger allowance.

Third, the earlier offload discussion focused on hot-high lift scarcity. That is the obvious flight case but not necessarily the worst unloading case. The complete mass account shows that an empty carrier remains heavy enough to retain contact in hot-high air. Cold-dense static lift is 1,078.250 tonnes, while empty arrival mass is only 1,013.146 tonnes. The vehicle wants to rise precisely when the mission requires stable ground contact.

This is a general warning for a Department of Resilience. Dramatic environmental conditions do not always govern system design. A response platform can be sized by the state that looks operationally benign but creates the worst physical mismatch after mission actions change its mass, heat or geometry.

## 3. Competing complete arrangements

Four complete-system arrangements face twenty-two non-substitutable gates. Pass counts are diagnostic, not weighted scores.

| Reference | Arrangement | Gates passed | Governing failure |
|---|---|---:|---|
| `C1` | helium compression spine | 16/22 | compression mass, stationary heat, mooring and reset |
| `C2` | aerodynamic-dominant low-static-lift body | 15/22 | one-cell loss, energy, slow flight and mission clock |
| `C3` | fixed service-ballast hybrid | 18/22 | energy mass, field removal and reset |
| `C4` | cellular hydrogen-conversion hybrid | 22/22 | selected behavior reference; all evidence remains open |

`C1` tries to preserve inert helium while mechanically varying its occupied volume. The concept is attractive because it avoids consuming lift gas, but the compression vessels and machinery become an aircraft-sized high-pressure and thermal system. In the model it cannot close cold-dense unloading, stationary heat, N-2 mooring or the 168-hour reset boundary together. It remains a valuable rival lineage: if materials or adsorption systems transform the pressure-mass relationship, it could displace the selected concept.

`C2` reduces static lift enough that the unloaded aircraft naturally stays on the ground. It solves one transition by forcing the vehicle to fly much more like an airplane. Slow-flight control, one-cell isolation, propulsion loss, energy mass and the 42-hour relative service clock then fail together. It also concentrates public safety in active power during precisely the approach and transfer phases in which smoke, weather, debris and ground interaction are most severe.

`C3` carries enough fixed or mission ballast to maintain contact. That is physically legible but economically destructive. Every tonne of ballast is moved 4,000 kilometres, increases aerodynamic lift and energy, consumes volume and enlarges landing loads. The solution becomes self-defeating once energy and tankage are allowed to respond to gross mass. It also turns post-mission ballast disposal, field removal and reset into central requirements.

`C4` couples a cellular lifting body to a dual-use hydrogen system. It wins only at the declared behavior level because it places the variable-support function inside a mass flow the carrier already owns. Mission hydrogen and lift hydrogen remain separately inventoried and contained, yet share qualified conversion principles, oxygen handling, water management and energy controls. Twenty-four cells limit the lift increment of one tear; six conversion trains limit the consequence of one failed process lane; six energy buses and twelve pods preserve N-2 propulsion.

The decision is not “hydrogen is best.” The decision is that variable support must be treated as an aircraft-scale primary system and that `C4` is the strongest present object for destructive comparison.

## 4. The provisional physical grammar

The external requirement envelope is 300 metres long, ninety-six metres wide and fifty-six metres high. A 0.59 bounding-box fill factor accommodates 950,000 cubic metres of lift volume in a multi-lobe form. The 420-metre-square receiver leaves 120 metres of longitudinal and 324 metres of lateral envelope margin before approach, exhaust, public exclusion and recovery clearances are applied.

Six fifty-metre longitudinal bays each contain four lateral lift cells. A single cell therefore holds about 39,583 cubic metres and contributes approximately 35.94 tonnes of hot-high lift or 44.93 tonnes of cold-dense lift. Cell walls, valves, sensing and pressure relief must make that arithmetic real: a tear or fire that propagates beyond one cell invalidates the casualty model.

The selected spatial grammar has eight longitudinal zones:

| Zone | Extent | Principal contents | Required separation |
|---|---:|---|---|
| nose and recovery | 0–32 m | crush structure, recovery, weather sensing | tanks do not reach impact face |
| forward machinery | 32–78 m | energy citadels `E1–E3`, thermal panels, pod drives | three fire and fluid domains |
| forward refuge | 78–98 m | eight-person flight refuge, exits | egress avoids machinery |
| cargo keel A | 98–150 m | load block one, wet pack one, bilateral robots | early-wave bilateral access |
| cargo keel B | 150–202 m | load block two, wet pack two, bilateral robots | no restrained load crosses the split |
| aft refuge | 202–222 m | mission refuge and remote safe-state control | one-refuge safe hold |
| aft machinery | 222–268 m | energy citadels `E4–E6`, conversion and thermal plant | no common drain, bus or coolant loop |
| tail and recovery | 268–300 m | recovery and control surfaces | removal survives one blocked end |

The cargo system contains sixteen dry positions and two segregated wet positions. Two eight-position blocks remain separately identifiable but jointly governed as one atmospheric service load. Positions alternate port and starboard exits so that a blocked side slows recovery without making a module inaccessible. Cargo mass, cube and deck close at 317.5 tonnes, 1,354 cubic metres and 312 square metres, leaving reserves against the registered 340-tonne, 1,400-cubic-metre and 352-square-metre limits.

This is not an exterior design. It is an allocation of obligations. Each zone must later acquire real frames, membranes, ducts, pipes, cable bends, fire barriers, inspection paths, actuators, pressure-relief directions, maintenance access and crash load paths. Those details may change the envelope or kill the concept.

## 5. Coupled mass–energy closure

The complete fixed non-fuel mass is assembled from thirteen provisional packets:

| Packet | Mass |
|---|---:|
| primary load lattice and envelope | 275.000 t |
| lift cells, ballonets and valves | 45.000 t |
| propulsion and protected distribution | 145.000 t |
| energy-conversion hardware | 75.000 t |
| flight controls and sensors | 32.000 t |
| cargo keel, restraint and robots | 50.000 t |
| contact, mooring and leveling | 95.000 t |
| lift-gas conversion trains | 60.000 t |
| captured-water tanks | 28.000 t |
| heat-rejection surfaces | 32.000 t |
| crew refuges and life support | 24.000 t |
| recovery, fire, lightning and icing | 37.000 t |
| integration reserve | 20.000 t |
| **fixed non-fuel mass** | **918.000 t** |

Every line is a research hypothesis. Their purpose is not to imply design confidence but to prevent invisible systems from weighing zero.

Let `M₀` be fixed mass before mission fuel and tankage, `P₀` be gross-independent cruise and mission power, `q` be the lift-power coefficient per tonne of dynamic lift, `T` be cruise duration, `Eₓ` be placement and transition energy, `r` be energy reserve, `s` be usable fuel energy and `k` be tank mass per tonne of fuel. With hot-high static lift `Lₛ`, mission fuel `F` increases gross and therefore the dynamic-lift power term. The closed form is:

\[
F=\frac{(1+r)\left[T\left(P_0+q(M_0+(1+k)F+M_p-L_s)\right)+E_x\right]}{s}.
\]

Collecting `F` yields a feedback coefficient of 0.131406, safely below one but large enough to matter. The solution is 131.236 tonnes of mission fuel and 72.180 tonnes of tankage. Rated departure gross is 1,461.415 tonnes; the assigned service load produces 1,438.915 tonnes.

At 240 kilometres per hour in the declared hot-high density, a 4,300-square-metre frontal reference and 0.055 hull drag coefficient produce 44.021 megawatts of parasite power. Aerodynamic support adds 41.840 megawatts at rated departure, and hotel and mission loads add ten megawatts. Cruise demand is therefore 95.861 megawatts. The 192-megawatt installed system leaves N-2 capacity after two sixteen-megawatt pods are unavailable.

Mission energy before reserve is 1,802.687 megawatt-hours. The 17.5-percent deliverable reserve raises onboard energy to 2,185.075 megawatt-hours. Nominal arrival retains 22.966 tonnes of reserve fuel. These figures are substantially larger than the predecessor because fuel, containment and dynamic-lift energy now live in the same equation.

The small service-clock margin makes aerodynamic efficiency a strategic technology variable. A five-percent cruise-time penalty alone increases relative service from 41.917 to 42.794 hours and misses the air ceiling. The program therefore cannot “make up” poor lift-to-drag ratio with more fuel without also reopening mass, power and time.

## 6. The cold-dense offload transient

The key state is empty arrival, not takeoff. After nominal fuel burn, the carrier retains tankage and reserve fuel for a mass of 1,013.146 tonnes. Cold-dense static lift is 1,078.250 tonnes, creating 65.104 tonnes of uncontrolled upward force. The mission requires 120 tonnes of final ground reaction for positive contact and station keeping, so the vehicle must create 185.104 tonnes of support change.

The modeled mechanism removes hydrogen from selected cells, surrenders the corresponding displaced volume through an isolated air-filled ballonet or controlled cell contraction, reacts the hydrogen outside the lift cell and stores the product water in the lower keel. Merely burning hydrogen while preserving the same sealed displaced volume would not remove the required buoyancy and receives no credit. For one tonne of hydrogen lift gas converted under the declared volume-replacement behavior:

\[
\Delta S = \frac{\rho_{air}-\rho_{H_2}}{\rho_{H_2}} + 8
= 12.611 + 8
= 20.611\ \text{tonnes of support change}.
\]

The first term removes the net buoyant contribution of the hydrogen volume. The second captures atmospheric oxygen mass. Water production is nine tonnes per tonne of hydrogen.

The resulting five-state unload schedule is:

| Payload removed | Payload remaining | H₂ converted | Controlled ground reaction |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0% | 340 t | 0.000 t | 274.896 t |
| 25% | 255 t | 2.245 t | 236.172 t |
| 50% | 170 t | 4.490 t | 197.448 t |
| 75% | 85 t | 6.736 t | 158.724 t |
| 100% | 0 t | 8.981 t | 120.000 t |

This trajectory keeps positive ground reaction throughout transfer instead of allowing the aircraft to become airborne between cargo waves. The process must be symmetric enough that water mass and lost lift do not create an unacceptable rolling or pitching moment. Cargo actuation and conversion must be interlocked: if support change lags, payload removal stops before reaction crosses the registered limit. The system must also reverse a partial unload safely, which may require restoring gaseous lift from stored hydrogen, accepting a new cargo state or entering a declared non-flight hold.

Six trains nominally convert 10.8 tonnes of hydrogen per hour. With one train isolated, nine tonnes per hour remain; the 8.981-tonne endpoint is reached in 0.998 hour. Five trains retain 309.167 tonnes of total support authority, 124.063 tonnes above the cold-dense requirement. Conversion yields 149.530 megawatt-hours of electrical energy, more than eighty percent of the 175-megawatt-hour placement demand. Heat and exhaust remain limiting, so electrical output is not free capacity.

The water is not destination ballast. It remains vehicle-owned, contained and traceable. It may later become certified service water only through a distinct treatment, release and civil-custody process. The receiver contributes no water, anchor, replacement cargo or common loader to make the aircraft stable.

## 7. Contact, landing and no-anchor mooring

Sixteen ten-square-metre feet are arranged as eight bilateral stations. At rated hot-high arrival the 490.546-tonne reaction produces 30.077 kilopascals. Applying the 1.15 landing dynamic factor and 1.10 imbalance factor raises pressure to 38.047 kilopascals. After one foot is lost, 150 square metres remain and pressure reaches **40.583 kilopascals**, leaving 19.417 kilopascals against the sixty-kilopascal bearing screen. The local factored load is **41.369 tonnes per foot**, leaving 3.631 tonnes against the provisional forty-five-tonne structural rating.

That narrow local margin matters more than the comfortable average soil pressure. A valid contact system needs independent vertical travel, active leveling, bearing and shear sensing, local isolation valves, structural brakes and downward cassette removal. Pressure alone does not establish cyclic settlement, lateral shear, liquefaction, buried-object response or erosion under exhaust. Representative ground tests must include those failure modes.

Landing is a progressive transfer from aerodynamic lift to the feet. No payload may release before structural arrest and rebound control. The aircraft may not unload while suspended from thrust. A 420-metre-square public-exclusion area remains under receiver authority during approach, exhaust control and transfer.

Prepared anchors receive no universal credit. At forty metres per second, the declared projected area and side-force coefficient produce a 4.505-meganewton broadside force. A 1.25 control reserve raises demand to **5.631 meganewtons**. With two of twelve pods unavailable, vector thrust contributes 5.5 meganewtons. At the minimum 110-tonne reaction and after one foot loss, controlled friction adds 0.455 meganewton, producing 5.955 meganewtons and a **0.325-meganewton margin**.

This is one of the weakest margins in the design. It depends on gust coefficient, local flow, pod-vector performance, surface friction, foot control and synchronized thrust. It also assumes public exhaust and acoustic limits can coexist with 5.5 meganewtons of ground-proximate vector force. A representative receiver article must invalidate or replace those coefficients before station-keeping credit is granted.

## 8. Energy isolation, heat rejection and environmental control

The carrier uses six energy citadels and buses, each normally serving a bilateral pod pair. Cross-ties exist for restoration but remain open in the registered mission state. One bus loss and two pod losses are distinct casualties. No common fuel sump, coolant loop, oxygen supply, exhaust path or control dependency may turn a local failure into a full-aircraft loss.

High-temperature conversion is assigned a provisional 52-percent electrical efficiency. Seventy-two percent of waste heat leaves through a controlled exhaust path; the remainder reaches the surface-cooling system. Eight isolated radiator panels provide 480 square metres. Under route airflow, icing and fouling derate the available flux; at station, fan-driven rejection and a 0.70 fouling factor govern.

The behavior model closes three heat states after one panel is unavailable:

| State | Surface heat | N-1 rejection | Margin |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| cruise | 24.776 MW | 56.700 MW | 31.924 MW |
| cold-dense conversion | 30.918 MW | 41.160 MW | 10.242 MW |
| placement ceiling | 36.185 MW | 47.040 MW | 10.855 MW |

These are thermal allocation screens, not proof of heat exchangers. Smoke, salt, ice, ash, biological fouling and wind direction can reduce heat transfer while also restricting exhaust. The aircraft must avoid using floodwater or a municipal water system as an implicit heat sink. A conversion train that survives chemically but overheats its neighboring lift cells has not survived.

Environmental technology should be pursued as a single compound problem. Membranes, lightning protection, fire barriers, de-icing, thermal panels, exhaust and vehicle-scale sensing interact through the same skin. The program should build powered envelope sectors early, not certify isolated material coupons and infer whole-body behavior.

## 9. Payload order, balance and civil release

The two cargo keels contain sixteen dry positions and two wet packs. Dry modules average 18.125 tonnes; the two wet packs are 13.75 tonnes each. Positions form four unload waves, alternate port and starboard primary exits and preserve an alternate exit on the opposite side. Eight robotic lanes—four per side—provide a rated 254-tonne-per-hour aggregate transfer rate.

At the assigned manifest, longitudinal center of gravity is 152.74 metres, lateral center is zero and vertical center is 6.33 metres. The limits are not merely departure checks. Each unload wave must remain within longitudinal, lateral and vertical bounds while conversion water is simultaneously added to the keel. A signed mass-and-balance state therefore links payload serial, cargo actuator, cell-conversion command, water-tank inventory, foot reaction and flight-control authority.

The receiver contains no common pre-release loader or common energy source. The aircraft must complete transfer through its own bilateral robots and protected power. The clear transfer core is 420 metres square with a four-kilometre approach lane, local offline navigation and a sixty-kilopascal surveyed bearing floor. Atmospheric and terrain receivers remain at least five kilometres apart before independent civil release. A fail-open corridor may connect them afterward but cannot alter either release.

The distinction between technical unload and public service remains constitutional. Vehicle authority may arrest the carrier or stop cargo. Payload authority controls serial state. Receiver authority controls exclusion, ground admission and transfer. Independent civil authority decides when the resulting capability becomes public service. No one technical command should be able to manufacture civil release by declaring its own platform ready.

## 10. Crew survival, casualty states and recovery

Sixteen people operate the aircraft in four groups: flight and navigation, energy and lift state, landing and mooring, and cargo/public interface. Eight occupy the forward flight refuge and eight the aft mission refuge. Each refuge has two external exits and enough local control to hold the vehicle safe after the other occupied zone is lost.

Two refuges do not make the aircraft safe by themselves. Air, thermal protection, communication, fire isolation, pressure control and command must remain genuinely separate. A refuge whose control lane, exit or atmosphere crosses a machinery fire boundary is not independent. One occupied-zone loss may deny the mission but must not deny a safe hold or escape.

Twenty casualty worlds distinguish vehicle safety from formation service. The model preserves behavior after one lift cell, two lift cells in isolation, one pod, two pods, one bus, one conversion train, one radiator panel, one foot, one transfer lane, one unload side, one refuge, a nominal-network denial, cold-dense transfer and the governing gust. Complete loss of one `GCH-340R` denies the atmospheric cell because its cargo cannot be quietly redistributed before release. The terrain cell still preserves the formation-level public effect. Denial of the entire air path or the entire terrain path is likewise tolerated by the other mode. Denial of both is outside the declared guarantee.

Recovery must address a 300-metre disabled article, not just removable engines. Forward and aft recovery zones, sectional lift-cell stabilization, local water and fuel isolation, downward contact-cassette removal and lateral machinery extraction are first-order architecture. A blocked end cannot deny every critical removal path. The recovery force needs heavy aerostructure shoring, membrane repair, contaminated hydrogen handling, water transfer, crane and transporter systems, and authority to establish a large public safety boundary.

Reset reaches 162 hours on the structure, envelope and twenty-four-cell path, leaving six hours to the 168-hour ceiling. Energy and thermal reset require 154 hours; contact and mooring 146; lift-water processing and gas refill 132; cargo robotics 112; crew and receiver handback ninety-six. These paths are concurrent only if depots, people, spares, instruments and authorities actually exist in parallel.

## 11. Mission clock and formation consequence

The atmospheric path begins with sixteen hours of origin technical release and loading. The 4,000-kilometre cruise at 240 kilometres per hour consumes 16.667 hours. Arrest, lift conversion and unload occur concurrently in 1.25 hours. Receiver build and civil release require eight hours. Atmospheric public service therefore occurs at **relative hour 41.917**, five minutes inside the forty-two-hour air boundary, and at **absolute hour 47.917** after the six-hour regional activation offset.

That five-minute relative margin is intentionally uncomfortable. It reveals that the inherited air clock is now a hard performance requirement. A one-train rate shortfall extends transfer to 1.5 hours and produces relative hour 42.167. A five-percent cruise penalty produces hour 42.794. One hour of origin rework produces hour 42.917. A combined stress reaches hour 44.544. The atmospheric vehicle may still beat the overall hour-54 second-region deadline in those worlds, but it no longer meets its own independent air-path boundary.

`SAT-170R` reaches absolute public service at hour 49.57. Terrain therefore remains the governing complete `DCP-2` clock, leaving 4.43 hours to hour 54. The atmospheric path’s deep correction does not change that endpoint, but it reduces false confidence: the prior formation appeared to have 6.083 hours of margin because the air clock was mistakenly treated as the two-mode clock.

The next campaign must test the complete formation, not two separate demonstrations. Two `GCH-340R` and four `SAT-170R` articles must depart separated origins, enter separated receivers, survive one-mode denial, reach independent civil release and reset without hidden common loaders, energy, communications, release authorities, yards, recovery trains or industrial lots.

The institutional implication is larger. A new **Civil Aviation Safety Authority** inside the Department should own occupied-refuge, ground interaction, chemical conversion, public plume and civil-release rules, with an **Independent Test Authority** holding representative proof.

## 14. The $600 billion technology and proving portfolio

The technology portfolio is nested across research, tooling, infrastructure, proving and certification; it is not added again to the program total. Its eighteen campaigns are intentionally large enough to sustain rival technical lineages until evidence selects among them.

The largest campaigns address lift-gas conversion, atmospheric proving, lift-cell materials, mission hydrogen, manufacturing and digital certification, cellular structure, propulsion, contact and mooring. Other campaigns cover powered-hull interaction, slow-flight lift, thermal control, robotic unloading, compound weather, crew refuge, recovery, complete `DCP-2` missions, hydrogen infrastructure and a non-hydrogen energy rival.

Each campaign has five required properties:

1. a decisive coefficient whose error can overturn the architecture;
2. at least three rival technical lineages where lock-in would be dangerous;
3. a representative physical object rather than only simulation;
4. a destructive or denial trial tied to a mission failure transition; and
5. an institutional owner distinct from the production prime.

The program should be ambitious about invention but conservative about credit. Examples include:

- **self-sealing cellular membranes** with embedded optical strain, hydrogen permeation and lightning-state sensing;
- **reversible or dual-use lift-gas conversion** that can create water and electrical power without allowing flame propagation into a cell;
- **very-high-specific-energy hydrogen containment** with crash, boil-off and damaged-lot isolation;
- **distributed boundary-layer propulsors** whose vector thrust serves cruise, gust rejection, landing and ground stabilization;
- **active lifting-body control** at unusually low speed and high gust exposure;
- **load-aware contact feet** that infer bearing and shear failure before a local sink becomes a structural casualty;
- **robotic serial unloading** coupled directly to live mass, lift and reaction state;
- **sectional recovery structures** that let a 300-metre vehicle be made safe, divided and removed without a purpose-built airport; and
- **offline digital certification** that reconstructs every membrane lot, weld, catalyst, software build, payload serial and mission-state limit under network denial.

Innovation is not a reason to skip rivals. The hydrogen architecture must face an independent non-hydrogen energy sector and continued helium-compression research. If either can close mass, heat, transient, safety and reset with better evidence, the program should change vehicles.

## 15. Evidence constitution and falsification order

All 150 representative evidence gates remain open. They cover geometry, lift, offload transient, energy, aerothermal performance, contact, mooring, payload and crew, mission and reset, and industrial program. A behavior-model pass grants no readiness, procurement or rendering credit.

The first decisive object should be a **full four-cell lift bay connected to one complete conversion train**. It should include representative primary structure, membranes, valves, sensors, oxygen handling, water containment, thermal surfaces, controls and fire boundaries. It should be tested through:

- hot-high and cold-dense pressure states;
- one cell tear, valve failure and propagation attempt;
- lightning, icing, salt, smoke and thermal fouling;
- symmetric and asymmetric hydrogen conversion;
- one-train loss during a timed unload profile;
- water-tank leak and arrested partial conversion;
- safe stop, partial reversal and recovery; and
- destructive proof that a local failure remains local.

The second object should add four contact feet, two vector pods and one complete cargo quarter. It should execute the cold-dense partial-unload trajectory while one conversion train, one foot and two pods are unavailable. The trial should randomize soil, gust direction, actuator delay and sensor error. The stop rule is any uncommanded loss of ground reaction, bearing exceedance, reaction overshoot, local structural overload, inaccessible payload position, public plume violation or failure to reach a safe hold.

Only after those articles should the program build a powered half-body, a full lifting-body demonstrator and complete production-representative aircraft. Full-mission trials must preserve sealed coefficients and denial schedules. An independent authority—not the prime, operational sponsor or workbook author—should own gate closure.

The evidence model is intentionally harsh. One failed representative result does not automatically kill every lineage, but it removes the affected coefficient and any downstream readiness claim until a preregistered repair and repetition succeeds. Repeated retuning on the same range does not create independent proof.

## 16. Fiscal propagation and strategic meaning

Replacing the $932.25745-billion Pass 83 atmospheric account with the $1.7297432-trillion complete-system program adds $797.48575 billion. The non-atmospheric, non-terrain `DCP-2` remainder stays at $294.7264 billion. Adding the unchanged $1.58204456-trillion `SAT-170R` program and $170.78694-billion federated receiver produces a **$3.7773011-trillion `DCP-2`**. Adding the current $7.38314155-trillion dynamic one-region base produces an **$11.16044265-trillion national boundary**.

This increase should not be read as evidence that the Department is unaffordable. It is evidence that the earlier vehicle boundary was incomplete. A serious national program must decide whether the benefit of an independent atmospheric access mode warrants the industrial effort, whether a different mission range or payload changes the optimum, and which capability belongs in prepared civil infrastructure instead of mobile mass.

Nor should the figures be compared casually to one annual defense budget. The comparator spans twenty-five years and includes the creation of industrial lineages, ranges, depots, reserves and operations. The policy claim is not that every provisional platform should be bought. It is that a resilience force deserves the same first-principles willingness to create technology, industry and human capital that the United States applies to military force projection.

At maturity, this sector would project a distinct form of power. It could place water, energy, medical, communications and logistics capacity into a disaster zone without treating military access as the default institutional pathway. It could support domestic regions, allies and invited partners with a force whose doctrine, safety authority, public accountability and export economy are designed for civil stabilization from inception.

### Falsified

- A 663-tonne empty atmospheric carrier is not a complete-system mass account.
- A sixty-tonne variable-buoyancy allowance is not an offload-control architecture.
- Hot-high air does not govern every mission phase merely because it governs minimum static lift.
- Sixty square metres of contact does not survive dynamic, imbalance and one-foot-loss closure.
- Prepared anchors, receiver ballast, common loaders or common pre-release energy cannot be hidden inside an independent atmospheric mode.
- A platform-only workforce and cost estimate cannot represent this capability.

### Still provisional

Primary-structure mass, membrane life, hydrogen permeation, lift-to-drag ratio, slow-flight control, fuel containment, conversion efficiency and rate, water-tank dynamics, heat flux, exhaust safety, soil shear, gust coefficient, friction, pod thrust, robotic transfer, human workload, reset duration, production rate, workforce and cost all remain hypotheses. None has representative evidence.

### New burden created by the correction

The carrier now requires a new lift-cell materials sector, lift-gas conversion industry, atmospheric hydrogen custody chain, six-lane energy architecture, compound climate ranges, 300-metre article recovery service, civil atmospheric-release profession and an industrial ecosystem on the order of 105,000 workers. Those are not support details. They are part of the capability.

### Next decisive force question

The two `DCP-2` carriers now have comparable internal depth. The next force pass should not optimize either one in isolation. It should build a **common carrier comparison and representative mission compiler** that holds payload, origin, receiver, public-service effect and denial worlds constant while comparing the air and terrain paths across measured or explicitly open coefficients. It should expose which capabilities genuinely require two modes, which failures remain common, which industrial lineages are shared, and whether the hour-54 guarantee survives stochastic delay and reset distributions.

### Rendering boundary

Requirements diagrams may become useful after representative subsystem geometry replaces the present lift-cell, conversion, contact, exhaust and recovery coefficients. Exterior concept art remains blocked. Rendering the current envelope would convert an evidence agenda into an apparent design and would bias later configuration choices toward an unearned shape.

## Conclusion

Pass 87 changes the atmospheric carrier from a large airship concept into a complete-system research program. Its central innovation is not hydrogen alone. It is the recognition that a civil strategic carrier must actively govern support while payload, atmosphere, energy and ground interaction change together.

That recognition produces a harder and more expensive machine, but also a more useful national project. It creates measurable technology problems, identifiable professions, independent safety authorities, industrial lineages, proving ranges and exportable civil capabilities. It is exactly the kind of ambitious, first-principles institution-building that an equal Department of Resilience would require.

The result remains deliberately unproven. All 150 evidence gates are open. The proper next step is not a prettier vehicle. It is a representative four-cell conversion article designed to fail honestly.
