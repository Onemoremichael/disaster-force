# Maritime, air-mobility, and medical systems

## Design judgment

The Department of Resilience requires organic maritime and aviation forces because catastrophic operations are frequently decided by damaged ports, airfields, roads, local hospitals, and distribution systems. Organic mobility is not a claim that the Department should manufacture every transport type. It is an assurance claim: the Department must own enough access, movement, reception, and patient-flow capacity to make its concurrency promise credible when commercial markets and military lift are unavailable or committed elsewhere.

The principal procurement error would be to begin with a large ship or aircraft and search for useful payloads. These forces instead begin with five independent questions:

1. What effect must cross the strategic distance?
2. Through what intact, degraded, improvised, or absent receiving node?
3. What must leave the affected area on the return movement?
4. How is the effect sustained after the first delivery?
5. What smaller systems connect the strategic platform to people, patients, utilities, and work sites?

## The mobility architecture

Mobility has five layers. A complete operation may require all five.

| Layer | Function | Typical failure if omitted |
|---|---|---|
| Intertheater | Move formations and bulk stocks between national or global regions | response arrives too late or depends on borrowed lift |
| Theater | Move between functioning hubs and damaged subregions | cargo accumulates at the first intact port or airfield |
| Entry | Create access without normal port, runway, road, or utility support | high-capacity transport cannot discharge |
| Distribution | Deliver to local nodes and points of use | nominal throughput never becomes service |
| Retrograde | Remove patients, evacuees, waste, damaged equipment, and empty containers | receiving nodes congest and the force cannot regenerate |

The Department should therefore measure **delivered mission-module days** and **completed passenger or patient journeys**, not ton-miles alone.

## Maritime Response Force

### MR-1: littoral access craft

This family enters first and connects larger ships to shore. Variants include shallow-draft landing craft, amphibious cargo carriers, personnel and patient transfer boats, survey craft, and uncrewed hydrographic or obstruction-clearance systems.

Common requirements are:

- operation from beaches, damaged quays, rivers, canals, and austere ramps;
- bow, stern, side, and crane transfer options;
- protected patient and passenger movement rather than cargo-only decks;
- organic navigation marking, hydrographic survey, communications, and night operation;
- debris-tolerant propulsion and replaceable protective components;
- standardized interfaces with trucks, pallets, containers, pumps, hoses, and medical modules; and
- shore crews, traffic control, fuel, maintenance, and recovery equipment in the same formation.

The small connector is not an accessory. During a hospital-ship deployment, the Navy reported that small boats made more than 200 shore patient transfers per day possible.[^mercy-transfer] Any large maritime concept must therefore include its connector throughput in the parent system's requirement record.

### MR-2: port-opening and waterway-restoration group

The port-opening family makes damaged maritime infrastructure usable. It combines:

- rapid hydrographic and structural survey;
- channel and berth debris removal;
- salvage, towing, diving, cutting, lifting, and emergency patching;
- temporary navigation aids and traffic management;
- mobile fenders, ramps, dolphins, causeways, cranes, and lighting;
- hazardous-material containment and sampling;
- emergency power, firefighting, dewatering, and communications; and
- digital berth, cargo, customs, and onward-movement control.

This is a formation rather than a single vessel. The Department should maintain pre-engineered port kits for recurring geometries and an authority pathway for emergency structural and environmental decisions.

### MR-3: resilience support carrier

The resilience support carrier is a large, purpose-built mobile base for distributed response systems. Its mission is not to perform every function internally. It receives, sustains, launches, repairs, and commands modular forces when shore infrastructure is absent.

#### Required functions

- roll-on/roll-off and lift-on/lift-off handling for road-mobile and container-compatible modules;
- flight deck and hangar support for helicopters and vertical-lift uncrewed systems;
- wet or stern access for littoral craft;
- workshops for vehicles, aircraft, pumps, electronics, clinical equipment, and autonomous systems;
- bulk electrical generation, water, oxygen, fuel handling, cold chain, waste treatment, and stores;
- protected command, communications, geospatial, weather, and public-information spaces;
- configurable clinical, laboratory, shelter, and workforce accommodations;
- large numbers of simultaneous shore connections rather than one oversize umbilical; and
- ability to exchange modules and crews while remaining on station.

#### Concept band

This document does not select displacement or hull form. The useful concept band is a family with approximately 20,000–60,000 tonnes of payload and mission-support capacity, 15–30 days of independent high-tempo operation before substantial replenishment, multiple landing spots, and several independent discharge paths. Physics closure must determine whether one large hull, two medium hulls, or a mothership-plus-barge system creates the best availability and port-access portfolio.

The support carrier should normally use non-nuclear propulsion. Nuclear power is reserved for the water-energy family in [Water, energy, and utility systems](water-energy-and-utility-systems.md), where continuous high-output process energy can justify the licensing and lifecycle burden. Combining reactor, hospital, aviation, bulk cargo, and high-cycling small-craft functions in one universal ship would concentrate risk and create mutually incompatible safety cases.

### MR-4: modular sealift and sustainment ship

This family carries the unglamorous mass that determines campaign duration: construction equipment, bridge sets, water and power modules, vehicles, pipe, cable, food, shelter materials, spares, and replacement crews. It should emphasize:

- rapid loading from dispersed civilian and Department facilities;
- roll-on/roll-off access at varying quay heights;
- self-discharge where shore cranes are lost;
- modular refrigerated, medical, hazardous, and battery cargo zones;
- low crew burden and high reliability;
- austere refueling and ship-to-ship transfer; and
- return carriage of waste, evacuees, repairables, and empty distribution assets.

The sovereign fleet floor should cover first rotations in simultaneous campaigns. Assured commercial and allied capacity can carry later bulk flow, but only if exercised, tracked, and subject to priority agreements.

### MR-5: riverine and inland-waterway response group

Coastal ships cannot solve urban and continental flooding. The inland family comprises shallow-draft workboats, modular barges, amphibious carriers, pump barges, clinical and shelter barges, tow systems, and bank-to-bank utility crossings. It is designed for restricted air draft, debris, current, contaminated water, damaged locks, improvised launch points, and road transport between watersheds.

Its decisive design variable is deployability by road and rail, not ocean endurance. Barges provide efficient work surfaces for pumps, cranes, generation, storage, and treatment, but require tow, mooring, bank access, crew support, and waterway survey.

## Maritime medical force

### Why the existing hospital-ship pattern is insufficient

The Navy's current hospital ships began as converted oil tankers and advertise up to 1,000 beds, 12 operating rooms, and five-day activation.[^navy-hospital-ships] Those figures describe nominal internal capacity, not a complete disaster care pathway. The ships require approximately 1,200 medical personnel at full operation, suitable anchorage or berth, shore-to-ship patient transfer, referral criteria, clinical supplies, discharge destinations, and local health-system integration. They are valuable evidence but not the Department's design template.

A national resilience medical fleet should optimize **completed appropriate episodes of care per scarce clinician-day**, not beds afloat.

### MH-1: littoral stabilization ship

A small, high-readiness ship supports the first 72 hours and distributed coastal access. It should provide emergency medicine, resuscitation, limited surgery, obstetric capability, diagnostics, isolation, pharmacy, blood and oxygen support, and short holding capacity. It needs several patient-transfer paths and a low staff-to-hull ratio.

The ship operates as part of a coastal health group with shore teams, patient-transfer craft, rotorcraft access, telemedicine, public-health reconnaissance, and definitive-care evacuation. Its value is speed and access, not long inpatient census.

### MH-2: regional clinical support ship

The regional ship supports weeks of degraded local care. Its modular clinical mix can emphasize:

- emergency and general surgery;
- dialysis and renal support;
- maternal, neonatal, and pediatric care;
- respiratory and infectious-disease care;
- burns and wound care;
- imaging, laboratory, pharmacy, sterile processing, and blood support;
- rehabilitation and behavioral health; and
- outpatient chronic-disease continuity.

It should separate high-acuity, infectious, ambulatory, and staff flows; carry genuine medical-gas, HVAC, wastewater, sterilization, and clinical-data redundancy; and be able to exchange specialty modules without rebuilding the ship.

### MH-3: strategic health-support platform

The strategic platform is a sustained regional health node rather than a larger floating trauma center. It hosts specialty teams, laboratories, manufacturing and repair of medical consumables, training, workforce rest, blood and pharmaceutical logistics, and referral coordination. Its inpatient complement expands only when staff, downstream discharge, and shore transfer can support it.

The platform may share a support-carrier hull family, but clinical construction, shock/vibration limits, infection control, elevators, piping, power quality, and accreditation must be purpose-designed. A roll-on medical module placed in an ordinary cargo hold is not an equivalent hospital.

## Air Response mobility force

### Ownership rule

The Department should own aircraft where response demand is correlated, access is specialized, configuration is unique, readiness must be measured continuously, or another national mission could preempt borrowed capacity. It should use commercial capacity for ordinary passenger and freight movement when the market is deep and priority access is credible.

This produces a mixed force rather than an imitation military air arm.

### AM-1: strategic mission-module airlift

The Department needs an assured floor of long-range, roll-on/roll-off aircraft for rapid movement of complete initial-entry formations. The first-principles question is not whether to design a novel large airframe. It is whether a purpose-built cargo-handling, power, communications, maintainability, austere-loading, and mission-module configuration on a proven airframe family can meet the need more cheaply and safely.

Novel strategic aircraft development is admitted only if the later load-and-network model reveals a persistent outsized-cargo or damaged-runway gap that existing airframes cannot close. Industrial ambition does not justify reinvention where a mature transport core works.

### AM-2: short-field disaster airlifter

The distinctive fixed-wing requirement is a medium aircraft optimized for frequent cycles between intact regional hubs and damaged or short fields. Its requirement band includes:

- useful payload rather than maximum payload on 900–1,500 meter damaged or expeditionary surfaces;
- low-pressure landing gear, foreign-object tolerance, and rapid inspection;
- self-loading pallets, vehicles, patients, and water or power modules;
- reverse flow for patients, evacuees, samples, and repairables;
- ground-independent start, lighting, communications, and cargo handling;
- high-cycle fatigue life and field-replaceable vulnerable components; and
- civil airspace, noise, and emissions compatibility.

The design should be evaluated against runway repair plus larger-aircraft alternatives. It exists only where the network analysis shows that repeated short-field access reduces time to effect enough to justify a unique fleet.

### AM-3: vertical access family

This family moves small teams, patients, repair parts, sensors, and priority cargo where roads and runways are absent. It includes crewed rotorcraft and uncrewed cargo systems across distinct payload bands.

No universal vertical-lift platform is proposed. Medical evacuation needs vibration, access, clinical power, pressurization or altitude constraints, and patient monitoring; external-load construction needs stability and lifting performance; urban delivery needs noise, downwash, precision, and obstacle control. Common engines, avionics, support equipment, training elements, and digital interfaces should unite otherwise different variants.

### AM-4: airborne command, sensing, and relay

Persistent aircraft and high-altitude systems can restore communications, map damage and fire, provide weather observations, relay responder traffic, and host edge computation. They should be sized as communications and information services with explicit coverage, endurance, latency, spectrum, backhaul, weather, and launch-base requirements—not procured as generic surveillance aircraft.

### AM-5: airfield-opening group

Aircraft are effective only when a receiving system exists. Every deployed air package therefore includes scalable elements for:

- runway and pavement assessment;
- debris removal and limited rapid repair;
- temporary lighting, navigation, weather, and air traffic services;
- fuel quality, storage, dispensing, and fire protection;
- cargo handling and passenger or patient flow;
- perimeter and public safety;
- crew rest, maintenance, power, water, and communications; and
- slot allocation and onward movement.

Aircraft count without airfield throughput is not readiness.

## Ground and deployable medical system

### Care echelons

| Echelon | Primary function | Target posture |
|---|---|---|
| H-0 | Public health intelligence, warnings, self-care support, medication continuity | persistent and digital/local |
| H-1 | Triage, first aid, stabilization, primary care, pharmacy and outreach | hours; mobile and neighborhood-scale |
| H-2 | Emergency, observation, diagnostics, limited surgery, maternal care and short holding | 12–24 hours; road/air deployable |
| H-3 | Modular inpatient, surgical, intensive, infectious, renal and specialty care | 24–72 hours; theater hospital campus or ship |
| H-4 | Definitive specialty care and long rehabilitation | national/allied network, evacuation or reinforced regional center |
| H-5 | Recovery of the health system: staff, facilities, supply, records, public health and finance | weeks to years |

The force must close the chain between echelons. A stabilization team without transport and a receiving bed creates a clinical queue, not a completed care pathway.

### HM-1: community health continuity team

Small multidisciplinary teams maintain medication, oxygen, dialysis referral, maternal care, behavioral health, infection control, accessible communications, and surveillance among displaced or isolated populations. This corrects the recurrent mismatch between trauma-oriented federal teams and the chronic or primary-care needs encountered after major storms; GAO documented such a mismatch after the 2017 hurricanes.[^gao-health]

### HM-2: mobile acute-care unit

The unit provides resuscitation, diagnostics, short observation, minor procedures, limited surgery, and patient preparation for movement. It is built around staffed treatment positions, not tent area or stored beds. It deploys with power, oxygen, pharmacy, blood, sterile processing, laboratory, water, sanitation, waste, records, communications, security, and crew support.

### HM-3: modular theater hospital campus

The campus combines independent clinical blocks around a common utility and logistics spine. Modules may include emergency, surgery, ICU, medical/surgical wards, isolation, renal, obstetric, pediatric, burn, rehabilitation, behavioral health, imaging, laboratory, pharmacy, dental, mortuary, staff health, and administration.

The governing equation is:

```text
usable staffed capacity = min(
  clinical spaces,
  qualified teams by shift and specialty,
  utilities and consumables,
  referral and evacuation flow,
  infection-control limits,
  discharge and community support
)
```

Unused shell beds are not counted. Clinical quality systems, credentialing, adverse-event review, and patient-rights controls operate in the field; GAO has emphasized that operational federal care still requires systematic quality management.[^gao-quality]

### HM-4: patient movement system

This is a command-and-logistics system with clinical transport assets. It matches patient condition to origin, vehicle, escort, destination, route, timing, and records; preserves accessible movement for people with mobility, sensory, cognitive, or medical needs; and tracks family reunification and return.

Variants include ground ambulance and bus modules, protected high-clearance movement, littoral transfer, rotary and fixed-wing evacuation, and non-medical assisted transport. Each receiving location must accept the patient before departure unless immediate lifesaving evacuation makes that impossible.

### HM-5: health logistics and workforce regeneration group

The limiting clinical resource is often personnel rather than equipment. This group provides credentialing, scheduling, replacement teams, staff housing, food, sanitation, mental-health support, medical maintenance, blood and pharmaceutical cold chain, oxygen production, laundry, sterile processing, waste, and rest.

Existing Disaster Medical Assistance Teams illustrate both speed and scale limits: ASPR describes a typical team as 35 people, mobile within six hours, able to arrive within 48 hours, and self-sufficient for 72 hours.[^aspr-mscc] A peer establishment needs multiple rotations and an enduring professional pipeline, not a larger cache attached to the same intermittent labor model.

## Requirement records before concept selection

Every mobility or medical program must publish:

- design reference missions and time windows;
- payload or patient demand by origin and destination;
- access conditions and probability of node failure;
- complete cycle time and utilization;
- crew and specialty staffing by shift and rotation;
- fuel, electrical, water, oxygen, cooling, waste, and maintenance balances;
- reception, distribution, evacuation, and retrograde capacity;
- availability after scheduled and unscheduled maintenance;
- weather, contamination, noise, safety, and regulatory envelope;
- interoperability with local, allied, commercial, and Department systems; and
- transfer condition and peacetime utilization.

## Requirement verdicts

| Concept | Verdict | Governing condition |
|---|---|---|
| One universal disaster ship | Reject | Cargo, nuclear, aviation, clinical, shallow-water, and port-opening safety cases conflict |
| Resilience support carrier family | Admit | Must close connector, module, workshop, crew, replenishment, and availability architecture |
| Converted large hospital ship as future standard | Reject | Nominal beds and hull volume do not optimize staffing, activation, patient flow, or specialty match |
| Three-tier purpose-built medical fleet | Admit | Final hull mix follows care-pathway and coastal-access modeling |
| Nuclear-powered general response carrier | Hold | Process-energy mission may justify a water-energy platform; generalized hotel loads do not yet justify nuclear lifecycle burden |
| Purpose-built short-field airlifter | Hold for network model | Unique fleet justified only by material time-to-effect advantage over airfield repair and existing aircraft |
| Organic strategic-airlift floor | Admit as requirement | Aircraft design remains open; complete first-entry formations must not depend on contested borrowing |
| Large uncrewed cargo fleet | Admit by route and payload tier | Weather, safe landing, energy, maintenance, airspace, noise, and ground handling must close |
| Modular theater hospital | Admit | Count staffed, supplied, connected clinical pathways rather than erected beds |
| Hospital ship without connectors and shore health teams | Reject | It is not a complete medical delivery system |
