# Flood response force engineering

## Executive judgment

A Department of Resilience funded and valued as a peer instrument of national power should not build a larger inventory of portable pumps, sandbags and rescue boats. It should create a national **hydraulic-control force**: a distributed and deployable system able to understand moving water, act before arrival, exclude it from priority zones, arrest structural failures, move people and cargo through distinct flood environments, redirect and remove water, manage contamination, restore drainage and access, and transfer a safer watershed and infrastructure system to civil operators.

The unit of capability is not a pump, barrier or amphibious vehicle. It is a controlled change in the coupled system:

`rain / river / tide / surge / structure → inundation → people and infrastructure → drainage and discharge → contamination → recovery`

Pass 13 produces four institutional conclusions.

1. **The force needs two readiness systems.** A geographically distributed **Flood Intercept Network** (`FIN`) must inspect, instrument, pre-stage, close, reinforce and evacuate before or during onset. A deployable **Hydraulic Control Group** (`HCG`) must create sustained regional water-control, access and recovery capacity. Slow river rise and forecast coastal surge reward forward action; flash flood and sudden breach reward permanent local coverage. Heavy national formations cannot recover lost warning time after they arrive.
2. **Inland and coastal groups share a technical core but require different operating modules.** Inland operations are dominated by river stage, levees, drainage basins, shallow streets, bridges, sediment and long routes. Coastal operations add tide, surge, waves, salt, surf, port damage, tidal reversals, marine corrosion and littoral access. One “flood vehicle” or universal hydraulic ship would fail both.
3. **Water removal is a system balance.** Pump nameplate means little without actual head, intake and debris condition, power, pipe or channel capacity, downstream stage, discharge authority, continuous maintenance and the inflow that continues while pumping. A barrier without interior drainage can worsen the protected-side flood. A rescue craft that cannot reach its launch site, pass hidden obstacles, receive a patient or decontaminate between sorties is not rescue capacity.
4. **Strategic-scale technology investment can change the frontier.** The Department should pursue continuous flood digital twins, dense expendable sensing, modular megaflow electric pumps, rapidly assembled drainage trunks, adaptive self-monitoring barriers, active breach-arrest systems, optionally crewed earthworks, autonomous surface fleets, purpose-built low-wake urban rescue craft, current-capable rescue platforms, heavy amphibious logistics and coastal or riverine hydraulic works carriers. These are research and acquisition programs with test ranges, competing prototypes and production reserves—not accessories bought after disasters.

This pass defines the mature formation, purpose-built system families, governing calculations, industrial base, a 25-year $1 trillion capital/RDT&E thought experiment, failure cases and the next configuration model. It advances the integrated `FIN` and reference `HCG-100` to `R2`, not to physics closure. No flood platform is yet authorized for rendering.

## The decision problem

Flood response is often represented as a sequence of warnings, evacuation, rescue and cleanup. That frame understates the engineering mission. Water is a moving mass whose elevation, momentum, path and contamination can be changed. The force can alter consequences at several points:

- before onset by mapping the exposed system, protecting closures, staging barriers and pumps, lowering controllable storage, clearing drainage and moving people;
- during onset by maintaining observation, closing gates, reinforcing weak reaches, rescuing people and preventing cascading infrastructure failure;
- during peak by controlling breach growth, protecting critical zones, maintaining interior drainage, creating access and sustaining isolated communities;
- during recession by routing and pumping trapped water, treating or containing contaminated flows, reopening routes and restoring utilities; and
- after recession by removing sediment and debris, repairing control structures, drying buildings, stabilizing slopes and transferring operations.

This is not a case for federalizing every levee, drain or rescue service. The mature Department owns the sovereign capabilities that must be ready despite rare demand, require national standardization, depend on deep engineering and industrial investment, or must move between regions. Local and state institutions retain territorial knowledge, land-use authority, law enforcement, evacuation authority and routine systems. The Department brings a standing national force able to add hydraulic, access and industrial mass on a war-footing scale.

Current evidence is useful as a lower bound, not an organizational template. USACE flood guidance treats reaction time, continuous levee patrol, drainage structures, pump staffing, backup power, debris at trash racks, breach dimensions and performance data as one operating problem.[^usace-flood] FEMA's typed swiftwater team makes night operations, hydrology, rope and boat rescue, medical care, infrastructure loss and coordination with aircraft and vehicles part of the qualification envelope.[^fema-swiftwater] Fixed systems demonstrate the physical scale: the three New Orleans Permanent Canal Closures and Pumps sites provide 24,300 cubic feet per second of combined capacity, approximately 688 m³/s, with independent emergency power.[^usace-pccp] These sources show that a complete flood capability already spans prediction, structures, pumping, power, people, access and maintenance. The new institution should integrate and industrialize that chain at much greater scale.

## Flood is not one operating environment

The force should classify missions by hydraulic and access state, not by the presidential declaration label or weather event.

| Operating state | Dominant clock | Physical discriminants | Primary force problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| Forecast river or basin rise | hours to weeks | hydrograph, storage, levee condition, drainage, soil saturation | pre-position, reinforce, create storage, protect closures, evacuate |
| Flash or pluvial flood | minutes to hours | rainfall intensity, small-catchment response, street geometry, culverts, hidden openings | distributed warning, rapid rescue, traffic exclusion, local pumping after peak |
| Coastal surge and compound flood | hours to days | tide, surge, wave, wind, river discharge, rainfall, salt, port state | staged exclusion, evacuation, littoral rescue, gate/pump coordination, coastal access |
| Levee, dam or control-structure failure | minutes to days | head difference, breach growth, erosion, seepage, foundation, downstream occupancy | warning, evacuation, arrest widening, reduce flow, close and stabilize |
| Trapped urban or polder basin | days to months | stored volume, continuing inflow, sewer state, discharge elevation, contamination | isolate inflow, preserve interior drainage, pump, treat, dry and reopen |
| Riverine navigation and access loss | days to months | current, depth, debris, banks, bridges, locks, channel migration | search, cargo, temporary crossings, channel clearance and shore interfaces |
| Post-fire or slope-driven flood/debris flow | minutes to seasons | sediment concentration, boulders, burned soil, channel constriction, repeated rain | exclusion, debris interception, high-abrasion earthworks, warning and route defense |

Depth alone cannot type the mission. Shallow moving water can sweep a person or vehicle; deep static water can conceal holes, vehicles, wires and sharp debris; salt water changes corrosion and electrical hazards; sewage, industrial chemicals and medical waste change entry and discharge rules. CDC and NIOSH guidance identifies drowning, unseen physical hazards, sewage, toxic materials, biological agents, downed power and contaminated residues as simultaneous worker hazards.[^niosh-flood] Equipment must publish a multidimensional operating envelope:

`depth × velocity × wave × debris × contamination × visibility × slope × ground bearing × egress`

Every rescue, pump, robot and amphibious platform should carry a machine-readable envelope, current sensor state and abort logic. “Amphibious” is not a universal permission to enter water.

## Outcomes and force metrics

The force should report civilian outcomes and controllable production, not equipment activity.

### Life safety

- probability of warning before a location-specific arrival threshold;
- time to first and last searched structure by hydraulic zone;
- people reached, medically stabilized and transferred per hour;
- probability of access before a person's shelter, health or mobility tolerance is exceeded;
- patient-capable and accessibility-complete transport, not only passenger seats; and
- responder exposure-hours in water, current, contamination and unstable structures.

### Water control

- cubic metres of damaging storage prevented or removed;
- net basin drawdown after continuing inflow, backflow and gravity discharge;
- population- and asset-hours kept below damage or life-safety depth;
- probability that a barrier segment, gate or breach intervention holds through the load history;
- critical facilities and utility nodes kept operable; and
- time until gravity drainage, normal pumps or civil operators can assume the system.

### Access and recovery

- population reconnected to a dry or controlled route;
- tonnes, patients and vehicles per day across wet gaps;
- drainage nodes, culverts, canals, roads, rail and ports restored to stated capacity;
- contaminated water, sediment and debris characterized, contained, treated or lawfully discharged; and
- area released for safe re-entry with inspection confidence.

Several common metrics are insufficient alone:

| Activity measure | Why it fails as an effect measure |
|---|---|
| Pump nameplate | ignores head, losses, debris, downtime, downstream stage and continuing inflow |
| Barrier length delivered | ignores foundation, closures, seepage, overtopping, drainage and load history |
| Boats deployed | ignores water class, launch access, crew endurance, patient handling and sortie cycle |
| People evacuated | ignores accessible movement, destination safety, family unity and return pathway |
| Cubic metres moved | may transfer contamination or flood another community |
| Tonnes of rock placed | does not establish breach arrest, filter compatibility, erosion control or stability |
| Roads “cleared” | does not establish bearing capacity, bridge safety, drainage or traffic throughput |

## Force architecture

### Flood Intercept Network

The `FIN` is the flood equivalent of a distributed air-defense or ignition-intercept network: permanently postured, locally integrated and nationally standardized. It does not wait for a heavy formation to mobilize.

Regional nodes should contain:

- a flood intelligence and modeling cell linked to NOAA, USGS, local gages, drainage operators and public warning;
- deployable stream, tide, rainfall, soil, levee, sewer, water-quality and infrastructure sensors;
- reconnaissance drones and small autonomous surface vehicles;
- closure inspection, gate actuation and backup-control packages;
- modular barrier, erosion protection, sand-boil and seepage-control stocks;
- road-mobile pump and rapid-hose sets with independent power;
- shallow-static and current-capable rescue detachments;
- local high-water mobility and accessible evacuation vehicles;
- water-entry PPE, decontamination, medical support and filtered rest; and
- a pre-negotiated escalation and receiving plan for national formations.

Its principal readiness metric is not equipment count. It is the probability that a target segment, neighborhood, critical facility or population receives an effective action before a case-specific hydraulic threshold. The complete clock is:

\[
T_{action}=T_{forecast/detect}+T_{verify}+T_{decide}+T_{mobilize}+T_{travel}+T_{site}+T_{effective}
\]

Forecast events can produce negative response time: the force acts before water arrives. Flash floods and sudden breaches may leave only the already distributed node relevant. The national posture model must therefore use regional hazard covariance, route failure and simultaneous watch conditions, not equal geographic spacing.

### Hydraulic Control Group

The deployable `HCG` is a complete field hydraulic utility and public-works formation. Its reference unit, `HCG-100`, is defined by 100 m³/s of installed gross water-movement capacity across a portfolio of heads and debris conditions—not by one pump or one pipe.

Its permanent structure should include:

| Formation element | Operational responsibility |
|---|---|
| Command and hydraulic intelligence squadron | common operating picture, forecasts, basin balance, objectives, safety zones, civil coordination |
| Survey and control-structure battalion | topography, bathymetry, levee/gate/dam inspection, instrumentation, closure control |
| Pump and drainage battalion | intakes, pumps, power, manifolds, pipe/channel connection, discharge and maintenance |
| Barrier and breach battalion | temporary exclusion, seepage control, erosion protection, breach arrest, sheet/pile and cofferdam works |
| Amphibious access battalion | shallow rescue, current rescue, patient movement, high-water mobility and heavy wet-gap logistics |
| Autonomous earthworks battalion | debris, channels, berms, haul, material placement, dredging and hazardous-zone work |
| Water quality and environmental battalion | sampling, plume mapping, solids, oil/chemical isolation, treatment interfaces and discharge monitoring |
| Infrastructure protection battalion | power, water, wastewater, communications, hospitals and transport-node defense |
| Sustainment and repair battalion | power/fuel, workshops, spares, crew rest, decontamination, logistics and retrograde |

The group fights a water budget. Its commander should publish a time-indexed basin balance, uncertainty, operating constraints and consequence priorities. Pumping a low-value zone while an upstream inflow remains uncontrolled or a downstream stage makes discharge impossible is not progress.

### Supported joint formations

An HCG does not independently deliver a regional campaign. It normally operates under a Joint Resilience Command with:

- Air Service sensing, lift and inaccessible-site rescue;
- Maritime Service coastal access, port opening, barges, tow and offshore support;
- Infrastructure Service power, water, wastewater and communications restoration;
- Health Service rescue medicine, contamination surveillance and hospital continuity;
- Community Service evacuation, shelter, disability access and return; and
- Theater Opening and Sustainment Commands for movement, reception, fuel, material and maintenance.

The HCG controls the hydraulic plan. Incident civil leadership sets consequence priorities and authorizes evacuation, land access and discharge decisions. Technical authority can refuse an unsafe barrier, pump, breach or entry plan. These roles must be explicit before deployment.

## HCG-100 first-order configuration

### Water mass balance

For a bounded basin:

\[
\frac{dV}{dt}=Q_{in}+Q_{rain}+Q_{backflow}-Q_{gravity}-Q_{pump}-Q_{loss}
\]

The dewatering time is not `stored volume / pump nameplate`. A screening case uses:

- flooded area: 50 km²;
- average excess depth: 1.5 m;
- initial stored volume: 75 million m³;
- installed gross pumping: 100 m³/s;
- operating availability and hydraulic utilization: 75%; and
- continuing net inflow after other controls: 20 m³/s.

Effective gross pumping is 75 m³/s and net removal is 55 m³/s. The idealized drawdown time is:

\[
T=\frac{75{,}000{,}000}{(75-20)\times86{,}400}=15.78\text{ days}
\]

This is a deliberately simple lower-bound screen. As depth falls, suction geometry, local sumps, sediment, disconnected pockets and minimum operating levels reduce productive capacity. Rain and sewer inflow vary. Roads and building basements require local pumping after the regional water surface recedes. A configuration model should therefore solve daily or hourly storage by zone rather than apply one constant rate.

Scale is unforgiving. At 75 m³/s effective output and no continuing inflow, removing one metre from 100 km² takes 15.43 days. Removing one metre from 1,000 km² takes 154.3 days. The ARkStorm scenario's hundreds of miles of flooding cannot be answered by a single national pump group.[^usgs-arkstorm] It requires multiple groups, surviving fixed drainage, controlled gravity paths, regional materials, long-duration rotation and an explicit triage of which basins are emptied first.

### Pump power

The ideal input-power screen is:

\[
P=\frac{\rho gQH}{\eta}
\]

For 100 m³/s, water density 1,000 kg/m³ and 82% pump-plus-drive efficiency:

| Total dynamic head | Input-power screen |
|---:|---:|
| 5 m | 5.98 MW |
| 15 m | 17.95 MW |
| 30 m | 35.89 MW |
| 60 m | 71.78 MW |

The result supports a common electric-drive architecture with an attached PRG source package, grid connection when safe, and selected mechanical emergency drives. It does not support one universal pump. Low-head basin drainage favors axial or mixed-flow machinery and very large passages; longer routes and higher lifts favor different pumps, pressure classes and smaller parallel trains. Intake losses, pipe friction, bends, elevation, sediment, screens, fouling, motor cooling and reserve raise the installed requirement.

USACE's official pump-station manual treats pump selection and conditions, discharge, drives, hydraulic tests, trash handling, power, controls, grounding, environmental protection, operation and maintenance as one design problem.[^usace-pump] The future force should extend that logic into standardized mobile stations with common power buses, controls, connectors, condition monitoring and depot overhaul.

### The conveyance constraint

For a circular line:

\[
Q=\frac{\pi D^2}{4}v
\]

The geometry is a direct warning against “drone-laid flood hose” as a complete answer.

| Internal diameter and velocity | Flow per line | Lines for 100 m³/s before reserve |
|---|---:|---:|
| 0.15 m at 6.56 m/s | 0.116 m³/s | 863 |
| 0.61 m at 3.42 m/s | 1.00 m³/s | 100 |
| 1.0 m at 2.0 m/s | 1.57 m³/s | 64 |
| 2.0 m at 2.0 m/s | 6.28 m³/s | 16 |

The 0.15 m case approximates the published 7,000 litre/minute UK high-volume pump and three-kilometre hose system; it is a valuable tactical capability, but a hundred-cubic-metre-per-second campaign is two orders of magnitude larger.[^uk-hvp] The United Kingdom also operates 1,000–1,650 litre/second ultra-high-volume pumps, showing that road-mobile and semi-mobile systems can reach the 1 m³/s class.[^uk-uhvp]

The Department should therefore develop a **Rapid Drainage Spine**, not only more hose. It would combine:

- large-bore semi-rigid or articulated surface trunk sections;
- high-integrity quick joints and remotely inspected seals;
- autonomous pipe carriers, reelers, jointing machines and anchor systems;
- road, rail, barge and temporary-channel interfaces;
- modular siphon, lift and pressure-break nodes;
- traffic, bridge, erosion and public-crossing protection; and
- distributed leak, pressure and water-quality sensing.

Small aerial drones can survey, mark routes, pull pilot lines and carry sensors. Heavy ground robots, barges, cranes and purpose-built haulers must move, join, anchor, test and recover the actual hydraulic mass. In many cases the best drainage spine is a cleared existing canal, storm sewer, road cut or controlled gravity corridor. The force should be measured by net flow along a verified path, not by kilometres of hose issued.

### Barrier material and force

A conventional emergency earth levee with height `h`, crest width `c` and side slopes `m:1` has cross-sectional area:

\[
A=h(c+mh)
\]

A 10 km line, 2 m high, 3 m crest and 3:1 side slopes requires 18 m² of section, 180,000 m³ of placed material and roughly 360,000 tonnes at an illustrative compacted density of 2 t/m³. This excludes foundation preparation, filter, geotextile, erosion protection, ramps, closures, drainage and reserve. The mass makes a national barrier stockpile alone an incomplete strategy; the force needs local material characterization, excavation and haul fleets, water-ballasted systems, modular structural barriers, pre-engineered permanent interfaces and deliberate choices about which zones can be defended.

Hydrostatic load grows with the square of depth. The horizontal force per metre of ideal vertical barrier is:

\[
F'=\frac{1}{2}\rho gh^2
\]

At 1, 2 and 4 m, this is approximately 4.9, 19.6 and 78.5 kN per metre before wave, current, impact, seepage and foundation effects. The base moment grows with the cube of depth. A barrier family must therefore publish depth, duration, wave, current, debris, overtopping, seepage, foundation, bend, termination and closure envelopes. Marketing length and nominal height are not enough.

The existing USACE PLUG research is evidence that the technology frontier can move. It tested lightweight, water-filled structures intended to arrest or close flowing breaches, including a 104-foot tube holding 400 tons of water, helicopter transport concepts, protection against overtopping and a full-scale facility intended for a 40-foot breach.[^usace-plug] The Department should revive the underlying mission at much greater scale: competing active closure concepts, instrumented destructive tests and transition from flow reduction to durable repair.

### Breach-flow screen

A simplified free-discharge screen for a rectangular breach is:

\[
Q_b\approx C_d b\sqrt{2g}h^{3/2}
\]

For a 12 m opening, 3 m head and an illustrative discharge coefficient of 0.6, flow is about 166 m³/s. This is larger than the entire HCG-100 installed pumping rate. It explains why breach response cannot begin with downstream dewatering. The sequence is:

`warn and evacuate → observe growth → protect breach shoulders → reduce differential head where possible → obstruct and dissipate flow → establish filter-compatible closure → armor and monitor → begin basin drawdown`

Real breach hydraulics, erosion and geotechnical failure are far more complex than the screen. The calculation only establishes the order of magnitude and the need for active flow-control systems, heavy material, cranes or barges, geotechnical authority and a purpose-built test complex.

## Purpose-built system families

### FL-1: Flood Intelligence Mesh

The mesh fuses national prediction with incident-scale observation. NOAA's National Water Model supplies high-frequency guidance across millions of river reaches and couples freshwater and coastal total-water-level modeling in its current configuration.[^noaa-nwm] USGS flood-inundation products tie surveyed impacts and map libraries to real-time gages, while NASA's near-real-time flood products use synthetic-aperture radar to observe surface water through cloud and darkness but still have revisit and latency limits.[^usgs-fim][^nasa-hydrosar]

The future mesh should add:

- air- and ground-deployed water-level, velocity, rainfall, soil-pressure and pore-pressure sensors;
- disposable current and drift beacons;
- levee deformation, seepage, temperature and acoustic sensing;
- sewer, culvert, pump-station and gate-state telemetry;
- autonomous surface and underwater bathymetry;
- water-quality and contaminant plume nodes;
- resilient local compute that can operate without national cloud connectivity; and
- a continuously updated uncertainty field, not one deterministic flood polygon.

The digital twin must answer operational questions: which road or structure becomes unreachable next, where a barrier changes interior drainage, which pump lowers consequence fastest, whether a breach intervention is reducing flow, where contamination will move and when a receiving channel becomes available. Model authority remains with accountable hydrologists and engineers. Machine learning may accelerate mapping and anomaly detection, but it does not receive independent authority to open a gate, flood another zone or declare a structure safe.

### FL-2: Megaflow Pump Train

The pump family should use common electric drives, controls, sensors and service interfaces across distinct wet ends.

| Class | Exploration band | Mission |
|---|---:|---|
| Tactical high-head | 0.1–0.5 m³/s at 20–80 m | streets, basements, tunnels, long hose, isolated facilities |
| Regional mixed-flow | 1–5 m³/s at 5–40 m | basins, canals, sewer bypass, trunk drainage |
| Basin axial-flow | 5–15 m³/s at 2–15 m | mass low-head drawdown through large passages |
| Barge or closure station | 20–50 m³/s at 1–10 m | rivers, ports, tidal barriers, major canals |

These are design-study bands, not selected pumps. Each deploys with intake excavation, debris screens, trash removal, priming, manifolds, pipe or channel interfaces, variable-speed drives, power conversion, cooling, condition monitoring, spill control, lighting, operator protection, spares and recovery equipment. The program should target rapid wet-end exchange so a common drive train can accept axial, mixed-flow, solids-handling or higher-head modules.

The ambitious research objective is not merely a larger impeller. It is a fault-tolerant modular pumping plant that can self-survey a temporary intake, tolerate variable submergence and solids, maintain efficiency over a wide head range, reconfigure around failed modules, and publish trustworthy net flow and energy under incident conditions.

### FL-3: Adaptive Barrier and Closure System

The barrier family needs:

- pre-installed standardized sockets and gate interfaces at nationally important sites;
- water- or granular-ballasted rapid barriers for suitable surfaces;
- modular structural panels and deployable foundations;
- inflatable or tensioned membranes for selected low-debris conditions;
- road, rail, utility and building-opening closures;
- seepage blankets, filters, relief wells and sand-boil control;
- overtopping armor and sacrificial energy dissipation;
- interior drainage, sump and pump integration; and
- continuous settlement, leakage, pressure and movement sensing.

The research program should develop barriers that actively report their own load and deterioration and can add ballast, anchors or relief capacity before failure. “Self-deploying” must not mean unsupervised flooding of occupied or downstream areas. Civil authority sets the protected and sacrificed zones; technical authority validates the installation and operating envelope.

### FL-4: Breach and Control-Structure Works

This family attacks flowing failures that ordinary construction equipment cannot safely approach. Candidate systems include:

- remotely placed edge armor and geotextile/filter cartridges;
- articulated water-ballasted plugs and hydraulic arches;
- autonomous aggregate, rock and precast placement;
- rapidly driven sheet, cellular and inflatable cofferdams;
- high-capacity grout and soil-mixing trains;
- cable, anchor and tension systems deployed from both banks or barges;
- dredge and underwater excavation modules; and
- temporary gate and culvert bypass assemblies.

The formation needs a staged closure doctrine. First reduce widening and flow; then establish filtered structural continuity; then armor, drain, inspect and transition to durable works. Dumping mixed aggregate into a breach without filters or foundation understanding can move erosion, obstruct a channel or fail suddenly.

### FL-5: Amphibious Rescue and Logistics Family

One universal amphibious vehicle remains rejected. The family contains at least five purpose-built classes.

| Class | Primary environment | Design priority |
|---|---|---|
| Shallow urban rescue craft | low-current streets, submerged obstacles | very shallow draft, protected propulsion, low wake, side access, quiet maneuvering |
| Swiftwater rescue craft | rivers, breached flows, fast channels | thrust reserve, directional control, self-righting, tether/anchor interfaces, rescue reach |
| High-water land carrier | intermittent road/water/debris transitions | wheel/track mobility, high seating, accessibility, sealed systems, winch and recovery |
| Heavy amphibious logistics carrier | sustained wet gap | payload, roll-on/off handling, shore interface, low ground pressure, repairability |
| Coastal surf and littoral craft | waves, current, salt, damaged ports | seakeeping, surf transit, corrosion, beaching, patient/cargo handling |

Every class should support night and degraded-visibility navigation, obstacle sensing, person-overboard detection, digital search marking, accessible boarding and decontamination. Crewed rescue remains the default near people. Autonomous surface craft should scout routes, deploy sensors, tow lines, carry low-risk cargo and act as communications relays. Remote or autonomous casualty contact requires a specific human-supervision and fail-safe case.

### FL-6: Rapid Drainage Spine

The spine is the missing connector between large pumps and a lawful receiving water. It is a system of large-bore trunks, temporary channels, siphons, lift nodes, manifolds, anchors, crossings, energy dissipation, valves and telemetry. A deployable pump group should carry route variants rather than assume that every incident offers an intact canal.

Research priorities are low-mass large-bore structures, foldable or nested sections, fast joints under contamination, robotic placement, controlled surface crossings, autonomous leak location, transient management and recovery without leaving a second debris problem. The system shares interfaces with the Rapid Water Grid but differs in contamination tolerance, pressure regime, solids passage, discharge control and acceptable leakage.

### FL-7: Hydraulic Works Carrier

The carrier is a mobile base for water-control machinery, not a disaster battleship.

**Riverine version.** A shallow-draft, modular barge train or self-propelled vessel carries low-head pumps, large intakes, pipe sections, cranes, excavators, sheet/pile systems, workshops, autonomous craft and command spaces. It can separate into several work nodes, use inland locks and waterways, and accept rail- or road-delivered modules. Draft, air draft, lock geometry, current, debris and bank interface govern the design.

**Coastal version.** A seagoing hydraulic works ship supports damaged ports, surge barriers, estuaries and low-lying coastal basins. It provides heavy lift, tow, dredge support, pipe and cable handling, electrical power, workshops, autonomous surface/underwater fleets, aviation spots and contaminated-water sampling. It must work through sea state and salt exposure, but its pumps still need a shore route and discharge system.

A nuclear-powered coastal variant remains a held option, not the reference. A pre-licensed 100–300 MWe nuclear water–energy platform could sustain months of pumping, grid support and clean-water production, but it does not solve shallow access, pipe mass, receiving-water stage, discharge authority, reactor security, crew, heat rejection or licensing. For flood response, high mobility and early arrival may dominate endurance. The concept should proceed only if campaign modeling shows repeated coastal missions where persistent electric process power—not another limiting interface—is decisive.

### EW-2/3: Autonomous Earthworks and Flood-Debris System

Flood campaigns require excavation, haul, berms, channels, bank repair, sediment removal, drainage clearance, building debris and contaminated material management. The purpose-built family should include optionally crewed excavators, dozers, loaders, articulated haulers, dredges and material processors with:

- wading and soft-ground packages;
- sealed and filtered operator stations;
- remote and supervised-autonomous modes;
- perception through rain, spray, dust and darkness;
- tool and payload recognition;
- local safe stop and no-connectivity work plans;
- remote recovery and self-extraction interfaces;
- machine-readable material tracking; and
- washdown, decontamination and corrosion control.

Robotics should displace exposure at unstable banks, contaminated sediment, moving water and breach shoulders. It should not erase geotechnical judgment or civil authority over excavation, demolition and material disposition. USACE debris practice already distinguishes emergency clearance, demolition, drainage structures, waterway debris and contaminated material.[^usace-debris] The future force should own the machinery and information system that turns those categories into controlled material flows.

## Inland and coastal force variants

The Department should standardize the command, sensing, power bus, controls, connectors, maintenance and workforce core, then attach environment-specific modules.

| Design dimension | Inland HCG | Coastal HCG |
|---|---|---|
| Dominant water | river, rainfall, groundwater, sewer, reservoir | tide, surge, wave, rainfall, river, salt water |
| Primary structures | levees, dams, culverts, canals, road/rail embankments | surge barriers, floodwalls, ports, dunes, seawalls, estuary gates |
| Access | roads, rail, bridges, locks, fields, shallow rivers | ports, beaches, surf, estuaries, offshore anchorage |
| Pump regime | mixed head; long routes; sediment and agricultural/urban solids | often high volume/low head; tidal reversal; salt and marine debris |
| Craft | road/rail-deployable shallow and current craft; barges | seagoing works ships, tugs, landing and surf craft, amphibious logistics |
| Material | local soil/aggregate, sheet/pile, geotextile, bridge and drainage kits | marine-grade barriers, armor stone, dredge, mooring, fender and corrosion systems |
| Health/environment | sewage, agriculture, industry, mold, wells | salt intrusion, fuel/port chemicals, sewage, marine habitat and fisheries |
| Sustainment | distributed inland bases and railheads | ports, offshore support, corrosion control and maritime crew cycles |

The variants must be able to combine. A hurricane creates coastal surge, urban rainfall and inland river flooding in sequence. An atmospheric-river campaign may require coastal ports for reception and inland waterways for movement. Common interfaces let modules cross-attach without pretending that one hull or vehicle can optimize every regime.

## Deployment sequence

### Wave 0: permanent preparation

- maintain basin, levee, drainage, evacuation and material data;
- survey pre-engineered barrier and pump sites;
- certify fixed-grid and PRG connection points;
- stock regional closures, sensors, hose, filters and rescue equipment;
- exercise local authority, warning and accessible evacuation; and
- monitor fleet, crews and industrial reserve.

### Wave 1: forecast intercept

- deploy sensors and reconnaissance;
- clear trash racks, culverts, drainage channels and pump intakes;
- close gates and install priority barriers;
- lower controllable storage where authorized;
- move local rescue and high-water teams into protected positions;
- evacuate high-consequence and mobility-limited populations; and
- begin national force generation before impact certainty.

### Wave 2: life safety and control

- search by hydraulic zone and last-known occupancy;
- maintain low-wake urban and current-capable rescue lanes;
- protect hospitals, power, water, wastewater and communications;
- arrest breaches and reinforce overtopping reaches;
- preserve interior drainage and prevent backflow; and
- open dry, elevated, aerial and waterborne logistics corridors.

### Wave 3: mass dewatering and access

- establish intakes, power and drainage spines;
- solve basin drawdown and downstream receiving capacity;
- pump by consequence priority;
- remove sediment and debris from drainage and routes;
- restore bridges, ports, railheads and utility corridors; and
- maintain contamination zoning and public-health surveillance.

### Wave 4: stabilization and transfer

- repair or replace temporary controls;
- dry and inspect buildings and infrastructure;
- restore normal drainage, water and wastewater operations;
- process waste and contaminated sediment;
- instrument repaired structures through the next wet period; and
- transfer authority, records, spares and residual risk to civil owners.

Each wave overlaps. Search continues during dewatering; rainfall continues after barriers are installed; recovery construction can change the hydraulic model. The headquarters must control transitions by effect and risk, not calendar day.

## Advanced Resilience Projects Agency flood program

The Department's `ARPA-R` should operate a Flood and Hydraulic Systems Directorate with program managers empowered to fund rival technical paths, cancel attractive failures and move successful prototypes into an independent Resilience Test and Evaluation Authority.

### Ten technology campaigns

| Campaign | Transformational objective | Evidence before operational credit |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Flood state in minutes | fuse forecasts, gages, SAR, drones, surface robots and infrastructure telemetry into uncertainty-aware operational maps | blind regional exercises; latency, depth/velocity error, false confidence and disconnected operation |
| 2. Megaflow modular pumping | 1–15 m³/s modules with common electric drives, wide head range, debris tolerance and rapid wet-end exchange | full-curve efficiency, cavitation, solids, intake, failure isolation, 30-day endurance and maintainability |
| 3. Rapid Drainage Spine | deploy 1–2 m-class trunks, siphons and channels at campaign tempo | route installation, joints, anchoring, transient loads, traffic crossings, leak detection and recovery |
| 4. Adaptive barriers | self-monitoring exclusion systems with fast foundations, interior drainage and controlled overtopping | full-scale wave/current/debris/load-history tests; seepage, settlement, termination and closure failures |
| 5. Active breach arrest | reduce flow through growing, inaccessible failures before durable repair | destructive breach tests across soil, head, width, debris, access and deployment modes |
| 6. Flood autonomy | surface, underwater and aerial swarms for mapping, sensing, line placement and low-risk cargo | navigation, lost-link, human detection, collision, recovery, cyber and mixed-fleet safety |
| 7. Optionally crewed earthworks | productive heavy work at breach, sediment and contaminated sites without continuous exposure | standard tasks in mud, current, rain, low visibility and communications loss; recovery and repair |
| 8. Purpose-built amphibious access | distinct urban, swiftwater, heavy-logistics and coastal platforms with accessible patient flow | instrumented hydrodynamics, obstacles, capsize/self-righting, wake, ingress/egress, contamination and crew cycle |
| 9. Contamination-aware dewatering | characterize and route floodwater without exporting unmeasured harm | real-time sampling limits, separation/treatment trains, solids, discharge monitoring and environmental fate |
| 10. Hydraulic works carriers | riverine modular and coastal seagoing bases that deliver pumps, material, craft, repair and power | complete ship/barge–shore mission threads, ports/locks, stability, cranes, pipe, crew, maintenance and cost |

The campaigns should pursue capabilities that do not yet exist at required scale. They should also retain unglamorous alternatives. A new autonomous breach machine competes against pre-installed sockets, locally cached aggregate, conventional cranes and better warning. Novel technology wins only when it improves outcome, readiness, exposure, lifecycle cost or deployability across the complete mission thread.

### Test enterprise

The national proving complex should include:

- full-scale levee and floodwall segments with controllable failure;
- a variable-current, depth, debris and contamination rescue village;
- pump basins with configurable head, solids, intake and discharge;
- long drainage-spine routes with roads, rail, slopes and crossings;
- wave, surge, salt and surf facilities;
- soft-ground and sediment earthworks ranges;
- damaged urban utilities and submerged obstacles;
- disconnected command and cyber-failure trials; and
- 30- to 90-day formation endurance exercises.

Program offices should not certify their own claims. Independent test reports must publish configuration, failure, maintenance, operator burden, uncertainty and the share of the complete civilian effect delivered.

## Industrial base

A peer resilience industrial base needs firms whose central business is disaster-control systems, not occasional modifications of defense or construction products. The Department should cultivate several prime-scale integrators and a deep supplier network.

### Prime-scale product sectors

1. **Hydraulic Systems Prime:** pumps, drives, intakes, manifolds, drainage spines, valves, controls and depot support.
2. **Flood Structures Prime:** adaptive barriers, closures, foundations, filters, breach-arrest systems and rapid control structures.
3. **Amphibious Access Prime:** urban, swiftwater, heavy-logistics, riverine and coastal craft with common mission systems.
4. **Autonomous Civil Works Prime:** optionally crewed earthmoving, dredging, material handling, mapping and recovery systems.
5. **Hydraulic Intelligence Prime:** sensors, models, edge compute, resilient communications, public-warning and incident digital twins.
6. **Environmental Process Prime:** water-quality sensing, contaminated-water routing, treatment, sediment and waste systems.
7. **Resilience Shipbuilding Prime:** riverine works carriers, coastal hydraulic ships, barges, tugs, launch systems and workshops.

The government should own interface standards, test data, mission software escrow, diagnostic access and sufficient technical data to compete sustainment. It should fund multi-source motors, power electronics, bearings, seals, large castings, composite or polymer structures, marine gear, sensors and control components. Industrial readiness includes warm production lines, tooling, trained labor, test stands, strategic materials, spares and repair capacity.

### Production model

The fleet should combine:

- sovereign core formations at high readiness;
- regionally hosted FIN equipment maintained to national standards;
- rotating government reserve;
- contracted surge production and commercial common equipment;
- pre-negotiated local material and haul networks; and
- allied pools using common hydraulic, digital and safety interfaces.

Commercial pumps, barges and earthmovers remain valuable. They should plug into the architecture where their performance, data, crew and repair state are known. The sovereign floor covers rare high-consequence systems, first-wave readiness, hazardous entry, national command, advanced prototypes, strategic spares and missions the market will not hold idle.

## A $1 trillion, 25-year flood portfolio

The following real-2026-dollar allocation is a strategic thought experiment, not an independent cost estimate or budget request.

| Portfolio | Capital/RDT&E | Purpose |
|---|---:|---|
| Research, prototypes and independent test | $130B | ten ARPA-R campaigns, destructive ranges, digital twins, rival prototypes and failed-path allowance |
| Pumps, power interfaces and drainage spines | $220B | HCG-100 water-control battalions, pipes/channels, intakes, drives, workshops and spares |
| Amphibious rescue and logistics | $150B | four environment-specific vehicle/craft families, patient systems, training and depots |
| Barriers, breach and autonomous earthworks | $160B | adaptive barriers, closures, filters, sheet/pile, breach systems, robots and material handling |
| Hydraulic intelligence and communications | $80B | sensors, gages, SAR integration, edge compute, models, warning and resilient networks |
| Riverine and coastal hydraulic works fleet | $90B | modular barge groups, coastal works ships, tow, dredge and shore interfaces |
| Regional bases, prepositioning and strategic stocks | $70B | FIN sites, materials, spare modules, rail/port interfaces and sheltered storage |
| Industrial expansion and multi-source supply | $45B | factories, tooling, large-component supply, test stands and repair ecosystem |
| Workforce, academies and national exercises | $30B | schools, simulators, wet ranges, qualification pipelines and long-duration exercises |
| Program reserve and uncertainty | $25B | technical failure, redesign, schedule and production risk |
| **Total** | **$1,000B** | capital and RDT&E only; routine personnel and operations excluded |

This balance intentionally spends more on water-control systems, ground work and access than on a small number of iconic ships. Carriers matter when they deliver a complete work system, but the national outcome comes from thousands of pumps, pipe sections, robots, barriers, craft, sensors, workshops and trained teams operating as formations.

### Phased build

**Years 1–5: test the frontier.** Establish the directorate and test authority; compete pump, drainage, barrier, breach, autonomy and craft prototypes; instrument representative regional systems; define common interfaces; begin industrial facilities; and field enhanced FIN prototypes.

**Years 6–10: first operational formations.** Produce early HCG-100s, distributed FIN nodes, amphibious families and riverine works groups; run multi-region exercises; retire failed technical paths; and establish depot and school capacity.

**Years 11–15: national concurrency.** Expand regional prepositioning, coastal groups, strategic stocks and industrial reserve; demonstrate simultaneous major campaigns; and introduce second-generation autonomous and hydraulic systems.

**Years 16–25: mature power projection.** Recapitalize early systems, maintain warm competing production, field proven coastal carriers, build allied interoperability, export civil variants and sustain continuous test against new climate and infrastructure conditions.

## Workforce and professions

The force requires a new civil-protection profession with operational authority and deep technical specialization. Core occupations include:

- hydrologists, hydraulic and coastal engineers;
- geotechnical, structural, dam and levee engineers;
- surveyors, geomatics specialists and model/data-assimilation teams;
- pump, motor, power-electronics and rotating-equipment technicians;
- pipe, valve, diving, welding, rigging, crane, dredge and marine crews;
- rescue swimmers, boat operators, rope technicians, pilots, medics and accessible-evacuation specialists;
- heavy-equipment and autonomous-system operators;
- water-quality, environmental, industrial-hygiene and public-health teams;
- mechanics, corrosion specialists, machinists and depot engineers;
- logistics, rail, port, material and contracting specialists; and
- community liaison, warning, shelter, displacement and return specialists.

Machinery does not eliminate people; it changes exposure and skill. A 24-hour pump station needs operators, maintenance and power staff even when controls are automated. A robotic excavator needs mission planning, monitoring, recovery and field repair. Water rescue requires sustained qualification and medical fitness. The Department should build active, regional reserve, auxiliary and industry-reserve components with common qualification records and recurring wet exercises.

The workforce model must be shift-complete. Deployed positions are multiplied by simultaneous units, shifts, relief and common support; institutional billets add training, depot, test, command, medical recovery and career depth. A vehicle inventory without qualified crews and replacements is not a force.

## Strategic projection and international role

A hydraulic-control force projects power by changing the material trajectory of a partner's catastrophe. It can keep a port, hospital district, industrial zone or capital drainage system functioning; restore inland access; arrest a dam or levee failure; and shorten mass displacement. Those effects are visible, politically consequential and often less escalatory than military presence.

The force should deploy under agreements that make sovereignty and distributional effects explicit. Pumping water out of one district can raise it elsewhere. Breaching a road or levee can protect a city while sacrificing farms. Discharge can export contamination across a border or fishery. Host authorities choose legitimate priorities; Department engineers state physical consequences and refuse technically unsafe operations; public records preserve who decided and why.

Export opportunity follows from standards and industrial depth, not only complete formations. Potential exports include:

- pump and drainage-spine modules;
- barrier sockets and closure systems;
- sensor and digital-twin services;
- urban and riverine rescue craft;
- autonomous earthwork kits;
- riverine works barges;
- training, depot and readiness systems; and
- licensed local manufacture of standard components.

The United States should avoid making partners dependent on proprietary cloud services or unique consumables. Export variants need local maintenance, open physical interfaces, manual-safe modes and governance suited to civilian use.

## Failure cases

| Failure case | Hidden bad assumption | Design response |
|---|---|---|
| Forecast is wrong in place or timing | national model equals incident truth | ensembles, local sensors, uncertainty, mobile reserve and reversible staging |
| Barrier protects against river but traps rain/sewer flow | exclusion equals safety | coupled interior drainage, sump/pump and backflow model |
| Pump underperforms | nameplate equals net discharge | full head/loss/debris curve, flow metering, reserve and alternate wet ends |
| Receiving water is too high | discharge path is always available | tide/stage windows, storage, alternate routes, siphon/channel and consequence model |
| Intake clogs or cavitates | open water guarantees supply | surveyed intake, trash handling, submergence, sediment and redundant bays |
| Breach outflow overwhelms dewatering | pumping is first intervention | evacuation, edge arrest, flow reduction and staged closure before drawdown |
| Universal rescue craft capsizes or grounds | amphibious label covers every water state | distinct classes, published envelopes, scouts, abort criteria and recovery |
| Robotics lose perception or link | autonomy removes supervision | local stop, bounded work plans, optional crew, redundant sensing and recovery teams |
| Pumping exports contamination | moved water equals reduced harm | sampling, plume model, isolation, treatment, discharge authority and monitoring |
| Access route fails under heavy modules | map route equals load-capable route | survey, bridge/ground-bearing checks, distributed modules and alternate water/rail lift |
| Crews exhaust during long drawdown | equipment availability equals force availability | shifts, relief, filtered/decontaminated rest, exposure tracking and rotation depth |
| Temporary works become permanent by neglect | emergency installation is safe indefinitely | inspection interval, storm-season design state, handover and funded removal/replacement |
| Second event arrives | first campaign can consume national inventory | concurrency standard, regional reserve, reconstitution and protected industrial surge |

## R3 evidence gate

No flood system reaches `R3` until evidence closes the following:

1. representative inland, urban, coastal, breach and contamination cases with uncertainty;
2. complete forecast/detection-to-effective-action distributions;
3. time-indexed basin and receiving-water models;
4. pump curves at representative head, solids, submergence and wear;
5. intake, trash, sediment, cavitation and maintenance behavior;
6. power, fuel, cooling, control and black-start configuration;
7. route-specific drainage-spine hydraulics, transients, joints, anchors and crossings;
8. barrier loads, foundation, seepage, overtopping, terminations and interior drainage;
9. breach growth, flow-reduction, filter and durable closure tests;
10. amphibious stability, maneuver, obstacle, wake, patient and decontamination performance by water class;
11. autonomous surface and underwater navigation, lost-link, collision and recovery;
12. optionally crewed earthwork productivity, safety and repair in mud, water and contamination;
13. environmental sampling, treatment, discharge and residuals performance;
14. critical-infrastructure protection and restart interfaces;
15. itemized deployment, recurring material and strategic-movement manifests;
16. workforce qualifications, shifts, exposure, rotation and training pipelines;
17. multi-formation readiness, correlated demand and reconstitution;
18. riverine/coastal carrier stability, lift, locks/ports, crew and maintenance;
19. lifecycle cost, industrial capacity, production and depot schedule; and
20. independent operational testing of the complete sensing–control–access–dewatering–recovery thread.

## What this pass changes

This pass changes the Department of Resilience concept in six ways.

- It replaces a “flood pump and rescue fleet” with a distributed `FIN` and deployable `HCG` hydraulic-control architecture.
- It gives the force a 100 m³/s reference formation while demonstrating that stored volume, continuing inflow, head and conveyance—not pump count—govern campaign time.
- It establishes inland and coastal variants with a common core rather than one universal vehicle or ship.
- It makes the Rapid Drainage Spine, adaptive barrier, active breach arrest, purpose-built amphibious family and optionally crewed earthworks full acquisition programs.
- It treats contamination, discharge, public authority and downstream consequence as part of hydraulic effectiveness.
- It gives `ARPA-R`, an independent test authority and prime-scale resilience industry explicit roles in creating capabilities beyond current technology.

The next gain comes from the 29-sheet configuration model. More platform description before pump, pipe, barrier, breach, access, power, mass and workforce balances close would create false confidence. No flood rendering is authorized. Pass 14 implements that model in [Flood hydraulic-control configuration model](configuration-model.md).
